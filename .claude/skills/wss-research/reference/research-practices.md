# What good research practice is, and what it means for deck research

Researched on 24 November 2026 under the `wss-research` skill. This file reports what was
found, labeled the skill's way, and then turns it into working rules for deck research.

## How this was researched, and its limits

- Three research passes, one each on history and archives, on judging sources online, and
  on rigor and stated certainty. Each was told to open the primary document for what it
  reported, to look for criticism of each method, and to say what it could not open.
- "Sourced" below means a research pass opened the document and reported the passage. I
  (the writer of this file) re-opened four of the quotations myself and three matched their
  sources word for word: the Genealogical Proof Standard's five parts, the British Library
  OCR figures and SIFT's four moves. The fourth, Wikipedia's exceptional-claims line, I
  could not re-fetch. I did not re-open the rest.
- Many journal articles were read as abstracts only. Those are marked.
- Sites that refused automated readers that day: historians.org, most of loc.gov, dni.gov,
  Project MUSE, web.archive.org, pexels.com, unsplash.com, flickr.com and several journal
  publishers. What could not be opened is listed at the end.

## The answer in brief

Fields that do this for a living (history, genealogy, medicine's systematic reviews,
intelligence analysis, climate assessment, journalism, professional fact-checking) arrive at
the same short list. Confidence: high that these are the stated standards, since the
standards themselves were read; lower on how well each works, where the evidence is
thinner and in places disputed.

1. Decide the question and the plan before searching.
2. Classify each source: is it the original or a copy, did its author witness the thing or
   repeat it, and why was it made.
3. Judge an unfamiliar source by what independent sources say about it, not by how it looks.
4. Trace every claim to where it first appears.
5. Count independent origins, not repetitions.
6. Search more than one place, and look for the contrary evidence.
7. Keep a record of what was searched, when, and what was set aside.
8. Keep what the source says apart from what you conclude.
9. State certainty in a small fixed vocabulary, with the reason.

## Findings

### 1. Plan first

- **Sourced.** Cochrane Handbook, chapter 4 (Lefebvre et al., updated March 2025): "Plan in
  advance the methods to be used for identifying studies." Searches "should be motivated
  directly by the eligibility criteria for the review."
- **Sourced.** Wineburg and McGrew's fact-checkers "took bearings" before reading unfamiliar
  content closely (in-press manuscript of 28 July 2018; published in Teachers College Record
  121(11), 2019).

### 2. Classify the source, the informant and the evidence

- **Sourced.** Elizabeth Shown Mills separates three things usually blurred together
  ("QuickLesson 17: The Evidence Analysis Process Map", Evidence Explained, undated, about
  2018):
  - the source: an original record, a derivative ("transcripts, translations, abstracts,
    extracts, nutshells, indexes, and database entries ... errors are frequent"), or an
    authored narrative;
  - the information in it: primary (firsthand), secondary ("Sources that repeat hearsay,
    tradition, or lore ... merely pass on what others say"), or undetermined;
  - the evidence drawn from it: direct, indirect or negative.
- **Sourced.** Her worked example is a newspaper item that is an original source carrying
  fourth-hand information: "It only means that this source made that claim."
- **Sourced.** Classical source criticism asks first what the document is (external
  criticism), then what its author meant, believed and was placed to know (internal
  criticism), statement by statement: "The criticism of a document is not to be performed en
  bloc." (Langlois and Seignobos, *Introduction to the Study of History*, 1898.)
- **Sourced.** The U.S. National Archives worksheet puts the same tests as questions: "Who
  wrote it? Who read/received it? When is it from? ... Why did the author write it? ... What
  other documents or historical evidence are you going to use?" (revised February 2017).
- **Sourced.** The American Historical Association: whether a document is primary or
  secondary "largely depends on the questions one asks of it" (*Statement on Standards of
  Professional Conduct*, 2019 text, read on a mirror; a 2023 revision was not read).

### 3. Judge an unfamiliar source from outside it

- **Sourced.** Wineburg and McGrew watched 45 people evaluate live websites: 10 historians
  with doctorates, 10 professional fact-checkers and 25 Stanford undergraduates. Asked to
  compare an advocacy group's site with the American Academy of Pediatrics, the fact-checkers
  scored 2.0 out of 2, the historians 0.7 and the students 0.16. "Fact checkers read
  laterally, leaving a site after a quick scan and opening up new browser tabs in order to
  judge the credibility of the original site." The authors call the study exploratory; the
  sample is small.
- **Sourced.** The same fact-checkers scanned a whole page of search results before clicking
  ("click restraint") and knew that search optimizers push some sites up and better ones
  down.
- **Sourced.** Mike Caulfield's four moves (SIFT, 19 June 2019): "Stop, Investigate the
  source, find better coverage, trace the original context."
- **Sourced (abstract only).** In a national sample of 3,446 U.S. high-school students, 96%
  never learned of a climate site's ties to the fossil-fuel industry (Breakstone et al.,
  Educational Researcher, 2021).
- **Sourced (abstracts, one summary).** Teaching lateral reading raises its use. Most of
  the studies are small, lack a control group, or come from the group that proposed the
  method. In one, 67 of 87 students read laterally after training, and only 36 of those 67
  then judged the site correctly (Breakstone et al., HKS Misinformation Review, 2021).

### 4. Trace a claim to its origin

- **Sourced.** Langlois and Seignobos: "No second-hand statement has any value except in so
  far as it reproduces its source."
- **Sourced.** Greenberg traced one biomedical claim through 242 papers and 675 citations
  (BMJ, 2009). Papers supporting it drew 94% of the citations to primary data; a subclaim
  "transformed from hypothesis to 'fact' through citation alone"; 24% of the citations for it
  led to papers that said nothing on the point. His remedy: "cite only primary data when
  making claims."
- **Sourced (abstracts).** In medical journals, between about one citation in seven and one
  in four does not support the claim it is attached to (Mogull, PLoS ONE, 2017: 14.5%;
  Jergas and Baethge, PeerJ, 2015: 25.4%). **Uncertain:** no comparable measurement was
  found for history or popular nonfiction.
- **Sourced.** The AHA calls it inappropriate to borrow "unexamined primary source
  references from a secondary work without citing that work."
- **Sourced.** A whiskey example: the claim that Elijah Craig made the first bourbon traces
  to Collins's *History of Kentucky* of 1874, which does not name Craig and was written about
  85 years after the event (Charles Cowdery, blog post, 14 June 2018; Collins was not opened).

### 5. Agreement counts only between independent sources

- **Sourced.** "Even if an observation were to be reproduced by a hundred different authors,
  these hundred copies would amount to no more than one observation." And: "as long as the
  statements are not proved to be independent we have no right to assume that their
  agreement is conclusive." The test for copying is the same details in the same order, and
  shared errors (Langlois and Seignobos, 1898).
- **Sourced.** Cochrane: "It is wrong to consider multiple reports of the same study as if
  they are multiple studies."
- **Sourced.** Nineteenth-century newspapers reprinted each other freely. The Viral Texts
  project found about 40,000 reprinted texts in pre-1861 U.S. newspapers, most circulating
  with no author's name (Cordell and Smith; Cordell, American Literary History, 2015).
- **Sourced.** "Citogenesis" is Randall Munroe's word (xkcd no. 978, November 2011) for an
  unsourced claim that enters Wikipedia, is repeated by a writer, and is then cited back.
  A documented case: an invented name for the coati, added in July 2008, later appeared in
  newspapers and a university press book (The New Yorker, 19 May 2014).
- **Sourced.** Wikipedia's own rule: "Do not use websites mirroring Wikipedia content or
  publications relying on material from Wikipedia as sources."
- **Disputed.** The newsroom "two-source rule". Reuters, AP and the BBC write it as a
  preference with exceptions ("Two or more sources are better than one", Reuters). Clark
  Mollenhoff (Nieman Reports, 1981) called any fixed count "nonsense unless there is a sound
  standard for weighing the credibility of the source": one direct witness beats several
  people repeating hearsay.

### 6. Interested sources

- **Sourced.** Wikipedia's rules list "press releases, the material contained within company
  websites, advertising campaigns" as self-published, and allow them only for statements
  about the source itself that are "neither unduly self-serving nor an exceptional claim" and
  involve no "claims about third parties".
- **Sourced.** "Any exceptional claim requires multiple high-quality sources" (Wikipedia,
  Verifiability; not re-fetched by the writer).
- **Sourced (abstract only).** When a university press release exaggerated, 58% to 86% of
  the news stories based on it did too, against 10% to 18% when it did not (Sumner et al.,
  BMJ, 2014; health science, 462 releases).
- **Sourced, as opinion.** Historians disagree about commissioned company histories. Niall
  Ferguson: "the word 'authorised' is synonymous with 'censored'" to many historians, though
  he argues they can be done honestly; David Cantor says sponsorship undermines credibility
  (Times Higher Education).
- **Sourced.** The Verification Handbook: "question seemingly authoritative sources as
  aggressively as we challenge any source." Its question: "How do you know that?"

### 7. Search engines and AI summaries are not evidence of reliability

- **Sourced.** Google describes ranking as aiming at "the most relevant, useful results",
  inferred from signals such as links from other prominent sites. The page does not say
  results are checked for accuracy (that absence is a reading of the page).
- **Sourced (abstract and first page).** Across 7,392 product-review queries watched for a
  year, most search results used affiliate marketing although few pages on the web do
  (Bevendorff et al., ECIR 2024).
- **Sourced (abstract only).** Searching to check a false article can raise belief in it
  when the results are poor (Aslett et al., Nature, 2024).
- **Sourced.** Measured error rates for AI-generated citations are high in every test found,
  2023 to 2025: 55% of GPT-3.5's and 18% of GPT-4's citations fabricated (Walters and Wilder,
  Scientific Reports, 2023); AI search tools wrong on more than 60% of 1,600 source-finding
  queries (Tow Center, March 2025, not peer reviewed); 13% of quotes attributed to BBC
  articles altered or absent (BBC, February 2025). **Uncertain:** whether these rates hold
  for current tools; no later measurement was found.

### 8. Digitized newspapers

- **Sourced.** In the British Library's nineteenth-century newspapers, measured on the
  clearest part of each page, word accuracy averaged 78%, and accuracy on capitalized words
  (names and places) 63.4% (Tanner, Muñoz and Ros, D-Lib Magazine, 2009). "At below 80% word
  accuracy, the search capacity will drop off steeply."
- **Sourced.** The Library of Congress says of Chronicling America that "achieving 100%
  accuracy in an automated OCR process is virtually impossible" (April 2025). **Uncertain:**
  no official accuracy figure for Chronicling America was found.
- **Sourced.** What is digitized is a selection. The U.S. program prefers long-running
  papers with statewide reach and good microfilm. In Britain about 20% of newspaper titles
  had been digitized by 2021, and search interfaces "give the impression of
  comprehensiveness, where none exists" (Beelen et al., 2025).
- **Sourced.** Mills: "negative evidence is not the same as negative findings. Merely not
  finding what we hope to find is a routine matter in historical research."

### 9. Period words

- **Sourced.** "Julep" dates from the fourteenth century, from the Persian for rose water
  (Merriam-Webster). An early "julep" is not a mint julep.
- **Sourced, through secondary writers.** The earliest known "bourbon" whiskey advertisement
  has moved from 1846 (the OED's citation) to 1840 and then to 1824 as more newspapers
  became searchable (John Baker, American Dialect Society list, 2014; Robert Moss, undated).
  The advertisements themselves were not seen.
- **Disputed.** Where the name "bourbon" comes from: Bourbon Street (Michael Veach) or
  Bourbon County (Moss, Baker).

### 10. Keep a record

- **Sourced.** Cochrane requires that "the search process (including the sources searched,
  when, by whom, and using which terms)" be documented, and that web pages be saved locally
  because they "may no longer be accessible".
- **Sourced.** PRISMA 2020 asks for every source searched and "the date when each source was
  last searched", and for studies "that might appear to meet the inclusion criteria, but
  which were excluded", with the reason.
- **Sourced.** The BBC requires "accurate, reliable and, wherever possible, contemporaneous
  notes of all significant research".
- **Sourced.** The AHA: "leaving a clear trail for subsequent historians to follow".
- **Uncertain.** No study was found showing that keeping a log makes conclusions more
  accurate. What the evidence shows is that unlogged searches cannot be checked.

### 11. Separate the record from your reasoning, and word certainty carefully

- **Sourced.** U.S. Intelligence Community Directive 203 (2 January 2015, read on a mirror):
  products "should clearly distinguish statements that convey underlying intelligence
  information used in analysis from statements that convey assumptions or judgments", and
  must not combine a confidence level and a likelihood in one sentence.
- **Sourced.** The IPCC uses two separate scales, confidence (from the evidence and how far
  it agrees) and likelihood (numeric), and asks for "a traceable account" behind each rating
  (Mastrandrea et al., 2010).
- **Sourced.** GRADE rates a body of evidence high, moderate, low or very low, and names
  what lowers a rating (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication
  bias). The rating "is a result of judgement, but the judgement process operates within a
  transparent structure" (Cochrane Handbook, chapter 14).
- **Sourced.** Undefined probability words are read very differently. Sherman Kent's own
  board read "serious possibility" as anything from 20 to 80 in 100. In an experiment with
  924 people, readers matched the intended meaning 66% of the time when the range was printed
  beside the word and 32% with no guidance; a definitions table kept elsewhere did not help
  (Wintle et al., PLoS ONE, 2019).
- **Disputed.** Whether defined words work at all. Dhami and Mandel (2021, abstract only)
  argue for numbers alone. Also, the same word means different things in different schemes:
  "likely" is 55 to 80% in ICD 203 and 66 to 100% for the IPCC.
- **Sourced (abstract and one passage).** People favor information that agrees with what
  they already think: a moderate effect, d = 0.36, across 91 studies and about 8,000
  participants, and it reverses when the unwelcome information serves a goal the person
  has (Hart et al., Psychological Bulletin, 2009).
- **Sourced.** The AHA: historians "are as interested in defining the limits and
  uncertainties of their own arguments as they are in persuading others".

### 12. Checklists

- **Disputed.** The CRAAP test (Meriam Library, CSU Chico; 25 questions under Currency,
  Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) is criticized by Wineburg, McGrew and Caulfield
  for directing attention to "easily manipulated features" of the page itself. Its own
  handout also asks "Can you verify any of the information in another source" and who the
  sponsor is. No head-to-head trial against lateral reading was found.

## What this means for deck research

Everything in this section is **inference**: the writer's application of the findings above
to research from old newspapers, government records, books and brand websites.

**Before searching**
- Write the question and what would count as an answer. Then search on purpose for the
  version that spoils the story. A good story is exactly the information a researcher is
  inclined to accept (finding 11).

**For each source**
- Note three things: what it is (original record, copy or index, or someone's narrative),
  who is speaking (a witness, or someone repeating), and why it was made (to record, to
  inform, to sell). A newspaper page image is an original; its searchable text is a copy
  with errors; a brand's "our story" page is a narrative by an interested party.
- Find out who is behind an unfamiliar site or book from somewhere other than the site
  itself before using it.
- A brand or company page is evidence that the company says so. Use it for plain facts
  about itself, attributed ("according to the distillery"). Do not use it for "first",
  "oldest", founder legends, or anything about a rival.
- An old newspaper item shows what was printed that day. It may be a paid notice or a
  reprint, and it does not prove the event happened as described.

**For each claim**
- Trace it to the earliest document that states it, and report the gap in years between the
  event and that document.
- Open the cited passage yourself before writing that a book or paper "says" something. If
  you saw the quotation only in a later book or blog, cite that as the carrier.
- Ten websites and three books repeating a story are one source until shown to be
  independent. Shared wording, shared order and shared mistakes mean copying.
- Several nineteenth-century papers printing the same item are probably one item reprinted.
  Write "reprinted in N papers; the earliest found is X", not "confirmed by N papers".
- A surprising or flattering claim needs more than one independent, strong source, or it is
  reported as legend: "tradition says; first recorded in [year]; no record from the time
  found".
- Read "whiskey", "bourbon", "old", "rye", "pure" and "julep" in the sense of the
  document's own date, and say so when the meaning has changed.

**Searching old newspapers**
- Names, places and numbers are the words the software reads worst. Search variant
  spellings and likely misreadings.
- Finding nothing means only that this search of this collection found nothing. Say what
  was searched and when. Small-town, short-lived and trade papers are the least likely to
  be digitized, and that is where local distillery news ran.
- Word any "first" as "the earliest I found in [collection], searched [date]".

**Tools**
- Search rank says nothing about truth. Read down the whole results page; expect brand and
  affiliate pages at the top for whiskey queries.
- Never take a date, quotation or citation from an AI or search-engine summary without
  opening the source and finding the words in it.
- Do not cite Wikipedia or a page copied from it. Follow its footnotes and open them.

**Keeping the record**
- Keep a search log for each deck in its outline: each archive, database and site, the
  date, the terms, and what was looked for and not found.
- Save a dated copy of any web page a claim rests on. Brand sites rewrite their history
  pages.
- Keep a short list of well-known stories the deck does not use, each with the reason.
- When a published deck is corrected, say what was wrong.

**Stating certainty**
- Keep "what the record says" and "what I conclude from it" in separate sentences.
- The skill's labels describe the evidence, not how likely something is. Where likelihood
  matters, put a rough number beside the word in the same sentence. A definitions table
  somewhere else is not read.
- Rate a claim up when independent kinds of record agree (a tax roll and a dated
  advertisement). Rate it down for a source with a motive, sources that disagree, a source
  far from the event, or a single thin source.
