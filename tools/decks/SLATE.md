## Tier one

### 1. The neighbour who built a copy (Laphroaig, Lagavulin and Malt Mill)

**Story.** Peter Mackie's firm owned Lagavulin and was sales agent for Laphroaig next door. Laphroaig ended the agency (1907 by Laphroaig's own account; sources say 1907 or 1908), and court cases followed. Laphroaig says that in 1907 Mackie blocked its Kilbride Stream with rocks and a court made him restore it. In 1908 Mackie built Malt Mill, a small distillery inside the Lagavulin site meant to make Laphroaig-style whisky, and hired away Laphroaig's brewer; it never matched Laphroaig and closed in 1962. Mackie was also the man behind White Horse and built Craigellachie distillery (1890–91).

**Why enthusiasts would not know it.** Most know Lagavulin and Laphroaig as Islay neighbours; few know about the agency, the stream, or that a fake Laphroaig ran inside Lagavulin for half a century.

**Bottles.**
- Laphroaig 10: the distillery Mackie wanted; ~$55.
- Lagavulin 16: Mackie's own distillery; ~$100.
- White Horse blended Scotch: Mackie's blend; **checked** $20–35 at US retailers (Liquor Barn $19.99, Wine and Cheese Place $31.99).
- Craigellachie 13: the Speyside distillery Mackie co-founded; **checked** ~$50–70 (Kroger $49.99, Wooden Cork $69.99).
- Optional: Lagavulin 8 (~$70) if two Lagavulins suit the night.

**Sources.**
- Laphroaig, "Our Heritage", laphroaig.com/heritage (read). "Water wars 1887–1908", the 1907 blocking, the 1908 copy still house; also Bessie Williamson.
- "Laphroaig" history page on the Edinburgh University whisky archive, dcs.ed.ac.uk/home/jhb/whisky/smws/laphroa2.html (read). Agency ended 1907, court case, water restored, Malt Mill with matching stills.
- Tom Bruce-Gardyne, "Peter the Great", Whisky Magazine, whiskymag.com/articles/peter-the-great/ (read). Mackie's life, White Horse, Craigellachie with Alexander Edward in 1890.
- Diffords Guide, Lagavulin history; Noel on Whisky, "Lagavulin vs Laphroaig part 2" (seen).
- **Primary material:** Scottish Court of Session records (the agency and water cases, not located online this session); Alfred Barnard's *Whisky Distilleries of the United Kingdom* (1887, public domain) describes both distilleries.

**Risks.** The year the agency ended and the order of the court cases differ between sources; the court records would settle it. Laphroaig's page is the distillery's own telling, and "Mackie sabotaged us" should be voiced as Laphroaig's account.

**Deck style.** Story with pours alongside; could run as a two-distillery profile.

**Tier one because** it has a clear turn, real people and four bottles that are each a character in it, all easy to buy.

### 2. The women of Cardhu

**Story.** John and Helen Cumming licensed a smugglers' farm distillery at Cardow on Speyside in 1824 (some sources say 1823 or 1811). Helen ran much of it; the distillery's lore has her flying a flag to warn neighbours of excise officers. Her daughter-in-law Elizabeth Cumming took over in 1872, rebuilt the distillery in the 1880s (the old stills were sold to William Grant, who used them at the new Glenfiddich), and in 1893 sold to John Walker & Sons for £20,500, keeping the family in day-to-day charge. In December 2003 Diageo replaced Cardhu single malt with a blended "pure malt" in the same bottle and label; after protests from other producers and drinkers it reversed course.

**Why enthusiasts would not know it.** Cardhu is known as "the Johnnie Walker malt". The two women and the 2003 row are rarely told together.

**Bottles.**
- Cardhu 12: the single malt; ~$50.
- Cardhu Amber Rock or Gold Reserve (no age statement): the modern range; ~$45–55.
- Johnnie Walker Black Label: the blend Cardhu was sold to supply; ~$35.
- Glenfiddich 12: made at a distillery that started on Cardow's second-hand stills; ~$45.

**Sources.**
- Wikipedia, "Cardhu distillery" (read). Helen's flag, the 1885 rebuild, the stills sold to William Grant, the December 2003 "pure malt" row.
- SMWS Unfiltered, "Whisky history: Cardhu" (seen); The Whiskey Wash, "Women in Whiskey: Helen and Elizabeth Cumming of Cardhu" (seen); Alcohol Professor, "Speyside's Cardhu" (seen, host refused fetch); Sunday Post, "Cardhu marks 200 years" (seen). The £20,500 sale and its terms.
- **Primary material:** the 1893 sale is documented by Diageo's archive; 2003–04 trade press (Scotsman, Harpers) covered the pure malt affair; Barnard (1887) describes Cardow.

**Risks.** The flag and flour stories are distillery lore; tell them as "the story goes". Founding date varies. The stills-to-Glenfiddich link came only from Wikipedia here; confirm before using it.

**Deck style.** Distillery profile told through two women.

**Tier one because** it puts people the usual account leaves out at the centre, has a modern turn, and every bottle is cheap and everywhere.

### 3. The longest glen in Scotland

**Story.** George Smith took out one of the first licences under the 1823 Excise Act for his Glenlivet distillery in 1824, and was said to carry pistols against his neighbours. As Glenlivet whisky became famous, distilleries well outside the glen called themselves "Glenlivet", which led to the joke that it was the longest glen in Scotland. In 1868 George and John Gordon Smith advertised in the *Wine Trade Review* that theirs was the only licensed distillery in Glenlivet; they registered a trademark and in 1881 John Gordon Smith said he would sue. The settlement (usually dated 1884) gave the Smiths "The Glenlivet" and let others keep "-Glenlivet" as a hyphenated suffix, which about 30 distilleries used into the late 20th century.

**Why enthusiasts would not know it.** Most know "The" Glenlivet; fewer know the 1868 advert, the trademark muddle or which of their own bottles once wore the suffix.

**Bottles.**
- The Glenlivet 12: the original; ~$45.
- Glen Moray Classic (Glen Moray is still registered with "-Glenlivet"): ~$25.
- Aberlour 12 or A'bunadh (once Aberlour-Glenlivet): ~$60 / ~$100.
- Macallan 12 Double Cask (once Macallan-Glenlivet): ~$80. Swap: a Cadenhead's bottling labelled with the old suffix, if a shop has one.

**Sources.**
- Dramface, "Ye Olde Glenlivet Whisky" (2022) (read). Elizabeth Grant's memoir, Smith's 1824 licence, Glen Moray and Tomintoul still registered with the suffix, Cadenhead's use.
- Scotchwhisky.com, "Why are other distilleries called Glenlivet?" (read). The 1868 Wine Trade Review advert, the 1875 trademark error, the 1881 statement of intent.
- Whisky Magazine, "The genuine article" (seen); The Spirits Business, "The Glenlivet: a brand history" (seen; gives 1886 for the court battle).
- University of Glasgow journal article on the case, journals.gla.ac.uk/bih/article/download/598/262/1813 (seen, not opened; probably the best scholarly source).
- **Primary material:** *Wine Trade Review* (1868), Court of Session papers, Elizabeth Grant of Rothiemurchus, *Memoirs of a Highland Lady* (public domain).

**Risks.** The ruling year is given as 1880, 1884 or 1886; Scotchwhisky.com says the outcome was an agreement rather than a court ruling. Which brands used the suffix and when must be checked per bottle.

**Deck style.** Place profile with a blind element (which of these is from the glen?) if it adds fun.

**Tier one because** the bottles are the story, they are cheap, and there is good primary material.

### 4. The gamekeeper's still (Aeneas Coffey)

**Story.** Aeneas Coffey was a Dublin-born excise officer who hunted illicit distillers (he was stabbed during a raid in Donegal in 1810), left the service around 1824 and ran a Dublin distillery. He patented a two-column continuous still in 1830 (granted February 1831). The leading Irish distillers mostly rejected it because its spirit was too light; Scottish grain distillers adopted it, which made cheap grain whisky and the blended Scotch trade possible. The still is still called a Coffey still, and Nikka makes its Coffey Grain in one (Nikka's account of when and how it acquired its stills was not checked).

**Why enthusiasts would not know it.** They know "column still" and "Coffey Grain" as terms. The man, his excise career and the irony of an Irishman's invention working against Irish pot still whiskey are less known.

**Bottles.**
- An Irish single pot still (for example Redbreast 12, ~$70, or Powers Three Swallow, ~$45): what Irish distillers kept making. Both are Irish Distillers products; neither is Jameson.
- Teeling Single Grain or Kilbeggan Single Grain: Irish grain from column stills, made after the Irish market finally took it up; ~$40.
- Nikka Coffey Grain: made in Nikka's Coffey stills; ~$65.
- Compass Box Hedonism: a Scotch grain-only blend; ~$100. Swap: Haig Club or a single grain from an independent bottler.

**Sources.**
- Mary Mulvihill, "Distilled genius that kick-started the whisky industry", Irish Times, 26 Feb 2004 (read). Excise career, 1810 stabbing, 1830 patent, Irish rejection, Scottish adoption.
- Wikipedia, "Aeneas Coffey" and "Column still" (seen). Patent granted 5 February 1831; Grange distillery first in Scotland, 1834.
- Scotchwhisky.com, "Whisky heroes: Aeneas Coffey" (seen); Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails entry (seen).
- **Primary material:** the British patent (1830/31); Parliamentary excise reports of the 1820s; Coffey's own testimony to inquiries.

**Risks.** His death date varies (1839 vs 1852). "Irish distillers rejected it" is a simplification: one source says 13 Irish distilleries adopted it within ten years. Tell it as "the big Dublin houses refused it".

**Deck style.** Story with a side-by-side tasting (pot vs column).

**Tier one because** it is a single person and a single invention you can taste the result of, with a turn that is not a gotcha.

### 5. One chemist, five countries (Dr Jim Swan)

**Story.** Jim Swan, a chemist from Heriot-Watt (PhD 1988 on wood extractives), co-published the first whisky flavour wheel in 1979 and became an authority on cask maturation. From 2002 he consulted for new distilleries around the world, and Whisky Advocate's obituary credits him with Amrut, Penderyn (where he was master blender), Kilchoman and Kavalan. Wikipedia also lists Cotswolds and Milk & Honey. He developed the STR (shave, toast, re-char) treatment of red wine casks that Kavalan and others use. He died at home in Longniddry on 14 February 2017, aged 75.

**Why enthusiasts would not know it.** Many have tasted several of these whiskies without knowing one man connects them.

**Bottles.**
- Penderyn Madeira Finish (Wales): ~$55.
- Kilchoman Machir Bay (Islay): ~$60.
- Kavalan Classic or a Kavalan with STR casks (Taiwan): ~$80.
- Milk & Honey Classic (Tel Aviv): **checked** $51–62 at US retailers.
- Swap: Cotswolds Single Malt (England, ~$65, not price-checked) or Amrut Fusion (India, ~$70).

**Sources.**
- Jonny McCormick, "Remembering Dr. Jim Swan", Whisky Advocate (read). Amrut, Penderyn, Kilchoman, Kavalan; consultancy from 2002; Tatlock & Thomson.
- Wikipedia, "Jim Swan" (read). Flavour wheel 1979, PhD 1988, list including Cotswold and Milk & Honey, STR casks.
- The Spirits Business, "Whisky expert Dr Jim Swan dies" (seen); Penderyn's tribute (seen); Kilchoman's tribute (seen).
- **Primary material:** Swan's published papers (Journal of the Institute of Brewing), the 1979 flavour wheel paper; distillery press releases.

**Risks.** The Cotswolds and Milk & Honey links rest on Wikipedia here; confirm from the distilleries. A night of five young single malts from five countries is a lot of variety; pick four.

**Deck style.** One person's career told through bottles (the direction the skill lists as "not built yet").

**Tier one because** it is the world-whisky night the slate otherwise lacks, with a single thread through it.

### 6. The report Taketsuru wrote

**Story.** Kiichiro Iwai, at Settsu Shuzo, helped send Masataka Taketsuru to Scotland (1918–20), where he studied in Glasgow and worked at Longmorn, Bo'ness and Hazelburn in Campbeltown. By the time Taketsuru returned in 1920, with his wife Rita, Settsu had dropped the plan, so he took his knowledge to Kotobukiya (Yamazaki) and later founded Nikka at Yoichi (1940 per SMWS). Iwai moved to Hombo Shuzo, which got a whisky licence in 1949; using Taketsuru's report, Iwai designed Hombo's Yamanashi distillery (1960) and its pot stills, whose design is still used at Mars Komagatake. Hombo named its blend Iwai Tradition after him.

**Why enthusiasts would not know it.** The Taketsuru and Rita story is well known in outline; that the same report fathered Mars through Iwai is not.

**Bottles.**
- Nikka From the Barrel or Nikka Taketsuru Pure Malt: Taketsuru's line; ~$65 / ~$80.
- Mars Iwai Tradition: named for Iwai; **checked** $35–48 (Wooden Cork). Hombo's own Japanese listing gives the origin as "UK, Japan (grain whisky)".
- Mars Iwai (blue label, bourbon-style): ~$40.
- Hazelburn 10 (Springbank, Campbeltown): named after the distillery where Taketsuru trained; ~$90 and hard to find. Swap: Longmorn via an independent bottler, or Nikka Coffey Grain.

**Sources.**
- Hombo, "IWAI: Kiichiro Iwai, the father of MARS" (read). Iwai sent Taketsuru; Iwai used the report to design the plant; Iwai-type pot still.
- SMWS, "A tale of two Japanese distilleries" (read). Settsu, Kihei Abe, Longmorn, Bo'ness, Hazelburn, 1920 return, Hombo 1949, Yamanashi 1960.
- Quill & Pad, "Mars Shinshu Japanese whiskies" (seen); Dekanta, "The makers of Japanese whisky" (seen).
- **Primary material:** the Taketsuru notebooks (held by Nikka; facsimiles published in Japan); Hombo company history.

**Risks.** Iwai Tradition and Nikka From the Barrel both use imported whisky in some or all blends (Hombo's listing says UK malt). That fits the story (Scotland in a bottle named for the man who sent Taketsuru) but overlaps the owner's Hakata deck on labelling; say it plainly and move on. Hazelburn is hard to get.

**Deck style.** Story with pours alongside; two lineages from one document.

**Tier one because** it turns a story enthusiasts half know into one with a second, less-told half and clear bottles on both sides.

### 7. The town that kept its licence (Kilbeggan)

**Story.** The distillery at Kilbeggan, County Westmeath, dates its licence to 1757 and was run by the Locke family from the 19th century. In 1947 the Locke family sold it to a Swiss-based group (Trans-World Trust in one account); in the Dáil, Oliver J. Flanagan alleged that Fianna Fáil figures were tied to a plan to sell its stock on the black market, and a tribunal of inquiry largely rejected the allegations. Production stopped in 1954 (some sources say 1953 or 1957). The village formed a preservation association in 1982; Cooley Distillery bought the Locke's and Kilbeggan names (1987–88) and restarted distilling at Kilbeggan on 19 March 2007, using a pot still described as among the oldest working in the world.

**Why enthusiasts would not know it.** Kilbeggan is a cheap blend on the bottom shelf to most US drinkers; the 1947 political scandal is almost unknown outside Ireland.

**Bottles.**
- Kilbeggan Traditional Irish Whiskey: the blend that carried the name; ~$25.
- Kilbeggan Single Pot Still: distilled at the restarted distillery; **checked** $38–45 at US retailers (US launch $44.99).
- Kilbeggan Single Grain: Cooley's column grain; ~$35.
- Connemara peated single malt (Cooley): the distillery that revived Kilbeggan; ~$50. Swap: Tyrconnell.

**Sources.**
- Dáil Éireann debates, 22 and 29 October 1947, oireachtas.ie (seen; the page refused a scripted fetch). Questions on the Swiss sale and export quotas.
- Oireachtas Committee of Public Accounts appendix 1950, archive.oireachtas.ie (seen).
- IBEC/Drinks Ireland, "Kilbeggan Distillery: the story of how community spirit kept a village's distilling tradition alive" (seen); Diffords Guide, "Old Kilbeggan Distillery" (seen; 1954 stop, 19 March 2007 restart); History Ireland, "The Locke family and the distilling industry in Kilbeggan" (seen; fetch refused); Irish Times, "An Irishman's Diary" (seen).
- **Primary material:** the Dáil record and the 1947 tribunal report; Irish newspapers of 1947 (Irish Newspaper Archives).

**Risks.** I could not open the Dáil pages or History Ireland article, so the scandal rests on search extracts; read them before building. Dates differ. The Dáil text names a buyer "Mr. Jameson" (a person, not the firm); handle so it does not read as the brand. Overlaps the existing Irish decline-and-return deck; keep this to one town.

**Deck style.** Place profile with history poured alongside.

**Tier one because** it has primary sources most whiskey writers never use, a real turn, and cheap bottles made at the place.

### 8. Where the wheat went (Stitzel-Weller)

**Story.** Old Fitzgerald began as a brand of S.C. Herbst of Milwaukee, who claimed a distiller named John E. Fitzgerald built his distillery. According to the Van Winkle family history (*But Always Fine Bourbon*, Sally Van Winkle Campbell, 1999), Fitzgerald was a Treasury agent with keys to the bonded warehouse who helped himself to the best barrels, and the name was an inside joke. Pappy Van Winkle bought the brand during Prohibition and made it the flagship of Stitzel-Weller (opened after Repeal; the 1935 date is from memory and unchecked), which made only wheated bourbon. Bill Samuels Jr. has said Pappy advised his father when Maker's Mark started in the 1950s and gave him the Old Fitz yeast, though first distiller Elmo Beam brought his own; a University of Louisville oral history credits Stitzel-Weller distiller Roy Hawes with help on the recipe. Stitzel-Weller stopped distilling in 1992; Weller went to Buffalo Trace, Old Fitzgerald to Heaven Hill, which launched Larceny in 2012 as a nod to the Treasury agent.

**Why enthusiasts would not know it.** They know Pappy and wheaters. The Treasury agent, the yeast story and where each brand ended up are scattered.

**Bottles.**
- Larceny Small Batch: named for the Fitzgerald story; ~$30.
- Old Fitzgerald Bottled in Bond (decanter series) or Larceny Barrel Proof: the brand itself; ~$100 / ~$60.
- W.L. Weller Special Reserve: Stitzel-Weller's other wheater, now Buffalo Trace; ~$30 shelf, often hard to find.
- Maker's Mark: the distillery Pappy is said to have helped start; ~$30.
- Swap: Rebel Yell (a Stitzel-Weller brand, now made elsewhere).

**Sources.**
- Chuck Cowdery, "Who was John E. Fitzgerald?" (2011) (read). Herbst's story vs. the Treasury agent; Van Winkle sale.
- The Whiskey Wash, "That old story about Pappy, Maker's Mark and Old Fitzgerald" (read). Samuels Jr. on the Old Fitz yeast and Elmo Beam's yeast.
- The Whiskey Wash, "Do the Stitzels get too much credit for modern wheated bourbon?" (seen); Michael Veach, "The Stitzel factor in bourbon" (seen); Federation of Historical Bottle Collectors, "The wisdom of Solomon: Old Fitzgerald" (2021) (seen).
- **Primary material:** Sally Van Winkle Campbell's book; University of Louisville oral history collection (the Hawes interview); Herbst-era advertising.

**Risks.** The Treasury-agent account rests mainly on one family book; the yeast story is secondhand. Both are "the story goes". Old Fitzgerald BiB is pricey and Weller is allocated in many states.

**Deck style.** One recipe across makers.

**Tier one because** it is a shared-recipe story with a con, a family book and four findable bottles.

### 9. The Colonel's castle (E.H. Taylor and George Stagg)

**Story.** Edmund H. Taylor Jr. bought the O.F.C. distillery in Frankfort in 1869 and was forced into bankruptcy in May 1877. George T. Stagg, a St. Louis whiskey merchant and creditor, bought him out at 20 cents on the dollar and took the O.F.C. and the Old Oscar Pepper distillery. Taylor left the partnership on 1 January 1887 and built Old Taylor, with its limestone castle, in Woodford County. When Stagg began using Taylor's script signature in 1889, Taylor sued; Kentucky's appellate courts ruled in 1894 and 1902. O.F.C. became Buffalo Trace. Old Taylor sat abandoned until Peristyle bought it in 2014 and opened it as Castle & Key in 2016; Sazerac, which owns the Taylor names, sued over the name and lost on summary judgment.

**Why enthusiasts would not know it.** They know E.H. Taylor as a Buffalo Trace label and Castle & Key as a pretty distillery. That the two men fought over the signature now on the label is less known.

**Bottles.**
- Buffalo Trace Kentucky Straight Bourbon: from the O.F.C. site Taylor built and lost; ~$30.
- Colonel E.H. Taylor Small Batch: the signature fought over; ~$45 MSRP, often more, allocated.
- Castle & Key Small Batch Bourbon: made in Taylor's castle; ~$50.
- Castle & Key Restoration Rye: ~$45. Swap: Stagg (barrel proof, allocated) if anyone has one.

**Sources.**
- Brian Haara, "The origin of Col. E.H. Taylor Jr.'s signature (as told by three trademark rulings)" (2013) (read). 1869, May 1877, 20 cents on the dollar, 1887, the 1894 and 1902 cases; Haara found 14 cases involving Taylor.
- The Whiskey Wash, "Old Taylor distillery trademark bourbon war being fought in the courts" (read). Peristyle 2014, Castle & Key 2016, Judge Van Tatenhove's ruling.
- Courthouse News, "Bourbon war waged in Sixth Circuit" (seen); Brian Haara, "Col. E.H. Taylor Jr. running from creditors in the summer of '77" (seen).
- **Primary material:** *Geo. T. Stagg Co. v. Taylor*, 95 Ky. 651 (1894); *Geo. T. Stagg Co. v. E.H. Taylor Jr. & Sons*, 113 Ky. 709 (1902); the federal Sazerac v. Peristyle docket.

**Risks.** E.H. Taylor is allocated. The Sixth Circuit appeal outcome was not checked.

**Deck style.** Story with pours alongside; can end at the castle.

**Tier one because** the court records are public, the people are vivid, and two distilleries in the story make the whiskey today.

### 10. Rye from a still nobody built for 100 years (Maryland rye)

**Story.** Before Prohibition, Maryland and Pennsylvania rye distillers commonly used the three-chamber (or "charge") still; one study cited by Leopold of 31 pre-Prohibition distilleries found bourbon plants used column stills and rye plants three-chamber stills. It cost far more copper per barrel, and after Repeal nobody built one. Maryland's rye industry largely died with it; Pikesville, a Maryland brand from the 1890s, outlasted the rest until production ended about 1972 and Heaven Hill bought the name in 1982. In Denver, Todd Leopold read the old government studies, had Vendome Copper & Brass build a three-chamber still (dates given as 2014–15 or 2018), and released Three Chamber Rye in 2021, alongside a Maryland-style rye. Sagamore Spirit opened in Baltimore in 2017.

**Why enthusiasts would not know it.** Many know Pikesville; few know what a three-chamber still is or that its loss is part of why "Maryland rye" vanished as a style.

**Bottles.**
- Pikesville Straight Rye (110 proof, Kentucky-made under a Maryland name): ~$55.
- Sagamore Spirit Straight Rye (Baltimore): ~$40.
- Leopold Bros Maryland-Style Rye: **checked** $50–66.
- Leopold Bros Three Chamber Rye: **checked** $70 (8-year standard, Seelbach's) to $240–250 (cask strength / holiday editions).

**Sources.**
- The Whiskey Wash, "Whiskey history revived as Leopold Bros goes old school with 3 chamber still" (read). Leopold reading IRS studies of Pennsylvania and Maryland distillers.
- Leopold Bros, "Three Chamber" tech sheet PDF (seen); Whisky Advocate, "Recapturing rye's roots" (seen; 2021 release, Abruzzi rye); Chuck Cowdery, "The chamber still is reborn" (2015) (seen); Punch, "Does craft exist in spirits anymore?" (seen).
- Baltimore Magazine, "Rye whiskey makes a comeback in Maryland" (seen); Heaven Hill, Pikesville page (seen); Garden & Gun, "Maryland's spirited revival" (seen).
- **Primary material:** pre-Prohibition US Internal Revenue studies of distilling practice that Leopold cites (not located online this session); Maryland newspapers on Chronicling America.

**Risks.** The still's build date conflicts between sources. The three-chamber bottles vary widely in price. Pennsylvania (Monongahela) rye could be folded in or split out (see candidate 13).

**Deck style.** A production choice you can taste, plus a lost style brought back.

**Tier one because** it is a lost style, a single piece of equipment and a person, with four bottles that map exactly onto the story.

## Tier two

### 11. Walkerville (Hiram Walker and Canadian Club)

**Story.** Detroit merchant Hiram Walker built a distillery across the river in Windsor, Ontario in 1858 and a company town, Walkerville, around it. His "Club" whisky sold so well in the US that, by most accounts, American distillers pushed for a country-of-origin label; Walker added "Canadian" in 1889 and made it part of the name in 1890 (the brand's own site says 1882). Today the Hiram Walker plant is owned by Pernod Ricard and run by Corby, and also makes Canadian Club under contract for Suntory. In 1998 master distiller Mike Booth released Lot No. 40, a 100% rye from a copper pot still, named after a lot where his ancestor Joshua Booth ran a mill and still in the 1790s; it was dropped and relaunched in 2012.

**Why enthusiasts would not know it.** Canadian Club is seen as a cheap mixer; that two rival companies' whiskies come out of one Windsor plant, and the Booth family story behind Lot 40, are not widely known.

### 18. Nearest Green

### 28. American single malt gets a definition

**Story.** American distillers made malt whisky for decades with no legal category of their own (Stranahan's from 2004, Westland, Balcones, Westward, Copperworks). ### 29. Tom Bullock's book

**Story.** Tom Bullock (born Louisville, 18 October 1872; died 1964), the son of a formerly enslaved Union soldier, tended bar at Louisville's Pendennis and Kenton clubs and then the St. Louis Country Club. **Why enthusiasts would not know it.** Cocktail historians know him; most drinkers do not.

**Bottles.** Drinks from the book built with Kentucky whiskey (for example Old Forester 1870 or 1897 for a Louisville pour of the era, ~$50; a rye such as Old Overholt BiB or Rittenhouse, ~$30); the book itself is public domain on Project Gutenberg.

### 30. Washington's distillers

**Story.** In 1797 George Washington's Scottish farm manager James Anderson persuaded him to build a distillery at Mount Vernon. Six enslaved men, Hanson, Peter, Nat, Daniel, James and Timothy, did the work; by 1799 it made about 11,000 gallons, mostly rye-based, sold to neighbours and Alexandria merchants. The distillery was rebuilt and reopened in 2007, with Dave Pickerell among those involved.

**Why enthusiasts would not know it.** Many know Washington had a distillery; fewer know the names of the men who ran it.

**Bottles.** Mount Vernon Premium Straight Rye ($225, sold at the estate and online but shippable only in Virginia and DC); Spirit of '76 bourbon (July 2026, about 300 bottles at $1,000, sold out); swaps: a Virginia rye (for example Catoctin Creek, ~$40) or a Pennsylvania-style rye.

**Sources.** Mount Vernon, "Ten facts about the distillery" and the digital encyclopedia entry "Washington's distillery" (seen; mountvernon.org refused fetch); Mount Vernon blog, "Making George Washington bourbon" (2018) (seen); Spectrum News, "Washington's rebuilt distillery to release first bourbon" (June 2026) (read). **Primary:** Washington's farm reports and ledgers (Mount Vernon library, Founders Online); Anderson's letters.

**Risks.** The bottles from the place are nearly impossible outside Virginia. Sensitive history; needs care with the enslaved men's names and roles, and the records are written by their enslaver.

**Deck style.** Story with pours alongside.

**Tier three because** the story matters but the natural bottles are not on US shelves.

## Why the tiers fall as they do

- **Tier one** stories have three things together: named people, a real turn (a court case, a rejected invention, a scandal, a forgotten still), and bottles that are characters in the story and cheap enough to buy for one night. Most also have primary sources a host can cite (court reports, Dáil records, patents, trade adverts).
- **Tier two** stories are good but short one of those: the bottles are tributes (Mary Dowling), all one brand (Four Roses, Pulteney), hard or costly (Hanyu, Campbeltown), or the story is partly known or contested (Canadian Club label law, Nearest Green's current litigation).
- **Tier three** stories are worth knowing but the bottles are generic, gone or local (Washington, Bullock, Shackleton) or the story is mostly a rule (American single malt).
