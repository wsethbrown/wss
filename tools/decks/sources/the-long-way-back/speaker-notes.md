## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] The Long Way Back

Tonight is about Irish whiskey, and how close it came to disappearing.

In the 1880s Irish whiskey was the best-regarded whiskey in the English-speaking world and Dublin was where much of it was made. A hundred years later there were two distilleries left on the whole island, both owned by the same company. Today there are dozens again. I'll go through how that happened, in order, and we'll taste four bottles along the way that each come out of a different part of the story.

## [SLIDE 2] Dublin's four big distillers in the 1880s

Start in Dublin in the 1880s. Four firms dominated the trade there. John Jameson at Bow Street, on the north side of the river. William Jameson, a separate firm of the same family, at Marrowbone Lane. John Power at John's Lane. George Roe at Thomas Street.

The picture is the Bow Street distillery as it was engraved for Alfred Barnard, a journalist who toured the distilleries of the United Kingdom between 1885 and 1887, twenty-nine of them in Ireland, and published the results in 1887.

These were big industrial sites in the middle of the city. They made whiskey in copper pot stills, they sold it in casks to merchants and bottlers, and it went all over Britain, the colonies and America.

## [SLIDE 3] Single pot still: malted and unmalted barley

What these distilleries made was a particular kind of whiskey that the industry now calls single pot still.

The mash is barley, but some of it is malted and some isn't. Malting is the step where you soak the grain and let it start to sprout, so that its starch can be turned into sugar. The Dublin distillers malted part of their barley and used the rest raw, sometimes with a little oats as well. Then they distilled it in large copper pot stills, usually three times.

The explanation you'll hear on every distillery tour is the malt tax. From 1785 malted barley was taxed in Ireland, so distillers cut their tax bill by using less malt and more raw grain. Some historians point out that distillers already used raw grain before the tax, so it probably hurried the change along rather than starting it.

What matters for tonight is that raw barley gives a different spirit from malt. It's often described as oily or creamy in the mouth, with a bit of spice.

## [SLIDE 4] Pour 1 of 4: Redbreast 12 Year Old

So let's taste one. This is Redbreast 12, made at Midleton in County Cork by Irish Distillers. It's a single pot still whiskey, which means it's made the way I've just described, malted and unmalted barley in pot stills, all at one distillery.

Redbreast began as a merchant's brand. Gilbey's, a firm of wine and spirits merchants in Dublin, matured pot still whiskey from the Bow Street distillery in their own casks and bottled it, and the Redbreast name appears from 1912. That was the normal way whiskey reached customers then. Irish Distillers bought the brand in 1986, and it's now made and bottled by the distillery.

Taste it neat. Before you think about flavour, pay attention to how it feels in your mouth, because that texture is what people mean when they talk about pot still whiskey. Keep a bit in the glass, because I'd like you to come back to it later.

## [SLIDE 5] Pot still and Coffey still

In 1830 a man called Aeneas Coffey patented a new kind of still. Coffey was Irish. He had risen to the head of the excise service in Ireland, left it in 1824 and become a distiller in Dublin, so he knew the trade from both sides. The patent was applied for in 1830 and granted early in 1831.

His still was a pair of tall columns. Wash goes in at the top, steam comes up from the bottom, and spirit comes off continuously, with no need to stop, empty and refill the way you do with a pot still. It was cheap to run, and the spirit came off strong and light in flavour.

The Dublin distillers didn't want it. They thought what came out of it hardly counted as whiskey, and their reputation rested on the heavier pot still spirit. Coffey ended up taking his business to London, and his best customers were grain distillers in Scotland. In the following decades Scottish firms learned to blend that cheap, light grain spirit with smaller amounts of their own malt whisky, and they had a product that was consistent, easy to drink and much cheaper to make.

## [SLIDE 6] 1909: A Royal Commission declines to limit whisky to the pot still

The Dublin firms fought this openly. In 1878 the four big houses published a book called "Truths about Whisky". It argued that patent still spirit was not whiskey and described it as "pure alcohol and water, or nearly so, without distinctive flavour or quality".

The argument came to a head in a Royal Commission on Whiskey and Other Potable Spirits, which sat from 1908 and published its final report in August 1909. It's the British version of the American "What is whisky?" fight from the same years. The commission did not condemn the patent still, and it recommended no restriction on the kind of apparatus used to make spirit sold as whisky. So grain whisky from a Coffey still was whisky, and so was a blend.

That was a defeat for Dublin. The blenders now had the law on their side, and the Scotch blends that people still know by name were already selling hard in England and abroad.

The picture is John's Lane, again from Barnard.

## [SLIDE 7] From 1922, two jurisdictions

Next came partition. Northern Ireland stayed in the United Kingdom, and the Irish Free State was set up in 1922 for the other twenty-six counties.

For whiskey that split the industry in two. Most of the distilleries, including the Dublin houses, Cork, Tullamore and Kilbeggan, were now in a new state. Bushmills, on the north coast of Antrim, and the big Belfast distilleries were in the UK, and kept selling into the British market on the same terms as before.

The photograph is Bushmills, taken by Robert French for the Lawrence studio in Dublin some time before the First World War.

You'll see the year 1608 on Bushmills bottles. That's the year James I granted a licence to distil in that part of County Antrim to Sir Thomas Phillips, a local landowner. It wasn't a licence for this distillery. The company that ran it was formed in 1784.

## [SLIDE 8] Pour 2 of 4: Bushmills 10 Year Old

Second pour, from the north. Bushmills 10 Year Old is a single malt, which means all the barley was malted, with no raw grain. Like most Irish whiskey it's distilled three times.

So this is a useful comparison with the Redbreast. Both come from pot stills, both are triple distilled, both are at 40 percent. The main difference is the raw barley in the Redbreast. See whether you find this one lighter in the mouth, and whether the Redbreast seems thicker by comparison.

Bushmills has changed hands several times. Irish Distillers owned it from 1972 until 2005, when it was sold to Diageo, and in 2014 Diageo traded it to the owners of Jose Cuervo for the other half of Don Julio tequila.

## [SLIDE 9] The export markets close, 1920 to 1938

In 1920 the United States went dry. America had been one of the biggest markets for Irish whiskey, and it was closed for thirteen years. You'll hear that the Irish distillers lost out because they refused to deal with bootleggers while the Scots happily shipped to Canada and the Bahamas for smuggling. I've seen that told many times and never seen good evidence for it, so treat it as a story. What is true is that the legal American market was gone.

In 1922 the Free State was founded. And in 1932 the Irish government under de Valera stopped paying land annuities to Britain, and Britain answered with penal tariffs on Irish goods. Ireland put tariffs back. That trade war lasted until 1938, and for six years Irish goods paid heavy duties to get into Britain, which bought about nine-tenths of the country's exports.

When Prohibition ended in 1933, American drinkers came back to a market where Scotch blends were ready, cheap and well distributed. The Irish distillers had less stock, fewer agents, and a style that was heavier and dearer to make.

The photograph shows liquor being destroyed during Prohibition.

## [SLIDE 10] 1966: The last three distilling firms in the Republic merge

By the 1960s the Republic had three distilling companies left. John Jameson and Son, John Power and Son, and the Cork Distilleries Company, which owned the old distillery at Midleton and made Paddy.

In 1966 they merged. Firms that had competed for generations decided that there wasn't room for three of them in a shrinking trade. The group is known as Irish Distillers. A launch booklet from 1966 shown by the Irish Whiskey Society uses the name United Distillers of Ireland, so the name seems to have changed soon after.

Bushmills joined the group in 1972, which put almost the whole Irish industry, north and south, under one owner.

## [SLIDE 11] Dublin closes and Midleton opens, 1971 to 1976

Before and after the merger, the old distilleries went one by one.

Tullamore and Locke's at Kilbeggan, the distillery on our cover, had already stopped in 1954. Kilbeggan's licence dates from 1757. The buildings were left standing, and Cooley started distilling there again in 2007.

Irish Distillers decided that the Dublin sites had no future. Bow Street stopped distilling in 1971 (some sources say 1972) and its production moved to John's Lane for a few years. Instead of rebuilding in Dublin, the company built one new distillery next to the old one at Midleton in County Cork, which took over production in July 1975. John's Lane closed in 1976. George Roe and William Jameson had gone long before. For the first time in a couple of centuries, nobody was distilling whiskey in Dublin.

## [SLIDE 12] 2 working distilleries on the island by 1980

Irish Distillers also owned Coleraine in the north, which by then made only grain whiskey. Once Midleton could make grain whiskey, Coleraine was no longer needed, and it closed in 1978. So by 1980 there were two working whiskey distilleries on the island of Ireland, Midleton and Bushmills, and Irish Distillers owned both.

New Midleton was designed to make everything under one roof. It has pot stills for pot still whiskey and column stills for grain whiskey, and it can run several recipes. So Powers, Paddy, Redbreast and the other old brands, which had once come from separate distilleries in different cities, were all made in one place.

It also meant that, through the 1980s, all new Irish whiskey was distilled at one of those two sites. Older stock from the closed distilleries was still being bottled for some years, but it belonged to the same company.

## [SLIDE 13] Pour 3 of 4: Powers Gold Label

Third pour, and it comes from one of the four Dublin houses. Powers was the whiskey of John Power and Son at John's Lane, the distillery in the engraving a few slides back. When John's Lane closed in 1976 the brand moved to Midleton with everything else.

Gold Label today is a blend of pot still whiskey and grain whiskey from the column stills at Midleton. That's exactly the combination the Dublin distillers refused to make a hundred years earlier, and John Power and Son was one of the four firms behind the book that said so. It's bottled at 43.2 percent, a little stronger than most standard blends.

Taste it next to the Redbreast you kept back. Both come from the same distillery and both contain pot still whiskey. The difference you notice is mostly the grain whiskey, which thins out the body and softens the spice.

## [SLIDE 14] The revival starts at Cooley in 1987

The revival begins with John Teeling, a businessman who thought that one company owning all of Irish whiskey was an opportunity. In 1987 he bought a former state-owned plant at Riverstown on the Cooley peninsula in County Louth, Ceimici Teoranta, which had made alcohol from potatoes, and added pot stills to its column stills to make whiskey. Cooley also took over the Locke's name and the old Kilbeggan buildings. Accounts differ on whether that was 1987 or 1988.

In 1988 Pernod Ricard, the French drinks company, bought Irish Distillers after a takeover fight, and it still owns it. In 1993 Irish Distillers notified the Competition Authority that it intended to buy Cooley. The Authority blocked the takeover.

Whiskey has to sit in a cask for three years before it can be sold as Irish whiskey, so a new distillery has to spend for a long time before it earns anything. Cooley stayed independent until Beam agreed to buy it in December 2011, for about 95 million dollars, and the sale completed in January 2012. It is now part of Suntory.

After that the numbers grew quickly. Teeling's sons opened a distillery in Dublin in 2015, the first new one in the city for over 125 years, and new distilleries opened in most counties. By the 2020s there were dozens.

## [SLIDE 15] Pour 4 of 4: Connemara Original Peated Single Malt

Last pour, Connemara, which Cooley launched in 1996.

Cooley did two things the rest of the industry had dropped. They dried some of their malt over peat smoke, which most Irish distillers had stopped doing long before, and they distilled twice, which is the Scottish practice, instead of three times.

So expect smoke, and a different weight from the Bushmills even though both are single malts. Compare it with the Bushmills if you've any left.

## [SLIDE 16] Reading an Irish whiskey label

To finish, a quick guide to what an Irish whiskey label tells you.

Irish whiskey has to be distilled and aged on the island of Ireland, for at least three years in wooden casks. That's set by the Irish Whiskey Act of 1980 and by the technical file that protects the name in the EU.

Single pot still means malted and unmalted barley, at least 30 percent of each, in pot stills at one distillery, like the Redbreast. Single malt means malted barley only, pot stills, one distillery, like the Bushmills and the Connemara. Blended means a mix of two or more styles, which in practice usually means grain whiskey with pot still or malt, like the Powers.

The last line is the practical one. Many of the brands on the shelf now are younger than their whiskey. A brand that launched in 2015 and sells a ten-year-old whiskey bought it from someone else. For years the only sources were Midleton, Bushmills and Cooley. Look on the back label for where it was distilled, and if it only names the company and not a distillery, you can assume it was bought in.
