# The Long Way Back

Research outline: Irish whiskey, 1880 to now

Irish whiskey, 1880 to now. A guide for anyone who wants to research the subject and build a talk of their own.

---

**A note on sources.** The facts in this deck were checked against the sources listed under each module in November 2026, and corrected where they were wrong. Where sources disagree, the outline says so. What could not be confirmed is listed under "Not confirmed" near the end.

## Module 1. The Dublin pot-still houses

**The question.** Who made Irish whiskey at its height, and how was it sold?

### Nailed down

- Four Dublin firms led the trade in the later nineteenth century: John Jameson (Bow Street), William Jameson (Marrowbone Lane), John Power (John's Lane) and George Roe (Thomas Street). The four published _Truths about Whisky_ together in 1878.
- Alfred Barnard, a journalist commissioned by _Harper's Weekly Gazette_, toured the distilleries of the United Kingdom between 1885 and 1887 (29 in Ireland by one count) and published _The Whisky Distilleries of the United Kingdom_ in 1887.
- Redbreast began as a merchant's brand: Gilbey's of Dublin matured and bottled pot still whiskey from Bow Street, and the Redbreast name appears from 1912. Irish Distillers bought the brand in 1986. Sources disagree on when Gilbey's started the product (1887 and 1903 are both given).

### Traps

- "Irish whiskey was the most popular in the world" is repeated everywhere. It is a fair summary of reputation, but find export and output figures before claiming a market share.
- The founding dates on bottles are company dates, not licence records.

## Module 2. Single pot still and the malt tax

**The question.** Why did Irish distillers mash unmalted barley with malt?

### Nailed down

- The traditional Irish pot still mash combined malted and unmalted barley, sometimes with small amounts of oats or other grain.
- A duty on malted barley was introduced in Ireland in 1785 and is the usual explanation for the unmalted share. Historians such as Fionnán O'Connor argue that the use of unmalted grain was already known, so the tax was a catalyst rather than the origin.
- The Technical File defines malt Irish whiskey as 100% malted barley, distilled in pot stills; blended Irish whiskey is a mix of two or more of the malt, pot still and grain styles.
- The Irish Whiskey Technical File sets the pot still mash at a minimum of 30 percent malted barley and 30 percent unmalted barley, with up to 5 percent other cereals; "single" means distilled at one distillery. A proposal to raise the other-cereal limit to 30 percent has been reported; check the current file before quoting the 5 percent.

- EU geographical indications register, Irish whiskey specification: https://ec.europa.eu/geographical-indications-register/eambrosia-public-api/api/v1/attachments/55944

### Primary documents

- The malt duty statutes for Ireland (1785, repealed 1880).

### Traps

- The malt-tax explanation is the standard one and may be only part of the story. Raw grain was cheaper whatever the tax. Present it as the usual explanation.
- Triple distillation is common in Irish whiskey but is not a legal requirement, and not every Irish distillery does it.

## Module 3. The Coffey still and the blenders

**The question.** Why did the Dublin houses refuse the patent still, and what did it cost them?

### Nailed down

- Aeneas Coffey rose to the head of the excise service in Ireland, left it in 1824 and distilled in Dublin (the Dock Distillery, Grand Canal Street). He applied for his patent in 1830; it was granted on 5 February 1831 (no. 5974). He later moved his business to London and died in 1852.
- _Truths about Whisky_ (Dublin distillers, 1878) called patent still spirit "pure alcohol and water, or nearly so, without distinctive flavour or quality".
- The Royal Commission on Whiskey and Other Potable Spirits was announced in February 1908, sat until 1909 and published its final report in August 1909. It did not condemn the patent still and recommended no restriction on the apparatus used to make spirit sold as whisky.

- Coffey is sometimes called the inventor of the column still. He improved and patented a design; others had continuous stills earlier.
- The Commission's exact definition of whisky is often quoted from secondary sources. Read the Command Paper before quoting it.

## Module 4. Partition, Prohibition and the trade war

**The question.** Which blows of the 1920s and 1930s did the most damage?

### Nailed down

- US national Prohibition took effect in January 1920 (sources give the 16th or 17th) and ended with the ratification of the Twenty-first Amendment on 5 December 1933.
- The Irish Free State was established on 6 December 1922 for 26 counties. Northern Ireland, created under the Government of Ireland Act 1920, opted out the next day and remained in the United Kingdom.
- The Anglo-Irish trade war began in 1932 when the Fianna Fáil government stopped paying land annuities; Britain imposed penal tariffs on Irish imports and Ireland replied with its own. Britain took about 90 percent of Irish exports. The 1938 agreement ended it, with a one-off payment of £10 million.

### Primary documents

### Traps

- The claim that Irish distillers refused to sell to bootleggers while the Scots supplied them is widely told and thinly sourced. Name it as a story; the sources that repeat it are popular retellings.
- The claim that the Irish government restricted whiskey exports during the Second World War is also commonly told. Check Irish government records before using it.
- Do not blame any single blow. The decline was the sum of lost markets, competition from blends, and the cost of the pot still style.

## Module 5. Consolidation: Irish Distillers and Midleton

**The question.** How did the industry end up as one company with two distilleries?

### Nailed down

- In 1966 John Jameson & Son, John Power & Son and the Cork Distilleries Company merged. Most accounts call the group Irish Distillers from the start; a 1966 launch booklet reproduced on the Irish Whiskey Society forum uses the name United Distillers of Ireland, so the name seems to have changed soon after.
- Bow Street stopped distilling in 1971 (Jameson's own timeline says 1972), and its production moved to John's Lane. New Midleton took over production in July 1975. John's Lane closed in 1976.
- Irish Distillers took over Bushmills in 1972. Pernod Ricard sold Bushmills to Diageo in 2005; in 2014 Diageo traded it to Jose Cuervo for the other half of Don Julio. It is now owned through Proximo Spirits.
- Bushmills' 1608 date is a licence to distil granted by James I to Sir Thomas Phillips for the district; the distillery company was formed in 1784.
- Tullamore closed in 1954 (Wikipedia). Locke's at Kilbeggan stopped production in 1954 and closed in 1957 (some give 1953 or 1958). Kilbeggan's licence dates from 1757.
- The Cork Distilleries Company, formed in 1867, owned the old Midleton distillery and made Paddy.
- Coleraine, by then a grain distillery in the group, closed in 1978, leaving Midleton and Bushmills as the only working distilleries on the island until Cooley.

## Module 6. The revival from Cooley

**The question.** Who broke the monopoly, and how did the industry grow again?

### Nailed down

- In 1987 John Teeling bought Ceimici Teoranta, a former state-owned potato-alcohol plant at Riverstown on the Cooley peninsula, County Louth, and added pot stills to its column stills. (Some sources give 1985 or 1989; 1987 is the most common.)
- Cooley took over the Locke's name and the Kilbeggan site, in 1987 or 1988 (sources differ). Distilling restarted at Kilbeggan in 2007.
- Pernod Ricard won Irish Distillers in 1988 after a contested takeover in which Grand Metropolitan (through GC&C Brands), Allied-Lyons and Guinness had also bid.
- Connemara, Cooley's peated, double-distilled single malt, launched in 1996. Cooley is owned by Suntory Global Spirits (Suntory bought Beam in 2014).
- Teeling's distillery in Newmarket, Dublin, first distilled in March 2015 and opened in June 2015, the first new distillery in the city in over 125 years.
- In 1993 Irish Distillers notified the Competition Authority of a proposed acquisition of Cooley (notification CA/62/93). The Authority blocked it.
- Beam agreed to buy Cooley in December 2011 for about $95 million; the deal completed on 18 January 2012. At the time Cooley was one of three sources of Irish whiskey and the only independent one.
- The Irish Whiskey Act 1980 requires maturation in wooden casks for at least three years, in the State, in Northern Ireland, or across both.

## Not confirmed

Nothing on the slides rests on these; the script either avoids them or names the disagreement.

- **The Royal Commission's exact definition of whisky.** Secondary sources agree it did not restrict the still; none quoted the definition. Searched for the "diastase of malt" wording. Settle with the final report, Cd. 4796 (1909).
- **The merged group's first name in 1966.** Only one source (an Irish Whiskey Society forum post with a scanned booklet) gives "United Distillers of Ireland"; Wikipedia says Irish Distillers from the start. The script reports the booklet as such. Settle with the _Irish Times_ archive for July 1966.
- **Bow Street, 1971 or 1972.** Most sources say 1971; Jameson's own timeline says 1972. The slide uses 1971 and the script mentions the other date.
- **Cooley and Kilbeggan, 1987 or 1988.** Sources split; the script says so. Settle with Cooley Distillery plc filings.
- **Tullamore's 1954 closure** rests on Wikipedia alone. Settle with Revenue licence records or the Offaly History society.
- **The bootlegger story** (Irish distillers refused to supply smugglers). Only popular retellings repeat it; the script names it as a story.
- **How hard the 1930s trade war hit whiskey in particular.** Sources describe tariffs on Irish goods generally; whiskey-specific figures were not found. The script speaks of Irish goods. Settle with Free State trade statistics for spirits, 1932–1938.
- **The Second World War export-restriction story** (Module 4 trap). Not used in the deck.
