# Cardhu: The Women Behind Johnnie Walker's Malt

Research outline: Helen and Elizabeth Cumming, Cardow, and the malt John Walker & Sons bought in 1893

_This is the other half of the speaker notes. It does not tell you what to say; it tells you where to look, what is settled, what rests on a single account or on family tradition, and which repeated claims will trip you up. Facts were checked in November 2026 against the documents listed at the end, each marked with how much of it was read. Prices, stock and what Diageo says about its blends move quickly: re-check them before the night._

> **THE SPINE OF THE STORY**
> John and Helen Cumming take a 19-year lease on Cardow farm, Knockando, and make whisky there without a licence (from 1811) → after the Excise Act of 1823 John Cumming takes out a licence (1824) and their son Lewis runs the distillery for most of the next fifty years → Lewis dies (February 1872) and his widow Elizabeth takes over the farm and the distillery → she builds a new, larger distillery beside the old one (1884–85), which Alfred Barnard describes on his tour (published 1887) → the old plant, stills included, is sold for about £120 to William Grant, who uses it to start Glenfiddich (1886–87) → the Cummings sell Cardow to John Walker & Sons for £20,500, with Walker shares and a board seat for Elizabeth's son John (19 September 1893); Elizabeth dies the next May → Cardhu becomes Walker's, and later Diageo's, Speyside malt → Diageo sells a blend of malts under the Cardhu name as "Cardhu Pure Malt" (from mid-2003), the industry objects, Diageo changes the packaging (4 December 2003) and then drops the pure malt (March 2004) → the Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009 ban the phrase "pure malt" and reserve listed distillery names, Cardhu among them, for whisky wholly distilled at that distillery.

## How to use this

Each module gives the question worth answering, what is established (with dates), the sources, threads worth pulling, and the traps. The nineteenth-century story rests mainly on Gavin D. Smith's article for the Scotch Malt Whisky Society (2022), which quotes family letters and local newspapers, and on Alfred Barnard's own account of his visit. The 2003 chapter rests on BBC News, the Guardian and the trade press of the time, and the 2009 chapter on the regulations themselves. Where a claim rests on one account, on the distillery's own marketing, or on family tradition, it is listed in **Not confirmed** and said aloud as "the story goes" or "one account says".

## Module 1. John and Helen Cumming at Cardow

_Who were the Cummings, and what is known about Helen's part before the licence?_

### What's established

- **1811.** "On Whitsunday 1811, one John Cumming took on a 19-year lease on Cardow Farm in the parish of Knockando" (Smith, SMWS Unfiltered, 2022). Diageo's brand site and the Aberdeenshire Historic Environment Record both date the distillery's beginnings to 1811, as an illicit still.
- **The work.** Smith: John's wife Helen "became adept at producing good quality spirit". Diageo's site calls John and Helen its founders.
- **1816.** John Cumming was convicted three times of illicit distilling; only nominal fines were paid (Smith only; see Not confirmed).
- **Where.** Cardow is on the north side of the Spey above Knockando, "in a wild and desolate district" 4½ miles from Carron station (Barnard, 1887). Cardhu is the Gaelic spelling of the place name; Cardow the anglicised one.

### Go to the source

- › _Gavin D. Smith, "For the love of Cardhu", Unfiltered (Scotch Malt Whisky Society), February 2022: the family history, with dates, quotations from letters and local newspapers. Read via the Wayback Machine._
- › _Alfred Barnard, The Whisky Distilleries of the United Kingdom (1887), the Cardow entry: the only eyewitness description of the old and new distilleries. A transcription is on Whiskipedia._

### Threads worth pulling

- John Cumming's 1816 convictions should be in the records of the local justices; Smith gives no reference.

> **TRAPS**
> Helen is now called "the first woman of whisky" on Diageo's site. That is a brand title, not a historical finding. What the record shows is a farm wife who made the whisky on a farm whose lease and licence were in her husband's name.

## Module 2. The stories told about Helen

_Which of the Helen stories have a document behind them?_

### What's established

- **The flour.** Told by Smith: warned that excise officers were coming, Helen hid the still and smeared flour on her arms, saying she had been baking, so the smell of mashing passed for bread. Michael Jackson (1994) tells a version with the smell of her baking covering the still.
- **The flag.** Smith: excise officers sometimes lodged at the farm, as there was no inn, and while they ate Helen raised a red flag on the barn to warn neighbours who were distilling. Diageo's 2021 visitor experience tells the same story and now flies a red flag beside a statue of Helen (STV News, 2021).
- **Elgin.** Smith gives this one explicitly as tradition: she walked barefoot over the hills to Elgin to deliver whisky.
- **Her age.** Smith says Helen lived to 97; other accounts say 98. John died in March 1846, aged 72 (Smith).

### Go to the source

- › _Smith (2022), as above: the fullest telling, with the hedges in place._
- › _STV News, "Distillery shines a light on the important women in its past", 17 June 2021: Diageo's version, including the flag, the 1811 date and the statue._

> **SAY IT AS A STORY**
> No contemporary document for the flour, the flag or the walk to Elgin was found. They come down through the family and the distillery, and each later telling makes them a little bigger (one has her hiding whisky in bladders under her skirts). Tell them as "the story goes".

## Module 3. The licence and Lewis Cumming

_What changed in 1824, and who ran Cardow for the next fifty years?_

### What's established

- **1823–24.** After the Excise Act of 1823, which lowered duty to draw illicit distillers into the legal trade, John Cumming took out a licence for Cardow (Smith). Barnard: "Cardow was established as a licensed Distillery in 1824, previous to which date illicit distillation was carried on both there and in other places in the neighbourhood." Some reference works give 1823.
- **Lewis.** John and Helen's eldest son Lewis was 21 when the licence was granted and went on to run the distillery, with the licence later in his name (Smith).
- **1832.** Lewis to a Portsmouth merchant: "The quality I have no doubt will please, my still being of the smallest kind. I use no other fuel but peat, which is a great thing for making good whisky." (quoted by Smith only)
- **February 1872.** Lewis Cumming dies aged 69. The Elgin Courant called him "one of the most kind and hospitable men to be found in the whole county" (quoted by Smith).

> **TRAPS**
> A story told about two women skips the man who ran the distillery longest. Lewis held it for most of half a century. Saying so makes the women's part more believable, not less.

## Module 4. Elizabeth Cumming rebuilds Cardow

_What did Elizabeth inherit in 1872, and what did she build?_

### What's established

- **1872.** Elizabeth, Lewis's widow, 24 years younger than him and the daughter of a local farmer, was pregnant with her fourth child when he died; a five-year-old daughter died three days after her father (Smith). She ran the farm and the distillery.
- **The old distillery.** Barnard: the works had been built on the farm "without any title to the buildings, beyond the currency of the lease, which periodically expired at the end of every 19 years". When he saw them "the buildings were of the most straggling and primitive description, and although water power existed, a great part of the work was done by manual labour."
- **1884–85.** "A feu had been obtained to a piece of ground in close proximity to the old work, and an entirely new Distillery had just been built on the most approved plan" (Barnard). The Aberdeenshire Historic Environment Record dates the rebuild 1884–85. Barnard gives the new distillery a capacity of 60,000 gallons a year and an expected output of about 40,000, against 25,000 at the old works.
- **The whisky.** Barnard calls the old distillery's make "of the thickest and richest description, and admirably adapted for blending purposes. Our guide told us that a single gallon of it is sufficient to cover ten gallons of plain Spirit, and that it commands a high price in the market." It was sold mainly in Leith. Barnard names only "Mrs. Cumming, the proprietress of the Distillery".
- **4 January 1886.** Approached through her brother-in-law about selling, Elizabeth wrote: "I could not possibly entertain such an idea, being that I have three sons to provide for… it would not be justice to my family." Smith says the approach was for the Distillers Company (Smith only).
- **1886.** Her eldest son Lewis died; his brother John left medical school at Aberdeen to run the distillery for her (Smith).

### Go to the source

- › _Barnard (1887), the Cardow entry, transcribed on Whiskipedia: read the whole page. Smith quotes the same passages, so two transcriptions agree._
- › _Smith (2022): the letter of 4 January 1886 and the family deaths._

### Threads worth pulling

- Several writers quote a passage, attributed to Barnard in 1893, praising "Mrs Lewis Cumming" as bookkeeper and correspondent and crediting her alone with the 1884 enlargement. The 1887 Cardow entry does not contain it. Where it was first printed was not found.

> **TRAPS**
> One web biography has Lewis Cumming's son also dying in 1872. Smith places the son's death in 1886, after the January letter. Go with Smith, who quotes the documents.

## Module 5. The old stills go to Glenfiddich

_Is it true that Glenfiddich started on Cardow's equipment?_

### What's established

- **1886–87.** Smith: the equipment from the old Cardow, "including a rather worn pair of stills", was sold to William Grant, then building Glenfiddich at Dufftown. The International Directory of Company Histories (via company-histories.com): Grant "acquired a secondhand still and other equipment from the Cardhu Distillery" for a little less than £120. The Single Cask's distillery guide gives £120, bought from Elizabeth Cumming in autumn 1886.
- **The builder.** Grant had worked twenty years at Mortlach distillery (The Single Cask) and built Glenfiddich with his sons and a stonemason (company-histories.com; The Single Cask). William Grant & Sons is still owned by his descendants, now the fifth generation (both sources).
- **Christmas Day 1887.** The first spirit ran at Glenfiddich (company-histories.com; The Single Cask).
- Several older books seen only in search fragments (Lockhart 1951, McDowall 1967, Craig 1994) give £120; Craig adds £9 for carting. Gavin Smith's 1999 book apparently cites a payment of £102 9s 1d to Mrs Cumming for distilling equipment. Those books are lending-only and were not read.

### Go to the source

- › _Smith (2022) and the company history, above._

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> The sale is well attested; the price is "about £120". Nothing found says Glenfiddich's stills today are copies of the Cardow pair, so do not say it.

## Module 6. The sale to John Walker & Sons

_What did the Cummings get in 1893, and why did Walker want Cardow?_

### What's established

- **19 September 1893.** The Cummings sell Cardow to John Walker & Sons for £20,500, exclusive of stock. John Cumming is to manage the distillery; Elizabeth sees to it that he gets 100 Walker shares, worth £5,000, and a seat on the board (Smith). STV's 2021 report, from Diageo, gives the same figures.
- **The first.** It was the first distillery John Walker & Sons owned (Diageo, quoted in Whisky Magazine, 2019; Aberdeenshire HER). Diageo's reason: "to secure the supply of its fine single malt".
- **19 May 1894.** Elizabeth Cumming dies suddenly, aged 67. A local obituary called her "a true friend of all the poor"; she had lent money without interest or security to neighbours in need (Smith).
- **John F. Cumming** retired from the Walker board in 1923 (Smith). He built The Dowans, near Aberlour, now a hotel.
- **1981.** The distillery, long called Cardow, was renamed Cardhu (Sunday Herald, 2003, reprinted by its author Ian Fraser).

> **TRAPS**
> The usual telling says "Elizabeth sold Cardhu to Walker". Smith has the family signing in 1893 with John running the distillery by then, and Elizabeth arranging his shares and seat. Saying "the Cummings sold, on Elizabeth's terms" is closer to the record.

## Module 7. What Cardhu is to Johnnie Walker

_How far does the record support calling Cardhu "Johnnie Walker's malt"?_

### What's established

- **Ownership.** Cardhu belongs to Diageo, which owns Johnnie Walker. Diageo's site: Cardhu is "a key component of Johnnie Walker blended whisky".
- **2019.** Diageo, announcing its £150 million tourism plan, called Cardhu "the first distillery to be acquired by John Walker & Sons in 1893 to secure the supply of its fine single malt, and remains a key single malt in Johnnie Walker today" (Whisky Magazine, 8 August 2019). Cardhu is one of the four distilleries Diageo presents as the "four corners" homes of Johnnie Walker, with Glenkinchie, Caol Ila and Clynelish.
- **2003.** The Sunday Herald described 12-year-old Cardhu as "a key ingredient of Johnnie Walker blended Scotch whisky" (Fraser, 2 November 2003).
- **Recipes.** Diageo does not publish what is in Black Label. Retailers and guides name Cardhu among its malts, alongside Caol Ila, Clynelish, Lagavulin and Talisker. Black Label is a blend of many malts and grain whiskies.

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> Supported: Cardhu was Walker's first distillery, bought to secure its malt, and Diageo calls it a key malt in Johnnie Walker. Not supported: that Johnnie Walker depends on Cardhu above its other malts, or any share of Cardhu in a given bottle. "Johnnie Walker's malt" means Walker's own first malt distillery; say that.

## Module 8. Cardhu Pure Malt, 2003–04

_What did Diageo do with the Cardhu name in 2003, and how did it end?_

### What's established

- **The demand.** Cardhu had become a top seller in Spain and Portugal, rising from 30,000 to 280,000 cases a year over a decade (BBC News, 4 December 2003). Diageo: "We cannot deliver any more Cardhu 12-year-old single malt than we do at present… it would be another 12 years before the additional product became available" (Jonathan Driver, quoted in the Guardian, 4 December 2003).
- **The change.** From July 2003 Diageo sold "Cardhu Pure Malt", a mix of malts from several of its Speyside distilleries, in a bottle almost identical to the single malt's, with "single" changed to "pure" (Fraser, Sunday Herald, 2 November 2003; BBC). Fraser reported Diageo was renaming the distillery Cardow; the Guardian says Diageo withdrew Cardhu from the UK to avoid confusion.
- **The protest.** William Grant & Sons, Glenfarclas and others objected; Grant's deputy managing director Tony Hunt wrote for BBC News Online (30 November 2003) that the industry was "united and outraged". The row reached Prime Minister's Questions and the Scottish Parliament.
- **4 December 2003.** At the Scotch Whisky Association council Diageo agreed to change the bottle from brown to green, explain the product to consumers, and not reformulate any other single malt this way; it said "for that we are sorry" (Guardian; BBC; FoodNavigator, 5 December 2003).
- **March 2004.** Diageo agreed to stop making Cardhu Pure Malt and to reintroduce the single malt in all its European markets (Morning Advertiser, 10 March 2004; the IPKat, 9 March 2004, reporting the BBC).

### Go to the source

- › _BBC News Online: "Whisky's reputation 'under threat'" (30 November 2003) and "Whisky branding deal reached" (4 December 2003)._
- › _Mark Tran, "Whisky industry settles on strict malt definitions", the Guardian, 4 December 2003._
- › _Ian Fraser, "Cutting up rough over Cardhu", Sunday Herald, 2 November 2003, on his own site._

> **TRAPS**
> Several summaries date the change to December 2003. The pure malt was on sale from July; December is when the packaging deal was struck. When the single malt was back on every shelf is unclear: the March 2004 announcement says Diageo would reintroduce it, and Wikipedia's "2006" rests on a dead page.

## Module 9. The law after Cardhu

_What do the rules say now?_

### What's established

- **23 November 2009.** The Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009 (SI 2009/2890) come into force. They define five categories, including "Blended Malt Scotch Whisky", "a blend of two or more Single Malt Scotch Whiskies that have been distilled at more than one distillery".
- **Regulation 9(1).** The name of a distillery in Schedule 1 "must not be used as a brand name, or as part of a brand name of a Scotch Whisky… unless the whisky has been wholly distilled at that distillery." Cardhu is in Schedule 1.
- **Regulation 11.** No Scotch may be labelled, sold or promoted with "the phrase 'pure malt' or any derivation of that phrase".
- In March 2004 the SWA was reported to be working on definitions so that distillery names could only be used on whisky made at that distillery (Morning Advertiser).

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> The regulations do not mention Cardhu's history, and the SWA's work on definitions had several causes. "Six years after the row, the law said what the protesters had asked for" is fair; "Cardhu caused the law" is not shown.

## Claims that don't hold up

- "Cardhu Pure Malt came out in December 2003." It was on sale from July 2003; December is when Diageo agreed to change the packaging.
- "The Glenfiddich stills are only a Wikipedia story." Three separate accounts give the sale, and several older books give the £120 price.
- "Elizabeth sold Cardhu by herself." By 1893 her son John was running the distillery, and the deal gave him the shares and the board seat.
- "Cardhu 12 costs about $50 in the US." Listings seen in November 2026 ran $65–75.

## Not confirmed

- **The flour, the flag and the walk to Elgin.** Family and distillery tradition. Said in the script as "the story goes".
- **John Cumming's three convictions in 1816.** Smith only. Said as "one account says" or left to the outline.
- **The 1886 approach came from the Distillers Company.** Smith only; the script says "a bigger firm".
- **The 1832 letter about peat.** Quoted by Smith only; kept off the slides.
- **The "Barnard 1893" passage praising Mrs Lewis Cumming.** Quoted in two web articles; its original was not found. Not used.
- **That Walker bought much of Cardow's output before 1893.** Found only in secondary accounts without a document. Not used.
- **That Cardhu is in Black Label.** Retailers and guides say so; Diageo publishes no recipe. Said as "the guides name Cardhu among its malts".
- **The exact price of the stills.** £120 in most accounts; a payment of £102 9s 1d appears in one book seen only in a search fragment. Said as "about £120".
- **When Cardhu single malt was back in every market.** Announced March 2004; the date it returned was not found.
- **Helen's age at death.** 97 or 98. Said as "her late nineties".
