## Part One: before the night

### The one-sentence version

A Speyside farm distillery was kept going by two women, Helen Cumming and her daughter-in-law Elizabeth, until Elizabeth's family sold it in 1893 to John Walker & Sons, whose first distillery it became, and its name was argued over again in 2003 when its owner tried to put other malts in the bottle.

### What the night is

The story runs in order, from a farm in 1811 to a row in 2003, and each glass arrives where it belongs: the distillery's own malt, the distillery that started on its old stills, the blend that bought it, and a malt whisky made from several distilleries.

Be fair to everyone in it. The Cummings were smugglers and then businesspeople. Diageo changed Cardhu in 2003 and reversed the change after protests. William Grant & Sons protested in 2003 and also owes its first stills to Cardow. All of that can be said plainly.

### How many bottles

Four, all at 40% or 43%, so the room can taste the differences without heat getting in the way.

## Part Two: the script

_SET UP: glass 1 (Cardhu 12) poured or ready to pour. The other three bottles can stay on the table in plain view; nothing tonight is blind._

## [SLIDE 1] Cardhu: The Women Behind Johnnie Walker's Malt

Good evening, and thanks for coming.

Tonight is about one distillery on Speyside, called Cardhu now and Cardow for most of its life, and the two women at the centre of its story before John Walker & Sons bought it in 1893. It was the first distillery the Walker firm ever owned.

We'll have four glasses, and each one comes in where it belongs in the story.

## [SLIDE 2] Glass 1: Cardhu 12 Year Old

_Pour glass 1 now if it is not already poured. Keep talking while you pour._

This is Cardhu 12, the distillery's own single malt. 40% ABV. It sells far more in Spain than it does here.

Diageo, which owns both Cardhu and Johnnie Walker, calls it a key malt in Johnnie Walker. Tonight we start by tasting it on its own.

_Ask: "What do you get first, and is there any smoke at all?"_

## [SLIDE 3] John and Helen Cumming leased Cardow in 1811

At Whitsunday in 1811 a farmer called John Cumming took a nineteen-year lease on Cardow, a hill farm on the north side of the Spey in the parish of Knockando. When the writer Alfred Barnard drove up there in the 1880s he called it "a wild and desolate district".

The farm made whisky, and it made it without a licence. The historian Gavin Smith, writing for the Scotch Malt Whisky Society, says John's wife Helen "became adept at producing good quality spirit". One account says John was convicted three times for illicit distilling in 1816 and paid only small fines.

The distillery today counts 1811 as its beginning and names John and Helen as its founders.

## [SLIDE 4] The stories told about Helen Cumming

Helen is the person the distillery tells stories about. None of them is in a document; they come down through the family and the distillery, so take them as stories.

The story goes that when she heard the excise officers were coming, she hid the still and rubbed flour on her arms, and told them she had been baking. The smell of the mash passed for bread.

There was no inn nearby, so the officers sometimes lodged at the farm. The story goes that while they ate the supper she had cooked, Helen went out to the barn and raised a red flag, so the neighbours who were also distilling knew to hide their stills.

And she is said to have walked barefoot over the hills to Elgin, a dozen miles or more, to deliver whisky. The distillery flies a red flag today next to a statue of her.

> **SAY THE CAVEAT OUT LOUD**
> The flour, the flag and the walk to Elgin have no contemporary document behind them. They are good stories and worth telling, as stories. Helen lived into her late nineties; accounts differ between 97 and 98.

## [SLIDE 5] Cardow was licensed in 1824

In 1823 Parliament cut the duty on whisky to bring the illicit distillers into the legal trade, and in 1824 John Cumming took out a licence for Cardow. Barnard: "Cardow was established as a licensed Distillery in 1824, previous to which date illicit distillation was carried on both there and in other places in the neighbourhood."

Their eldest son, Lewis, was 21 that year, and he went on to run the distillery for most of the next fifty years. It is worth saying his name, because a story about two women can skip the man who ran the place longest. Lewis died in February 1872, aged 69.

## [SLIDE 6] Elizabeth Cumming took over in 1872

Lewis had married late. His widow, Elizabeth, was 24 years younger than him and the daughter of a local farmer. When he died she was pregnant with her fourth child, and their five-year-old daughter died three days after her father.

Elizabeth took on the farm and the distillery. Blended Scotch was growing fast through the 1870s and 1880s, and blenders wanted malt. Cardow's buildings were old, and the farm's lease ran out every nineteen years.

## [SLIDE 7] Elizabeth built a new distillery in 1884

The old distillery had been put up on a leased farm, with no right to the buildings beyond the lease. Barnard saw it in the mid-1880s and wrote that "the buildings were of the most straggling and primitive description, and although water power existed, a great part of the work was done by manual labour."

Elizabeth got ground of her own next to the old works and built a new distillery in 1884 and 1885. Barnard: "an entirely new Distillery had just been built on the most approved plan, and with all the latest improvements and appliances." He put its capacity at 60,000 gallons a year, against 25,000 at the old works. He names her only as "Mrs. Cumming, the proprietress".

He also wrote down what the blenders thought of the whisky. It was "of the thickest and richest description, and admirably adapted for blending purposes," and he was told that "a single gallon of it is sufficient to cover ten gallons of plain Spirit." A little of it flavoured a lot of grain whisky, which is what blenders were buying.

## [SLIDE 8] The old stills went to Glenfiddich

That left an old distillery's worth of equipment. In 1886 William Grant, who had worked for twenty years at Mortlach distillery, was building his own distillery a few miles away at Dufftown, with his sons and a stonemason. He bought the old Cardow plant, a worn pair of stills included, for about £120. His distillery was Glenfiddich, and its first spirit ran on Christmas Day 1887.

The same year, a bigger firm asked through Elizabeth's brother-in-law whether she would sell Cardow. Her reply survives, dated 4 January 1886: "I could not possibly entertain such an idea, being that I have three sons to provide for… it would not be justice to my family."

Later in 1886 her eldest son died, and her son John left medical school in Aberdeen to run the distillery for her.

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> The sale of the old plant is in several accounts; the price is "about £120". Nobody says today's Glenfiddich stills are copies of the Cardow pair, so don't claim it.

## [SLIDE 9] Glass 2: Glenfiddich 12 Year Old

_Pour glass 2._

Glenfiddich 12, from William Grant's distillery at Dufftown. 40% ABV. The firm is still owned by Grant's descendants. Its first stills were the ones Elizabeth Cumming sold.

_Ask: "Put this next to the Cardhu. Two Speyside malts the same age: where do they part company?"_

## [SLIDE 10] In 1893 the Cummings sold Cardow to John Walker & Sons

In 1893 the family did sell. On 19 September they signed with John Walker & Sons, the Kilmarnock blenders, for £20,500, not counting the whisky in stock.

The terms were Elizabeth's. Her son John would go on managing the distillery, and she saw to it that he received 100 shares in John Walker & Sons, worth £5,000, and a seat on its board. He stayed on that board until 1923.

Cardow was the first distillery Walker's owned. Diageo says they bought it to secure the supply of its malt.

Elizabeth died suddenly the next May, aged 67. Her local paper called her "a true friend of all the poor": she had lent neighbours money without interest or security.

## [SLIDE 11] Diageo calls Cardhu a key malt in Johnnie Walker

Diageo owns both Cardhu and Johnnie Walker today. In 2019 Diageo called Cardhu "a key single malt in Johnnie Walker today" and made it one of four distilleries it presents as homes of the brand, with Glenkinchie, Caol Ila and Clynelish.

Diageo does not publish what is in each blend. The guides name Cardhu among the malts in Black Label, alongside Caol Ila, Clynelish, Lagavulin and Talisker. Black Label holds a lot of malts and a lot of grain whisky, so think of Cardhu as one important part of it.

## [SLIDE 12] Glass 3: Johnnie Walker Black Label

_Pour glass 3._

Johnnie Walker Black Label, 12 years old, 40%. A blend of malt and grain whiskies.

_Ask: "Go back to glass 1. Can you find the Cardhu in this, or does the blend cover it?"_

## [SLIDE 13] In 2003 Diageo sold Cardhu Pure Malt

Over the 1990s Cardhu became a big seller in Spain and Portugal, from 30,000 cases a year to 280,000. Diageo said it could not make more twelve-year-old Cardhu than it already did, and that new stock would take twelve years to come of age.

So from the summer of 2003 it sold "Cardhu Pure Malt": Cardhu mixed with malts from other Speyside distilleries, in a bottle that looked almost the same, with the word "single" changed to "pure".

The rest of the industry objected loudly. William Grant & Sons, Glenfiddich's owners, were among the loudest; their deputy managing director wrote for BBC News that the industry was "united and outraged". It came up at Prime Minister's Questions and in the Scottish Parliament.

On 4 December 2003, at the Scotch Whisky Association, Diageo agreed to change the bottle from brown to green and not to do the same to any other single malt, and said "for that we are sorry". In March 2004 it dropped the pure malt and said it would bring back the single malt.

> **GET THE DATES RIGHT**
> Many summaries say the pure malt came out in December 2003. It was on sale from July; December is when the packaging deal was made.

## [SLIDE 14] Since 2009 the law bans "pure malt"

The Scotch Whisky Regulations came into force on 23 November 2009. A whisky made from single malts from more than one distillery is now called a blended malt. Nobody may sell Scotch as "pure malt". And the name of a listed distillery, Cardhu among them, may not be used as a brand name unless the whisky was wholly distilled there.

The regulations don't mention 2003. They do settle each point the protesters raised.

## [SLIDE 15] Glass 4: Johnnie Walker Green Label

_Pour glass 4._

Johnnie Walker Green Label, 15 years old, 43%. It is a blended malt: malts from several distilleries and no grain whisky, the same kind of whisky Cardhu Pure Malt was. Green Label came out in 1997 as Johnnie Walker Pure Malt and took its present name in 2004. The malts usually named for it are Talisker, Linkwood, Cragganmore and Caol Ila; Cardhu is not on that list.

_Ask: "Now taste glass 1 again. If both had the same name on the label, would you have noticed?"_
