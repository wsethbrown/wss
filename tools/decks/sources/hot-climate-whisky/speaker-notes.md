## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] Hot Climate Whisky

Thanks for coming. Tonight is about three distilleries that make single malt in hot places. Amrut is in Bangalore, in India. Paul John is in Goa, also in India. Kavalan is in the north-east of Taiwan. We'll take them in the order they started selling single malt, and taste each one as we get to it.

Whisky ages differently in the heat, and all three distilleries had to deal with that. None of their standard bottles has an age on the label. I'll explain why as we go. The last pour is a 12-year-old Scotch, so you can compare.

## [SLIDE 2] Amrut was founded in Bangalore in 1948.

Amrut was founded in Bangalore in 1948, the year after India became independent. The founder was J. N. Radhakrishna Rao Jagdale. For about fifty years Amrut made what nearly every other Indian distiller made, which was the everyday spirits most Indians buy.

India drinks more whisky than any other country. Some of the best-selling whisky brands in the world are Indian, and you will probably never see them in a shop here. Most of that whisky is not made the way Scotch is.

## [SLIDE 3] What India calls whisky

Most whisky sold in India starts with molasses. Molasses is the thick syrup left over when sugar is made from sugar cane. India grows a lot of cane, so molasses is cheap.

The usual Indian whisky is spirit made from molasses, with some malt whisky mixed in for flavour, and colouring. The Indian trade has an odd name for this kind of drink, left over from colonial times. It is called Indian-made foreign liquor.

Under Indian law this is whisky. A blended whisky there can be mostly molasses spirit, with as little as 2 percent malt or grain whisky. Europe has a different rule. In the European Union whisky has to be made from grain, and it has to spend at least three years in wooden casks. So most Indian whisky cannot be sold in Europe as whisky.

Single malt is different. It is made only from malted barley, at one distillery. It counts as whisky in India and in Europe.

## [SLIDE 4] 2004: Amrut launches its single malt in Glasgow

Amrut had been making some malt whisky from Indian barley to mix into its everyday brands. By then the founder's son, Neelakanta Rao Jagdale, ran the company. He had joined in 1972 and took over when his father died in 1976. In the early 2000s the family decided to bottle some of the malt on its own, as a single malt.

They launched it on the 24th of August 2004, at an Indian restaurant in Glasgow.

The company says it launched in Scotland because it could not sell the whisky at home. An Indian single malt would cost several times as much as ordinary Indian whisky, and Indians who could afford that bought Scotch. Amrut thought Indians would only take an Indian malt seriously once whisky drinkers abroad had.

## [SLIDE 5] Selling an Indian malt in Britain

The founder's grandson, Rakshit Jagdale, led the launch. In the 1990s he was a business student in Newcastle, and he took a sample of Amrut's malt to the owner of a Glasgow whisky bar called the Pot Still.

The company's story is that in the early years its people carried bottles round restaurants and whisky shops one at a time and asked people to taste it. A lot of them were Indian restaurants at first. Sales were slow for several years.

Then came a review. The critic Jim Murray publishes a guide called the *Whisky Bible*. In the 2010 edition he gave Amrut Fusion 97 points and ranked it third in the world. That is one critic's opinion, but it was reported widely, and shops started asking for the bottle. It's the one we're about to taste.

## [SLIDE 6] Pour 1 of 4: Amrut Fusion

This is Amrut Fusion. It is called Fusion because it is made from two kinds of barley. About three-quarters is Indian barley with no peat. About a quarter is peated barley from Scotland. It is bottled at 50 percent, so it is the strongest of tonight's four.

Try it neat first, then add a few drops of water. The smoke may be faint, and you'll probably taste a lot of oak. The next slide explains why.

There is no age on the label. Amrut does not say how old Fusion is.

## [SLIDE 7] What a year in the warehouse costs

When Amrut started keeping malt in casks for longer, it found out how much the heat matters.

In Scotland a cask loses about 2 percent of its whisky each year to evaporation. Distillers call that the angels' share. In Bangalore Amrut loses a lot more. The family has given figures between 9 and 12 percent a year. Bangalore is on a plateau, so it is milder than most of India. Rakshit Jagdale has said that on the coast and in the north the loss can be 13 to 15 percent.

Heat also makes the whisky take flavour from the wood faster. Amrut's master blender, Surinder Kumar, says a year in Bangalore does about what three years do in Scotland. That is the company's own estimate. There is no agreed way to measure it.

## [SLIDE 8] What is left in the cask

This chart shows how much is left in a cask over time. It uses 2 percent a year for Scotland and 10 percent a year for a hot warehouse. Ten percent is in the range that Amrut and Paul John give for themselves. Kavalan's American importer gives at least 15 percent for its hottest warehouse floors. The chart assumes the loss is the same every year. In a real warehouse it varies.

After four years the Scottish cask still has about 92 percent of what went in. The hot cask has about two-thirds.

After twelve years the Scottish cask has about 78 percent. The hot cask has just over a quarter. And the heat has been pulling flavour out of the wood that whole time.

So a distillery in India or Taiwan that wanted a 12-year-old would have to fill nearly three casks to end up with what one cask gives in Scotland. It would have to store them for twelve years, and by then the whisky might taste of little but oak. That costs too much, so almost nobody does it.

## [SLIDE 9] Paul John: malt from Goa, first sold in 2012

The second distillery is Paul John. It belongs to John Distilleries, a Bangalore company owned by Paul P. John. Like Amrut, it started by making whisky for the Indian mass market. Its best-known brand is called Original Choice.

The company started making malt whisky in Goa in 2008. Its first single malt went on sale in London on the 4th of October 2012, before it was sold in India. So Paul John did what Amrut had done and went abroad first.

The distillery is at Cuncolim in south Goa, a few miles from the coast. It uses copper pot stills. Goa is at sea level, and it is hot and very humid all year. Bangalore's heat is drier. The company says each cask loses 8 to 10 percent a year.

## [SLIDE 10] Pour 2 of 4: Paul John Brilliance

This is Paul John Brilliance, at 46 percent. It has no peat. It is made from Indian six-row barley, which is the kind most Indian farmers grow, and aged in casks that held bourbon.

Taste it next to the Amrut. There is no smoke in this one, so see if the cask is easier to taste.

There is no age on this label either. Have a look at what the label does tell you.

## [SLIDE 11] Kavalan: private distilling in Taiwan from 2002

The third distillery is Kavalan, in Taiwan.

Until 2002 the government had a monopoly on alcohol in Taiwan. That year Taiwan joined the World Trade Organization, the monopoly ended, and private companies were allowed to distil.

One of them was King Car, a food and drinks group that Lee Tien-tsai founded in 1979. In Taiwan it is best known for canned coffee. In 2005 it built a malt distillery in Yilan County, on the north-east coast. The first spirit ran on the 11th of March 2006.

The whisky is called Kavalan. That is the old name of the Yilan area and of the indigenous people who lived there. King Car hired a Scottish consultant, Jim Swan, to advise on casks. Yilan is subtropical and humid, and the warehouses are hot for most of the year.

## [SLIDE 12] January 2010: Kavalan wins a blind tasting in Leith

Kavalan's first bottles went on sale in December 2008. That whisky is now called Kavalan Classic.

In January 2010 *The Times* asked the whisky writer Charles MacLean to set up a blind tasting for Burns Night. The paper wanted to see how a new English malt called St George's compared with Scotch. Nothing in the tasting could be older than the St George's, so every whisky was very young. It was held in Leith, the port of Edinburgh, with four judges.

One of the whiskies was a Kavalan. It was reported to be two years old, and it was not on sale in Britain at the time. It scored 27.5 out of 40 and came first. Two three-year-old Scotch blends came next, then St George's. The young Scotch malts came last.

It was one small tasting set up for a newspaper story, but the result was reported around the world. Kavalan has won bigger competitions since. In 2015 its Solist Vinho Barrique was named World's Best Single Malt at the World Whiskies Awards. That one is bottled from a single cask that had held wine.

## [SLIDE 13] Pour 3 of 4: Kavalan Classic

This is Kavalan Classic, the distillery's first single malt. The American bottle is 43 percent. In Britain it is 40 percent, so check your label. It will seem light after the Amrut.

It has no age on the label. Look at the colour, and taste how sweet it is from the wood. The distillery only started in 2006.

## [SLIDE 14] Why these labels carry no age

None of the first three bottles tells you how old it is. There are three reasons.

The first is cost. You saw the numbers on slide 8. Old whisky is very expensive to make in a hot warehouse.

The second is that people would compare the number with Scotch. In Scotland a four-year-old is very young. A drinker who saw "four years" on one of these bottles would expect something raw.

The third is the distilleries' own argument, which is that years are the wrong way to measure it. Kavalan's master blender, Ian Chang, has said a year in a cask in Taiwan does what four or five years do in a cooler place. That may be right. It also suits a distillery that has no old whisky to sell.

When a label does give an age, it has to be the age of the youngest whisky in the bottle. That is the law in Scotland and in the European Union.

## [SLIDE 15] Pour 4 of 4: Glenfiddich 12 Year Old

Last pour. This is Glenfiddich 12, from Dufftown in Speyside, in Scotland. It is made by William Grant and Sons and bottled at 40 percent. It is here so you have a Scotch with an age on the label to compare with the others.

All the whisky in it spent at least twelve years in a Scottish warehouse, losing about 2 percent a year. Taste it next to the Kavalan, which is close in strength. Then try it next to the other two.

If you hadn't seen the labels, would you have picked this one as the 12-year-old? Then go back to the one you liked best. I'll ask for a show of hands.

## [SLIDE 16] Reading a world whisky label

One more slide. This is what to look for when you see one of these bottles in a shop.

"Single malt" means the whisky is made from malted barley at one distillery. That is a legal definition in Scotland and the EU. Amrut, Paul John and Kavalan all follow it.

"Whisky", in the European Union, means it is made from grain and aged at least three years in wood. An Indian bottle that does not say "malt" or "grain" may be molasses spirit. That is legal in India, but it is not whisky in Europe.

If there is an age on the label, it is the age of the youngest whisky in the bottle. If there is no age on a malt from a hot country, that is normal, and it tells you very little.

The strength is on every label, so you can always compare that.

Thanks for coming, and thanks to whoever brought the bottles. Sláinte.
