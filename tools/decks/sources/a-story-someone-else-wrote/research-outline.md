# A Story Someone Else Wrote

Research outline: Value vs. Prestige: how to build and run this session with your own bottles

_This outline is not a shopping list. The bottles are interchangeable, what matters is the shape of the lineup and the way the evening is run. Pick three to five you can actually get, that you're curious about, or that you already own. The argument works with almost any grouping of bottles, provided each one is doing a job. What follows is the structure, so you can build your own version and run it in your own voice. Facts about the deck's six bottles were checked in November 2026; prices, proofs of batch-dated bottles and availability move quickly, so re-check them against the bottle in your hand._

> **THE ARGUMENT**
> One sentence: price is a story someone else wrote, and a blind tasting is the only way to find out how much of it you actually believe.
> That's it. Everything in the session serves that. The reveal is not a gotcha, it's a mirror. Some nights the expensive bottle wins on the cards, and that is a real result, not a failed session. The point is that the room finds out something true about its own palate. Do not build an evening that can only conclude one thing.

> **THE FAILURE MODE TO AVOID**
> The cheap-bottle-wins version of this night is smug, and the room can smell it. If you've secretly decided the outcome before you pour, you'll telegraph it, and the tasting becomes a performance of a conclusion instead of a test of one.
> Run it so you genuinely don't know how it will land.

## Building the lineup

Three to five pours is right. Two is thin and six is palate fatigue and nobody's notes are worth anything by the end. Each bottle should be doing a job. Cast the roles first, then find bottles to fill them.

### The roles

- **The Opener.** Lowest proof, approachable, widely available. It goes first because it's gentle, it needs a fair hearing before anything strong burns the room's palate down. Its job is to establish a baseline.
- **The Value.** Cheap, and unashamed of it. Ideally it has something objectively going for it, high proof, an age statement, a legal designation, so the reveal lands with a fact rather than a shrug. This is the bottle the night is quietly built around.
- **The Prestige.** The hyped one. Allocated, secondary-market, or simply expensive. It must be a genuinely good whiskey, if you pick a bad expensive bottle you've rigged the game and proved nothing.
- **The Craft.** Something where the money demonstrably buys something: a deliberate blend, an unusual process, non-chill-filtered, a real age statement. This is the bottle that keeps the night honest, because sometimes the price is the product.
- **The Ringer.** Something that isn't what the room expects, a rye among bourbons, a finished whiskey, a different country. Its job is to catch people scoring familiarity rather than quality.
- **The Closer.** Highest proof, barrel strength. It goes last because its heat lingers longest.

### The pairing that makes the night

Put two bottles from the same distillery in the lineup at very different prices. This is the strongest single move available to you, because it controls for everything, same water, same warehouses, same house style, and isolates the variable you care about.

It gives you a mid-session reveal that costs nothing and lands hard: two of these came from the same place. One is forty dollars. One is a hundred and twenty. Go look at what you wrote.

> **PICK YOUR OWN**
> Plenty of houses give you this pairing. Any distillery with a flagship and a halo bottle will do. You're looking for same producer, different price tier, both good.

## Ordering the pours

Ascend in proof. Almost always.

Palate fatigue is real and it is not symmetric, a 120-proof pour will flatten your ability to taste a 90-proof pour, but not the reverse. So climb.

Breaking the ladder has a cost, and you should pay it knowingly:

- If a pour sits below the one before it, the room will read it as thin, and that is your fault, not the whiskey's.
- If you must break the order, because a bottle's story wants a particular slot, say so out loud at the time. "This one is gentler than the last. Give your palate a beat."

### Where to put the ringer

Middle-to-late, but not last. It needs a room that's warmed up and paying attention, but still has palate left to notice that something is genuinely different.

## Running it blind

- **Cards out before the first pour.** Not after. If the cards arrive mid-session people back-fill from memory, and memory is where the price tag sneaks back in.
- **No bottles on the table.** Not covered, not in bags, gone. A bag has a shape.
- **Pour in order, one at a time.** Do not hand them all out up front; people will taste ahead, compare, and anchor.
- **Scores written before discussion.** This is the whole methodology. The first confident voice in the room moves everyone else's card if you let it.
- **Say "heat is not flavor" early, and mean it.** Otherwise the barrel-proof pours win on impact alone and the night tells you nothing.
- **Water at the barrel-proof pour.** Have everyone add a few drops and taste again. It reframes proof as a variable rather than a virtue.

### The tally

Average the room's score per sample on a whiteboard, live, before any name is spoken. Then reveal names and prices one at a time against the standing numbers.

The order of reveal is dramaturgy: go value first, prestige last. You want the room looking at a number they already committed to when the price finally appears next to it.

## The traps

- **Don't rig it.** A bad expensive bottle proves nothing. Every bottle on the table should be one you'd happily drink.
- **Don't tell them what they found.** Read the averages. Let the room draw the conclusion. If you narrate the moral, you've taken the evening away from them.
- **Beware "unfamiliar therefore worse."** People routinely mark down the ringer for not tasting like its neighbours. That instinct is worth naming out loud at the reveal, it is the same instinct prestige pricing feeds on.
- **Beware the loudest palate.** One confident person can move a room. Written-first scores are the defense; enforce them.
- **Prices move.** A bottle that was thirty dollars three years ago may be allocated now. Check the current shelf price before you print anything, and say which price you're using, shelf, secondary, or what you actually paid. They are three different numbers and the difference is itself part of the argument.

## Module 1. The fact worth knowing: bottled in bond

_What does "bottled in bond" actually guarantee, and what doesn't it?_

If your value bottle happens to be bottled-in-bond, you have been handed the best line of the night for free.

### What's established

- **3 March 1897.** The Bottled-in-Bond Act is signed into law. It grew out of a market full of adulterated whiskey, and made the federal government the guarantor of a spirit's authenticity in return for a tax incentive. It took effect in about 1901.
- **1906.** The Pure Food and Drug Act follows, nine years later, and bans adulterated and misbranded food and drugs in interstate commerce.
- **Today (27 CFR 5.88).** To be labelled bottled in bond, a domestic spirit must be distilled in one distilling season by one distiller at one distillery; stored at least four years in wooden containers; reduced only with pure water; bottled at exactly 50% alcohol (100 proof); and the label must carry the real name or trade name of the distillery and the number of the plant where it was distilled (and where bottled).

### Go to the source

- › _Code of Federal Regulations, 27 CFR 5.88 "Bottled in bond", via Cornell LII or eCFR: the current rule, word for word._
- › _Distillery Trail, "What is the Bottled-in-Bond Act of 1897, Passed March 3, 1897": the date and the background._
- › _Cato Institute, Regulation, Fall 2023, "Straight whiskey, bad regulation": a sceptical reading, that straight-whiskey distillers used the law against their rivals, the rectifiers. Worth reading so the story isn't told only one way._

### Threads worth pulling

- Who pushed the Act through. Several whiskey writers credit E. H. Taylor Jr. working with Treasury Secretary John G. Carlisle (Bourbon Veach, "The fight for Bottled in Bond", 10 August 2020, is the fullest account); this outline did not open those pages, so the names stay off the slides.
- Coppin and High, The Politics of Purity (1999), is the academic history of the whiskey and pure-food fights; it is cited by the Cato piece and is the place to go further.

> **BUT DON'T OVERCLAIM IT**
> Bonded means authentic, not good. It's a floor, not a ceiling, four years and 100 proof, nothing more. There is mediocre bonded whiskey. The guarantee is about honesty of production, not quality of outcome.
> Two smaller cautions. "Among the first consumer-protection laws" is how the Act is usually described, but no independent history ranks it; keep "among". And today's rule says "stored at least four years in wooden containers" in a bonded warehouse; the on-site federal storekeepers of the 1897 system are long gone, so "federally supervised" describes the Act's origin better than a modern warehouse.

## Module 2. The six bottles in the deck

_What is actually in each glass, and what can be said about it without overclaiming?_

Checked November 2026. "Shelf" prices are North Carolina's state retail prices from its August 2026 quarterly price list (a useful national benchmark, since every store in the state charges the same), and retailer listings where NC does not carry the bottle. The prices on the reveal slide (A $40, B $120, C $18, D $60, E $75, F $80) are the deck's own round figures: they sit within the ranges below, with Blanton's at its street price rather than its list price.

### What's established

- **A. Buffalo Trace.** Buffalo Trace Distillery, Frankfort, Kentucky. 90 proof, no age statement, the distillery's low-rye Mash Bill #1 (the distillery does not publish the recipe; writers estimate under 10% rye). NC shelf $32.95. The distillery calls itself "the world's most award-winning distillery"; it has been named Distillery of the Year in competition several times, but award counts quoted vary from 500 to over 1,000.
- **B. Blanton's Original Single Barrel.** Buffalo Trace Distillery, Mash Bill #2 (higher rye), 93 proof. Introduced in 1984 by Elmer T. Lee as the first modern bourbon marketed as single barrel; barrels come from Warehouse H, the distillery's metal-clad rickhouse. Eight stoppers, each a horse and jockey in a different pose, with letters spelling B-L-A-N-T-O-N-S. Launched at about $24 (VinePair). NC shelf $82.95; well over $100 is common in practice.
- **C. Evan Williams Bottled-in-Bond (white label).** Heaven Hill, 100 proof, at least four years old by law. NC shelf $21.95; Total Wine lists about $18. Heaven Hill is based in Bardstown, where it ages and bottles, but since 1999 it has distilled at the Bernheim distillery in Louisville; the label's DSP-KY-1 is the distilling plant.
- **D. Four Roses Small Batch Select.** Four Roses Distillery, Lawrenceburg, Kentucky. 104 proof, non-chill-filtered, six of the distillery's ten recipes (OBSV, OBSK, OBSF, OESV, OESK, OESF), aged six to seven years. The ten recipes are two mash bills (B, about 60% corn, 35% rye; E, about 75% corn, 20% rye) crossed with five yeast strains (V, K, O, Q, F). NC shelf $54.95.
- **E. Barrell Seagrass.** Barrell Craft Spirits, Louisville, which does not distill. A blend of sourced American and Canadian rye, each component finished separately in Martinique rum (rhum agricole), Madeira and apricot brandy casks. Released March 2021. 118.4 proof (59.2%) on the standard bottling. MSRP $90 at launch (Breaking Bourbon); currently around $75–90.
- **F. Elijah Craig Barrel Proof.** Heaven Hill, batch-dated, uncut and non-chill-filtered. Batch A125 (January 2025) was 118.2 proof and 10 years 7 months old, SRP $74.99. Batches have ranged from 118.2 to 140.2 proof. NC currently lists a 124.2-proof batch at $74.95.

> **TRAPS**
> Batch-dated bottles change. If your Elijah Craig is a different batch, its proof and age are on its label; read those, not the deck's.
> Barrell bottlings vary. Check the proof on your Seagrass before you print the reveal table.
> Mash bill percentages for Buffalo Trace and Blanton's are writers' estimates; the distillery does not publish them.

## The close

Land it without gloating. The finding is not expensive whiskey is a scam. The finding is:

Sometimes the money buys real age, real proof, real craft. Sometimes it buys a pretty cap and a good story. Both are fine to enjoy, as long as you know which one you're paying for.

Then the toast.

## Claims that don't hold up

- **"Four Roses runs five mash bills crossed with two yeasts."** It is the other way round: two mash bills and five yeasts, ten recipes.
- **"Barrell Seagrass is 120.38 proof."** The standard bottling is 118.4 proof (59.2%). A different figure may come from a particular bottling; read the label.
- **"Buffalo Trace launched Blanton's."** The distillery that launched it in 1984 was not yet called Buffalo Trace (it took that name in 1999). "From Buffalo Trace's Warehouse H" is fine as shorthand for the same site.

## The short shelf

- › _27 CFR 5.88, "Bottled in bond": the whole rule in a page._
- › _Distillery Trail, "What is the Bottled-in-Bond Act of 1897": the Act's date and full text._
- › _Four Roses, "Our recipes": the ten recipes, and why a blend of six is a deliberate choice._
- › _VinePair, "Buffalo Trace mash bill guide": the two recipes behind samples A and B._
- › _The North Carolina ABC price list: a fixed public price for nearly every bottle in Part One of the notes._

## Not confirmed

- **Buffalo Trace's age, "generally reckoned to be eight to ten years".** There is no age statement and no official figure; the slide says "generally reckoned", which is how it should be said.
- **"One of the most awarded distilleries in the world."** The distillery's own claim, tied to real competition wins; no independent ranking found. The slide's "one of the most" is the safe form.
- **"Among the first consumer-protection laws."** Widely repeated, mostly by whiskey writers and brands; no academic ranking found. Kept as "among".
- **Where Barrell's rye is distilled.** Reviews name Indiana, Tennessee, Kentucky and Canada in different combinations; Barrell says American and Canadian. The slide says "the U.S. and Canada".
- **The tasting notes on the sample slides.** These are the deck author's notes, not a published source. They describe a bottle; the room's own notes outrank them.
