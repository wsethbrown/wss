# A Story Someone Else Wrote

Speaker's notes

The Whiskey Share Society

_READ THIS FIRST. The bottles in the deck are an example, not a prescription. Swap in whatever you can actually get. The argument works with almost any lineup, provided each bottle is doing a job._

## Part One: before the night

### The one-sentence version

Price is a story someone else wrote, and a blind tasting is the only way to find out how much of it you actually believe.

### Pick three to five bottles

Past five, palate fatigue does your thinking for you. By the sixth pour people are scoring heat and nothing else, their notes go vague, and the scores stop meaning anything, which quietly destroys the entire premise of the evening. The whole session rests on those numbers being honest. Protect them.

If you have a lineup you love and it runs to six, cut one. You will not miss it.

### Cast the roles first, then find the bottles

Every bottle on the table should be doing a job. Decide the jobs, then go shopping.

With three bottles, you want:

- **The Hyped One.** Expensive, allocated, or chased. It must be genuinely good. If you pick a bad expensive bottle you have rigged the game!
- **The Sleeper.** Cheap, and better than it has any business being. In our experience, if it has a fact attached (high proof, an age statement, a legal designation) it lands better.
- **The Workhorse.** The one that is solid every single time. Nobody's grail, nobody's joke. It is the control, and it is the bottle that often wins out.

Add a fourth: **The Ringer.** Something that isn't what people expect. A rye among bourbons, a finished whiskey, something from another country. Its job is to catch people scoring familiarity instead of quality.

Add a fifth: **The Closer.** Barrel proof, cask strength. Goes last, because its heat lingers longest.

> **THE STRONGEST MOVE AVAILABLE TO YOU**
> If you can, put two bottles from the same distillery in the lineup at wildly different prices.
> It controls for everything (same water, same warehouses, same house style) and isolates the only variable you care about. Any distillery with a flagship and a halo bottle gives you this.
> It buys you a nice reveal that lands hard: two of these came from the same place. One is forty dollars. One is a hundred and twenty. Go look at what you wrote.

### Bottle ideas

Suggestions, not instructions. Prices are rough, they move, and allocation varies wildly by state. Buy what you can actually find. The prices below were checked in November 2026 against North Carolina's state price list for August 2026 and a few retailer listings.

**The Hyped Ones**

Chased, allocated, or simply expensive. Pick one. All of these are good whiskey, which is the point: the question is not whether they are good, it is whether they are that much better.

- **Blanton's Original.** The horse caps, the collector set, the secondary market. Around $65–85 on a shelf you will never see it on, and two to three times that in practice.
- **Weller Special Reserve or Weller Antique 107.** Wheated, Buffalo Trace, permanently unfindable, and priced accordingly.
- **Eagle Rare 10.** A genuinely lovely bourbon with a genuinely absurd markup.
- **E. H. Taylor Small Batch.** Bottled-in-bond, well regarded, hard to get.
- **Michter's Small Batch.** Widely available, widely argued about, priced well above its neighbours.
- **Angel's Envy.** Port-finished, beautiful bottle, a lot of people's first "nice" bourbon.
- **Basil Hayden.** Low proof, high price, a lot of shelf presence. A very interesting thing to put in a blind lineup.
- **WhistlePig 10.** If you want the hyped bottle to also be your ringer, a rye does both jobs.

**The Ones That Punch Above Their Weight**

The heart of the evening. Pick one, maybe two.

- **Evan Williams Bottled-in-Bond.** Around $18–22. 100 proof. Comes with the best story on the table (see slide 6). If you take one recommendation from this list, take this one.
- **Wild Turkey 101.** Around $25. A bar workhorse that beats bottles at four times the price with dispiriting (get it?) regularity.
- **Old Grand-Dad Bonded.** Around $25. 100 proof, high rye, an absolute bruiser for the money.
- **Rittenhouse Rye Bottled-in-Bond.** Around $28. If you want the value bottle to be your ringer too, this is how.
- **Very Old Barton 100.** Around $16 where you can get it, which is not everywhere. Almost insultingly good value.
- **Old Forester 100.** Around $30. Reliable, characterful, cheap.
- **Four Roses Bourbon (the yellow label).** Around $22 and far better than its price signals.
- **Heaven Hill Bottled-in-Bond 7 Year.** An age statement and 100 proof, for not much money.
- **Wilderness Trail Bottled in Bond.** A craft distillery actually doing the work.

**The Rock-Solid Ones**

Never disappointing, never anybody's grail. One of these belongs in every lineup, and one of them will surprise you by winning.

- **Buffalo Trace.** The definitional easy-to-like bourbon, when you can find it near shelf price.
- **Woodford Reserve.** Triple pot-distilled, dependable, a fixture for a reason.
- **Maker's Mark.** Wheated, soft, and much better in a blind lineup than its ubiquity suggests.
- **Knob Creek 9 Year.** An age statement, 100 proof, genuinely underrated.
- **Elijah Craig Small Batch.** Around $35 and consistently excellent.
- **Four Roses Small Batch or Small Batch Select.** A deliberately constructed blend. Good for showing that money can buy real craft.
- **Russell's Reserve 10.** Quietly one of the best value age-stated bourbons on any shelf.

**The Ringers**

Not what the room expects. Pick at most one.

- **A rye.** Rittenhouse, Pikesville, Sazerac Rye.
- **A finished whiskey.** Barrell Seagrass (triple finished), Angel's Envy (port), anything sherry- or rum-finished.
- **A wheater among high-rye bourbons.** Larceny, Maker's, Weller.
- **Something not American at all.** Redbreast 12 (Irish single pot still), a Japanese whisky, a Speyside single malt. This is the sneakiest version of the ringer (and the most fun).
- **A local craft distillery.** Especially satisfying if the room has been sniffy about it.

**The Closers**

Barrel proof. Goes last, always.

- **Elijah Craig Barrel Proof.** Batch-varying, usually 118 to 130 proof, ten-plus years.
- **Wild Turkey Rare Breed.** Around $50 and one of the great bargains in barrel-proof whiskey.
- **Booker's.** Uncut, unfiltered, expensive, and divisive, which makes it excellent blind.

> **HOW TO ORDER THEM**
> Ascend in proof. Palate fatigue is not symmetric: a 120-proof pour will flatten your ability to taste a 90-proof pour, but not the reverse.
> We recommend putting the ringer middle-to-late, never last. It needs a room that is warmed up but still has palate left.

> **MATCH THE DECK TO YOUR LINEUP**
> The deck ships with six sample slides on purpose. It is built long so you can cut it down, rather than short so you have to build it up. Delete any slides you do not want. Nothing else in the deck depends on there being six.
> Each sample is one slide (slides 4 to 9), and its letter, name, notes and discussion line all sit on that slide. Delete a sample's slide and its section in this script together.
> Then relabel the remaining samples A, B, C, and so on, and fix the letters in three places: the "One Thing To Notice" slide, the reveal table, and the discussion line on B's slide, which names sample A.
> Then ensure every letter in your lineup matches the letters in the deck and on the scorecard.

> **RUNNING ORDER: ABOUT AN HOUR**
> 0–5 min: welcome, the premise, tips on tasting, slides 1–3.
> 5–35 min: the blind pours, all on slide 3. Score, then talk, then the next pour.
> 35–50 min: the reveals, one slide per sample, slides 4–9.
> 50–60 min: the same-distillery pair, the tally, the point and the toast, slides 10–13.

## Part Two: the script

_STRUCTURE: slides 1 to 3, then the whole tasting happens off-slide. Every pour is scored blind. Only then do the sample slides go up, revealing what was in each glass._

_SET UP: scorecards out before the first pour. No bottles anywhere in the room, pour in another room if you can. Use a helper or mark everyone's glasses with tape and their names so as to not mix them up while they're carried out of the room._

## [SLIDE 1] A Story Someone Else Wrote

Good evening.

Tonight is fully blind. No labels, no prices, no bottles in the room. Nothing but what is in the glass.

Here is the argument: your palate already knows more than the price tag does. You just never get to prove it, because you almost never taste anything without being told first what it costs.

Tonight you do.

> **THE ONE RULE**
> Write your score before you talk. The first number is the honest one. Everything after it is negotiated with the room.

## [SLIDE 2] The premise

A bottle's price is a story someone else wrote.

Some of that story is real. Age costs money, because the barrel sits there for a decade and the angels take their cut. Proof costs money. Barrel selection costs money. That is craft, and it is worth paying for.

And some of it is theater. Manufactured scarcity. A waitlist. A cap shaped like a horse. A secondary market where a bottle that left the distillery at forty dollars changes hands for four hundred.

The only way to tell which is which is to take the bottle away.

Score each pour on its own merits. Names and prices at the end. Then we find out how much that price tag was influencing you.

## [SLIDE 3] Tips on tasting

Quickly, because I know you want the whiskey.

- **Nose.** Swirl it. Short sniffs, mouth slightly open, which lets the ethanol escape past your palate instead of scorching your nose. Do not bury your face in the glass.
- **Palate.** Small sip. Hold it a beat. Swallow, then breathe out through your nose. That is where most of the flavour actually arrives.
- **Finish.** What lingers, and for how long? And look past the burn. Heat is not flavour. Some of tonight's pours are strong and some are gentle. If you score heat, you are just scoring proof.
- **Score.** Write the number down before you talk with everyone. We'll chat and discuss between pours so we're not going too fast.

> **THE POUR SEQUENCE. THIS SLIDE STAYS UP.**
> Work through the whole lineup here. Say the letter, pour, wait, let them score, let them talk. Name nothing.
> Between pours, ask one open question and then be quiet:
> "Does anything here remind you of anything you have already tasted?"
> "Is this bigger than the last one? And is bigger reading to you as better?"
> "Does this taste like one thing, or like several things assembled?"
> At the ringer, say nothing at all. Let them sit with it.
> At the barrel-proof closer, have everyone add a few drops of water and taste again.

> **BEFORE YOU ADVANCE**
> Every card should be full. Every score written, committed. Nothing has been named.
> Say it out loud: "Pens down!"

## [SLIDE 4] Sample A: Buffalo Trace

> **THE REVEALS**
> One slide per bottle. Names only. No prices yet.
> The same four beats work for every bottle. Run them in this order and the section paces itself.
> Name it. "Sample A was ______."
> Give it one fact, the most interesting true thing about it. Where it is from, how it is made, what makes it unusual.
> Say why it sat where it sat in the lineup. "It went first because it was the gentlest." "It went last because its heat lingers longest."
> Ask the room one question, then stop talking.

_The Workhorse. Say what it is, and then say the honest thing about it: nobody chases this bottle, and it is in every decent bar in the country for a reason. Then look at the scores. This is the bottle that quietly wins more often than anyone expects, and if it did, that is the whole evening in one example. Hold its price until the reveal table._

Sample A was Buffalo Trace.

The flagship of one of the most awarded distilleries in the world. No age statement, though the juice is generally reckoned to be eight to ten years old. It built its reputation on being easy to like and easy to find, and for most of its life it sat on shelves around twenty-odd dollars, until the bourbon boom turned it into an allocated bottle.

It went first because it was the gentlest.

## [SLIDE 5] Sample B: Blanton's Original

_The Hyped One. Say what it is, and be fair to it. It is a good whiskey and you must say so, because if you sneer at it, your audience stops trusting you and the whole evening becomes a hit piece; people feel like they were set up and we don't want that!_

_Then say the true thing: it is well regarded and it is chased, and those are two different facts that have gotten tangled together. Again, don't talk prices yet if you can help it._

Sample B was Blanton's Original.

The world's first commercially marketed single-barrel bourbon, released in 1984 by Elmer T. Lee from Buffalo Trace's metal-clad Warehouse H. The horse-and-jockey caps spell out B-L-A-N-T-O-N-S across eight different stoppers. It is a consistent, well-regarded pour, and also one of the most hyped bottles in America, regularly sold well above shelf price on the secondary market.

_Ask: "Same distillery as sample A, different mash bill. Could you tell they were related?"_

## [SLIDE 6] Sample C: Evan Williams Bottled-in-Bond

_The Sleeper. This is the emotional centre of the night. Slow down. Say what it cost. Then say the fact that makes it more than a cheap bottle._

Sample C was Evan Williams Bottled-in-Bond. It is the cheapest bottle on the table tonight.

> **IF YOUR SLEEPER IS BOTTLED-IN-BOND, USE THIS**
> "Bottled-in-bond" is not marketing. It is law.
> It requires the product of one distilling season, by one distiller, at one distillery. Aged at least four years in a warehouse under federal supervision. Bottled at exactly 100 proof. And the label must name the distillery that made it.
> That standard was signed into law on March 3rd, 1897. It was among the first consumer-protection laws of its kind in the United States, nine years before the Pure Food and Drug Act.
> It exists because the whiskey market of the 1890s was full of grain neutral spirit, coloured and flavoured with whatever was at hand and sold as bourbon. Honest distillers wanted a way to prove they were not doing that. So they went to the government and asked to be inspected.
> The line: the cheapest bottle on this table is the only one carrying a federal guarantee of exactly what it is.

> **DO NOT OVERCLAIM IT. SAY THIS TOO.**
> Bonded means authentic, not excellent. It is a floor, not a ceiling: four years, 100 proof, one distillery. There is mediocre bonded whiskey.
> The guarantee is about honesty of production, not quality of outcome.

_Ask: "The cheapest pour of the night, straight after the most expensive one. Did you feel the drop?"_

## [SLIDE 7] Sample D: Four Roses Small Batch Select

_The bottle that keeps the night honest. Say what it is, and that here the money demonstrably buys something._

Sample D was Four Roses Small Batch Select.

Four Roses runs ten distinct recipes, two mash bills crossed with five proprietary yeasts. This expression blends six of them, every barrel at least six years old, bottled without chill-filtering at 104 proof. It is a deliberately constructed blend rather than a single barrel, built to show the distillery's range in one glass.

## [SLIDE 8] Sample E: Barrell Seagrass

_The Ringer. Reveal what it actually is, and ask: who caught it?_

Sample E was Barrell Seagrass.

The odd one out, and not even a bourbon. Barrell does not distill; they source rye from the U.S. and Canada, then finish it separately in three different casks: Martinique rum, Madeira, and apricot brandy. Blended back together at cask strength, it tastes unlike anything else on the table.

> **THE KIND TRAP. NAME IT GENTLY.**
> Look for anyone who marked the ringer down because it did not taste like its neighbours.
> That instinct, this is unfamiliar, therefore it is worse, is the same instinct that prestige pricing feeds on.
> It is worth saying out loud, and it is worth saying without a trace of gotcha in your voice. Everyone in the room has done it. Including you.

_Ask: "This is a triple-finished rye, not a bourbon. Did the finishing casks give it away, or fool you?"_

## [SLIDE 9] Sample F: Elijah Craig Barrel Proof

_The Closer._

Sample F was Elijah Craig Barrel Proof.

Uncut, unfiltered. Whatever strength it came out of the barrel at is exactly what went in the bottle. This release lands at 118.2 proof and ten years, seven months; the number changes with every batch.

It went last because its heat lingers longest, and anything after it would not have got a fair hearing.

> **ASK IT STRAIGHT**
> "If this scored highest for you, was that flavour, or was that impact?"
> There is no wrong answer. But make them sit in the question, because it is the same question the whole night is asking.
> And remind them: a few drops of water made it a different whiskey.

## [SLIDE 10] Samples A and B came from the same distillery

_Only if your lineup has the same-distillery pair. If it does not, delete this slide and go straight to the reveal._

Two of tonight's pours came out of the same distillery.

Same house, same water, same warehouses. Different recipes.

One of them costs around ______. The other, ______.

_Fill in the two prices from your own lineup. With the deck's bottles: around forty dollars, and around a hundred and twenty._

Now go look at your card. Not at what you think about that. At what you wrote down, before you knew.

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> If the room scored them close, that is the finding.
> If the room scored the expensive one higher, that is also the finding. Sometimes the money is real.
> Do not tell them what they should have found. The card already said it.

## [SLIDE 11] Now we put the prices back on

_Tally live. Read out the room's average for each sample before you put a single price on the board. We recommend going around the room and asking each person what their grand total was for each pour, and having someone record each person and average out the room for each pour. Fill in the Room score and Room rank columns as you go._

Now we put the prices back on.

> **THE MOMENTS TO PLAY FOR**
> The sleeper. Cheapest on the table. Where did it rank?
> The hyped one. The waitlist, the secondary market. Where did it rank?
> The ringer. Was not even the category everyone assumed.

Compare the column you cared about, score, against the column you could not see.

_Don't tell them what to feel. Let the numbers sit there._

## [SLIDE 12] The point

You ranked these on nothing but what was in the glass. That ranking is the honest one. Whatever you would pay, the number you wrote down before you knew anything is the truest thing on your card.

And none of this makes expensive whiskey a scam. Sometimes the money buys real age, real proof, real barrel selection, real craft. Sometimes it buys a pretty cap and a good story.

Both are fine to enjoy, as long as you know which one you are paying for.

## [SLIDE 13] Sláinte

Tonight, your palate already told you.

Sláinte.
