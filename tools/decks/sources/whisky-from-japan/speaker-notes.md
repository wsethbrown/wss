## Part One: before the night

### The one-sentence version

Japan learned whisky from Scotland and in 2021 wrote down what "Japanese whisky" means; Hakata, from a shochu distillery in Fukuoka, is made in Japan from Japanese barley with koji instead of malt, so it falls outside that definition and is sold in America as whisky from Japan.

### What the night is

History while they drink, then the turn. The first glass goes out on slide 2 and nobody is told what is unusual about it. The history (Perry, Torii and Taketsuru, Rita, the 2021 standard, the boom, the climate, mizunara) sets up slide 10, where the room finds out what they have been drinking. Then three slides of tasting, one per bottle, and Sláinte.

### How many bottles

Three, all from the same distillery: the Hakata 10, 12 and 16 Year. The only thing that changes from glass to glass is time in the sherry cask, so the room can taste what the cask does to the koji.

### Cast the roles, then find the bottles

- **Two more years (glass 2).** Poured on slide 14. Same spirit, same casks, more time. Tonight: Hakata 12 Year.
- **The most polished (glass 3).** Poured on slide 15, for the question of the night: does more time make it better, or more like everything else? Tonight: Hakata 16 Year.

## Part Two: the script

_SET UP: glass 1 (Hakata 10 Year) is poured as slide 2 goes up. Glasses 2 and 3 wait for slides 14 and 15. Read the script more or less as written._

## [SLIDE 1] Whisky From Japan

Good evening.

The title of tonight is **Whisky From Japan.** Notice I didn't say **Japanese Whisky.** That is what the producer calls it, on the label, deliberately.

By the end of the night you will know exactly why.

## [SLIDE 2] From Isolation to Distillation

_Pour the Hakata 10 Year now. Keep talking while you pour. Tell them: sip it whenever you like, we will come back to it properly in a bit._

Japan had no whisky at all until the late nineteenth century.

The country had been sealed for more than two hundred years. It took an American naval squadron in 1853 to force it open. Perry's expedition brought whisky with it: in 1854 its gifts to Japanese officials included casks of American whiskey. More came with the traders and diplomats who followed.

So everything in your glass tonight descends from something that showed up about a hundred and fifty years ago and was then rebuilt, from the ground up, by people who had to work out what it should taste like in a place that had never made it.

## [SLIDE 3] Rise and Fall of Empires

Two men, and one of the great falling-outs in whisky.

**Masataka Taketsuru** goes to Scotland in 1918, studies chemistry at Glasgow, apprentices at Longmorn and Hazelburn, marries a Scotswoman named Rita, and comes home with notebooks full of everything he had learned.

**Torii Shinjiro** hires him. In **1923** they build **Yamazaki**, Japan's first whisky distillery, outside Kyoto.

They disagreed about almost everything. Torii wanted a whisky the Japanese public would actually drink. Taketsuru wanted to make Scotch, properly, in Japan.

So in **1934**, when his ten years were up, Taketsuru left, went as far north as he could, and built his own distillery at **Yoichi** in Hokkaido, because the climate reminded him of Scotland. That became **Nikka.**

Suntory and Nikka. The two houses that still define the category, and they exist because two men could not agree.

## [SLIDE 4] And Rita

And Rita. Jessie Roberta Cowan, a doctor's daughter from Kirkintilloch, near Glasgow. She married Taketsuru in January 1920, against both families, and sailed with him to a Japan that wanted neither of them.

In Osaka she taught English and piano. While Yamazaki's first whisky sat in the cask for six years, that teaching helped keep them fed.

The story goes that when his contract ran out, she was the one who told him to go and build his own. I could not find that in a source I trust, so take it as the story. One account says the backing for the new company came through her: she taught English to the wife of a businessman, Shotaro Kaga, who helped put up the money.

Japan calls her the Mother of Japanese Whisky.

## [SLIDE 5] Japanese Whisky, Codified

Here is the thing that surprises people: **until 2021, "Japanese whisky" meant nothing at all.**

There was no rule. You could import Scotch in bulk, bottle it in Osaka, put a Japanese name on it, and sell it as Japanese whisky, entirely legally. A great deal of that happened, and some of it is still on shelves.

So in 2021 the trade body wrote a standard.

_Walk the list. Land hard on the third line._

Water from Japan. Made in Japan. **Malted grains must always be used.** Three years in a cask, in Japan. Bottled in Japan at 40% or higher.

> **SAY THE CAVEAT OUT LOUD**
> This is **not a law.** It is a voluntary standard, written by a trade association. There is no certification, no inspector, and no penalty for ignoring it. Plenty of producers are not members.
> It came into force on 1 April 2021. Whiskies already on sale under the old labels had until 31 March 2024 to comply. In 2025 the association said it was working towards making the standard law; check whether that has happened before you present.

## [SLIDE 6] Land of the Rising Dram

_They should be well into the 10 Year by now. Ask what they are getting before you move on._

The world caught on late, and then all at once.

In 2001, a Nikka single cask won a Whisky Magazine award and the category stopped being a curiosity. By 2015 a Yamazaki was being called the best whisky in the world, and the shelves emptied. You will hear that bottles that cost forty dollars started fetching four hundred; I could not pin those numbers to a source, so I won't stand behind them.

That is the boom. It is also exactly why the standard had to be written: when a category becomes that valuable, people start putting the name on things that have not earned it.

## [SLIDE 7] Making Whisky

The six steps, quickly, because one of them is about to matter enormously.

**Malting.** You soak the barley and let it start to germinate. The grain wakes up, thinks it is spring, and releases enzymes to convert its own starch into sugar. Then you halt it with heat.

That is the whole point of malting. **It is how you get the enzymes.** No enzymes, no sugar. No sugar, no fermentation. No fermentation, no alcohol.

Hold that thought.

Then mashing, fermenting, distilling, aging, bottling.

## [SLIDE 8] The Climate Is an Ingredient

Four distilleries, four climates, and this is the Japanese obsession.

Where a Scottish distiller inherits their weather, the Japanese chose theirs. Taketsuru went north to Hokkaido for cold. Suntory went to Hakushu, into forest at 2,300 feet. Yamazaki sits where three rivers meet, in perpetual mist.

None of that is decoration. Temperature swings drive whisky in and out of the wood. **The climate is an ingredient,** and Fukuoka, where tonight's whisky is from, swings from 95 degrees in summer to near freezing in winter.

## [SLIDE 9] Mizunara Oak

A quick, lovely accident.

Mizunara is a Japanese oak, and it is a **terrible** cask wood. It is porous, so it leaks. It is knotty, so it is hard to work. The tree needs about two hundred years before you can use it.

Japanese distillers wanted nothing to do with it. Then the war and the years after it cut them off from imported casks, and they had no choice. That is the usual telling; the details vary.

And it turned out that mizunara gives something no other wood gives: sandalwood, incense, a kind of temple smell. The wood they were forced to use became something the category is famous for.

> **WORTH SAYING PLAINLY**
> Tonight's whisky is **not** aged in mizunara. It is all ex-sherry. This slide is here because you cannot talk about Japanese whisky without it, not because it is in your glass.

## [SLIDE 10] What Is Actually in Your Glass

_This is the slide the evening is built on. Slow down. They have been drinking it for twenty minutes._

So. That whisky you have been enjoying.

It is made by the **Hikari Distillery** in Fukuoka, founded in **1912**, a maker of barley shochu. It is described as a family firm, with the family still living on the grounds; I could not confirm that in print. The barley is from Kyushu.

Now go back to that rulebook. The third line. **Malted grains must always be used.**

**There is no malted barley in your glass. None.**

One third of that grain is saccharified with **koji.** Koji is a mould. It is the mould used to make miso, soy sauce, and sake, and it does the same job malting does: it converts starch to sugar. It just does it from the outside, instead of waking the grain up to do it itself. The other two thirds of the barley is simply unmalted.

And under Japanese law, **a spirit made this way cannot be called whisky.** Japan's Liquor Tax Act defines whisky as grain turned to sugar by malt. Barley turned to sugar by koji and distilled in a pot still is shochu.

> **THE PART THAT MAKES IT WONDERFUL**
> This started life as **shochu.** Then it sat in sherry casks for a decade, and it went **too dark to be sold as shochu.**
> So: too dark for shochu. Made with koji, so it cannot be whisky. It has no comfortable legal category in its own country, and it is not sold there.
> It came straight to America, because the American rules only ask what the grain was, not what converted its starch.

Which is why they never call it Japanese whisky. They call it **whisky from Japan.**

That savoury note some of you have been trying to name? **That is the koji.** It is the same thing that makes miso taste like miso.

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> One reviewer also says the strength rules keep it out of Japan. The Liquor Tax Act caps single-distilled shochu at 45%, and Hakata is 42%, so I could not make that claim work. Leave it out.

## [SLIDE 11] How to Taste It

_Housekeeping. Keep it short, they have a drink in hand._

Four steps, and none of them are difficult. Look: colour tells you about the cask, not the quality. Nose: short sniffs, mouth slightly open. Taste: small sip, hold it, swallow, then breathe out through your nose. Finish: what lingers, and for how long?

Heat is not flavour. If you score heat, you are just scoring proof.

## [SLIDE 12] What You Might Find

Use the wheel if it helps. And a word of encouragement: if you are getting something savoury, brothy, or frankly like soy sauce, you are not doing it wrong. You are tasting the thing that makes this whisky what it is.

## [SLIDE 13] Hakata 10 Year

The one you have been drinking. Ten years in ex-sherry, 84 proof, around $80.

This is the one where the koji is loudest, because it has had the least time under the cask. Plum, dark cherry, raisin, and then that savoury, brothy thread underneath.

> **THE QUESTION FOR THE ROOM**
> "Now that you know what it is, does it taste different?"
> That is a real question, not a rhetorical one. Some people find the umami obvious the moment it is named. Others taste no difference at all. Both answers are honest, and the discussion is the interesting part.

## [SLIDE 14] Hakata 12 Year

_Pour the 12 Year in another glass if you can._

Two more years in the cask. Around $100.

Drier. The sherry has more of a say, and the koji has gone from a shout to a hum. Nutmeg, cherry, leather, tobacco. Several reviewers pick up a faint salinity on the finish.

Taste them side by side. Two years is doing an enormous amount of work here, and Fukuoka's summers are why.

_Ask: "What did two more years take away, and what did they add?"_

## [SLIDE 15] Hakata 16 Year

_Pour the 16 Year._

Sixteen years, around $150.

By now the sherry has largely won. Nuts, raisin, chocolate, green grape, prune, honey. The savoury note is still there, but it has been folded into everything else rather than sitting on top.

> **THE REAL QUESTION OF THE NIGHT**
> Line the three up. Does more time make it better, or does it just make it more like everything else?
> There is no correct answer, and I have watched rooms split evenly on it. The 10 is the strangest and the most itself. The 16 is the most polished. Whether polish is an improvement is genuinely up to you.

## [SLIDE 16] Sláinte

Here is what I want to leave you with.

The rulebook we read tonight exists for a good reason. It was written because people were importing Scotch, bottling it in Japan, and selling it as something it was not.

But a rule written to stop dishonesty will always catch a few honest things by accident. This is one of them. A family distillery in Fukuoka, making something from Kyushu barley and a mould their country has used for a thousand years, aging it for sixteen years in sherry casks, and doing it all in the open.

It is not Japanese whisky. It could not be more Japanese.

Sláinte.
