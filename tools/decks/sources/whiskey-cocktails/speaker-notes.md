# Whiskey Cocktails

Speaker's notes

The Whiskey Share Society

## Part One: before the night

The Old Fashioned, the Manhattan and the Sazerac. Four drinks, 18 slides, about 45 minutes.

**Running order:** slides 1–3 (7 min) · drink one (5 min) · slides 5–7 (8 min) · drink two (5 min) · slides 9–10 (5 min) · drink three (5 min) · slides 12–13 (4 min) · drink four (5 min) · slides 15–17 (4 min) · close (1 min).

**Before people arrive:** this is a Cocktails Chapter night, so the four pours are four drinks the room makes. Set up one station with a mixing glass, a bar spoon, a strainer, a jigger, a small knife or peeler, and plenty of ice. Put out the four whiskeys in order: Old Overholt Rye, Old Forester 86 Proof, Wild Turkey 101 Rye, Sazerac Rye. You also need white sugar cubes or simple syrup (one part sugar to one part water), Angostura bitters, Peychaud's bitters, a bottle of sweet vermouth (keep it in the fridge once open), a small bottle of absinthe or Herbsaint, an orange and a lemon. Each recipe below makes one full drink. Split each one between two or three people so nobody drinks four full cocktails.

## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] Whiskey Cocktails

Thanks for coming. Tonight is a Cocktails Chapter night. We are going to make four drinks and taste them: the plain whiskey cocktail, the Old Fashioned, the Manhattan and the Sazerac.

They are in the order they first appeared in print, from 1806 to about 1900. Some of the best-known stories about where these drinks came from are not true.

## [SLIDE 2] The first printed definition, 1806

The word "cock-tail" shows up in print around 1800. A London paper used it in 1798. A New Hampshire paper used it in 1803, but it didn't say what was in the drink.

The first definition is from the 13th of May 1806. It was in a paper called the Balance and Columbian Repository, in Hudson, New York. The week before, the paper had printed a joke list of what a losing candidate had spent on drinks in a local election. "Cock-tail" was on the list. A reader wrote in to ask what a cock-tail was, and the editor, Harry Croswell, answered. He wrote that a cock-tail is "a stimulating liquor, composed of spirits of any kind, sugar, water and bitters". He also said it was a good drink for elections, because it "renders the heart stout and bold, at the same time that it fuddles the head".

That is four ingredients: spirit, sugar, water and bitters. All four of tonight's drinks start from that recipe. Nobody knows where the name came from.

## [SLIDE 3] American whiskey in 1806 was young.

In 1806 most American whiskey was rye. It was made on farms and at small distilleries in Pennsylvania, Maryland and New York. Further west, in Kentucky, they made corn whiskey.

Most of it was sold young. Whiskey was moved and sold in barrels, so some of it aged a little on the way. Keeping it in charred barrels for years on purpose only became normal later.

Bitters are bark and roots steeped in alcohol. They were sold as medicine for the stomach.

People often say the cocktail was invented to make bad whiskey drinkable. It may be true, but nobody at the time said so.

## [SLIDE 4] Pour 1 of 4: Plain whiskey cocktail

Let's make the first one. This is as close to the 1806 drink as we can get.

**Recipe:** put 1 sugar cube (or 1 teaspoon of white sugar) in a small tumbler with ¼ oz of water and 2 dashes of Angostura bitters. Stir or crush until the sugar has dissolved. Add 2 oz of Old Overholt Rye and stir. No ice, no garnish.

Taste it at room temperature first. Then drop in one ice cube and taste it again a minute later.

Two things in this glass are newer than 1806. Angostura bitters went on sale in Venezuela in 1824, as a stomach medicine. And Old Overholt is a four-year-old straight rye at 86 proof, which is much older than most whiskey was in 1806. Abraham Overholt started making rye to sell at West Overton, Pennsylvania, in 1810. Beam makes Old Overholt in Kentucky now.

## [SLIDE 5] 1862: Jerry Thomas prints the recipe.

For the next fifty years bartenders made cocktails but nobody printed the recipes. In 1862 a New York bartender called Jerry Thomas published a book called How to Mix Drinks. It is usually called the first American bartender's guide, and it has a short section of cocktails.

His Whiskey Cocktail is on the slide. It is three or four dashes of gum syrup, two dashes of bitters, a wine glass of whiskey and a piece of lemon peel. You fill the glass one-third full of fine ice, shake it, and strain it into what he calls "a fancy red wine-glass".

Compare that with 1806. He uses syrup where the 1806 drink had loose sugar, because syrup mixes into a cold drink. He adds lemon peel. And he uses ice, which the 1806 definition doesn't mention.

## [SLIDE 6] Improved cocktails, and the old-fashioned way

The 1876 edition of Thomas's book adds an "Improved Whiskey Cocktail". It has two dashes of maraschino liqueur and one of absinthe.

In February 1880 the Chicago Tribune mentions "old-fashioned" cocktails. In 1882 a Chicago bartender told the same paper that old-fashioned cocktails were in fashion. He said they were made with whiskey, and that rye was more popular than bourbon. "Old-fashioned" meant the plain recipe, with sugar, bitters, whiskey and peel and nothing else. In 1895 a New York bartender, George Kappeler, printed a recipe for an Old-Fashioned Whiskey Cocktail. It is a small lump of sugar dissolved in a little water, Angostura bitters, ice, a piece of lemon peel and whiskey.

The whiskey had changed by then too. In Kentucky it had become normal to age bourbon for several years in charred barrels. Some of it was sold in sealed bottles with a brand name on them. Brown-Forman says its Old Forester was the first bourbon sold only in sealed bottles, starting in 1870.

## [SLIDE 7] The Pendennis Club claim is weak.

A common story is that the Old Fashioned was invented at the Pendennis Club in Louisville. The Pendennis is a gentlemen's club that was founded in 1881. The story is that a bartender there made the drink for Colonel James E. Pepper, who was a bourbon distiller. Pepper then took it to the bar of the Waldorf hotel in New York.

That story first appears in a book about the Waldorf bar from 1931, and even that book only says the drink "was said to have been" invented at the Pendennis. The Chicago paper was writing about old-fashioned cocktails in 1880, a year before the club was founded. And the drink is the 1806 cocktail with ice in it. There is no evidence the club made the first one.

## [SLIDE 8] Pour 2 of 4: Old Fashioned

Second drink. I've used Old Forester because it is a Louisville bourbon, from the same city as the Pendennis Club, and because it was sold in those early sealed bottles.

**Recipe:** in a rocks glass, dissolve 1 sugar cube (or ¼ oz simple syrup) in a barspoon of water with 2 dashes of Angostura bitters. Add 2 oz of Old Forester 86 Proof and one large piece of ice, or a few cubes. Stir for about twenty seconds. Cut a strip of orange peel, twist it over the glass so the oil goes on the surface, and drop it in.

No muddled fruit and no cherry. An orange slice and a cherry first show up in Old Fashioned recipes in 1933.

Taste it next to the first one. This one is cold, it has more water in it from the ice, and it is made with bourbon where the first was made with rye.

## [SLIDE 9] The Manhattan is printed in a bartenders' guide.

The Manhattan adds one ingredient to the cocktail, and that is vermouth. Vermouth is wine with spirit added to it, flavored with herbs.

The earliest mention of a Manhattan cocktail that anyone has found is from 1882. It was in a New York gossip column that was printed in small-town papers that summer. The first printed recipes came two years later, in The Modern Bartenders' Guide by O. H. Byron. He gives two. The first has twice as much vermouth as whiskey, with Angostura bitters and gum syrup. The second is equal parts whiskey and vermouth, with curaçao and Angostura. Both are sweeter than a Manhattan is today.

The whiskey was usually rye. For most of the 1800s the East Coast drank rye from Pennsylvania and Maryland. A Boston paper in 1883 does describe one made with bourbon.

## [SLIDE 10] The Jennie Jerome story is wrong.

The most common story is that the Manhattan was invented for a party at the Manhattan Club in New York in late 1874. The party is supposed to have been given by Jennie Jerome. She was a New Yorker who had married Lord Randolph Churchill in Paris that April. The party was to celebrate Samuel Tilden being elected governor of New York.

The dates don't work. Tilden was elected on the 3rd of November 1874. Jennie Jerome was in England, and on the 30th of November she gave birth to Winston Churchill at Blenheim Palace. She was not giving a party in New York that month.

There are other stories. In 1923 a man who had been a bartender at the Hoffman House in New York wrote that the Manhattan was invented in the 1860s by a man called Black, who had a bar on Broadway. Nobody has been able to back that up. We know the drink was being made in New York by 1882. We don't know who made it first.

## [SLIDE 11] Pour 3 of 4: Manhattan

Third drink. This is Wild Turkey 101 Rye. It is a Kentucky straight rye at 101 proof. The higher proof stands up to the vermouth.

**Recipe:** in a mixing glass with plenty of ice, put 2 oz of Wild Turkey 101 Rye, 1 oz of sweet vermouth and 2 dashes of Angostura bitters. Stir for about thirty seconds, until the glass is very cold. Strain into a chilled coupe or cocktail glass. Garnish with a cocktail cherry, or a strip of lemon peel if you'd rather.

If anyone wants to try something closer to Byron's second recipe, make one with 1½ oz each of rye and sweet vermouth and pass both round.

## [SLIDE 12] Peychaud's bitters and the Sazerac House

The Sazerac comes from New Orleans. Antoine Peychaud was a pharmacist on Royal Street. He was born around 1803, and most accounts say he came from what is now Haiti. He sold his own bitters. They are redder than Angostura and taste more of anise.

On the same street was a bar called the Merchants Exchange Coffee House, with a back door on Exchange Alley. A man called Sewell Taylor ran it. Taylor later went into importing a French cognac called Sazerac. In the 1850s the next owner renamed the bar the Sazerac Coffee House. Around 1870 Thomas Handy, who had worked for Taylor, took over the bar. A few years later he bought the rights to Peychaud's bitters.

The Sazerac cocktail is first named in print in 1899.

Another story is that Peychaud invented the cocktail. The story is that he served brandy in egg cups, that the French for egg cup is "coquetier", and that "coquetier" turned into "cocktail". It isn't true. The word was in print in New Hampshire and New York years before Peychaud was selling bitters.

## [SLIDE 13] Cognac or rye: the printed record starts with whiskey.

The usual story is that the Sazerac was first made with cognac. Then, in the late 1800s, an insect called phylloxera destroyed most of the vineyards in France. Cognac is made from wine, so it got scarce, and the bar switched to rye.

That story comes from a New Orleans drinks book published in 1937. The oldest recipe we have was printed in 1908, and it uses whiskey. One account says the Sazerac bar was already selling it in bottles, made with whiskey, in the 1880s and 1890s. It may have been made with cognac before that, but nobody has shown that it was.

The other ingredient is absinthe. You use it to coat the inside of the glass. The United States banned absinthe in 1912. Herbsaint is a substitute that has been made in New Orleans since 1934. It is still sold, and a lot of bartenders there use it.

## [SLIDE 14] Pour 4 of 4: Sazerac

Last drink. This one has the most steps. Sazerac Rye is a straight rye at 90 proof. It is made at Buffalo Trace in Frankfort, Kentucky. The company that makes it now owns the Sazerac name and Peychaud's bitters.

**Recipe:** chill a rocks glass with ice and water. In a mixing glass, dissolve 1 sugar cube (or ¼ oz simple syrup) with a few drops of water, add 3 dashes of Peychaud's bitters (and 1 dash of Angostura if you like), then 2 oz of Sazerac Rye. Fill with ice and stir for about thirty seconds. Empty the chilled glass, pour in about ¼ teaspoon of absinthe or Herbsaint, swirl it round to coat the inside and tip out the excess. Strain the drink into the glass, with no ice. Twist a strip of lemon peel over the top and throw the peel away.

Smell it before you drink it. You should smell the absinthe more than you taste it.

## [SLIDE 15] Where the muddled fruit came from

Prohibition ran from January 1920 to December 1933. For those years most people drank whatever bootleggers sold. The photograph is New York in 1921. Agents are pouring seized liquor into a sewer after a raid.

An orange slice and a cherry show up in Old Fashioned recipes in 1933, as a garnish. People usually say the fruit was there to cover the taste of bootleg liquor, but nobody has proved that. Crushing the fruit in the glass may only have started after the Second World War.

## [SLIDE 16] The origin stories were written down later.

Tonight's three origin stories were written down long after the drinks were first made. The man called Black is from 1923. The Pendennis Club story is from 1931. The cognac Sazerac is from 1937. The recipes are older. Thomas printed his in 1862, Byron in 1884 and Kappeler in 1895, and the first Sazerac recipe is from 1908.

After Prohibition ended there was not much straight whiskey, so distillers stretched it by selling blends. Rye lost ground to bourbon. These drinks came back when bartenders started reading the old books again. In 2007 David Wondrich published a book called Imbibe!, which reprinted Jerry Thomas's recipes with the history behind them.

## [SLIDE 17] The four recipes, for home

The plain whiskey cocktail is 2 oz of whiskey, a teaspoon of sugar, a splash of water and 2 dashes of Angostura. The Old Fashioned is the same drink stirred over ice, with orange peel. The Manhattan uses 1 oz of sweet vermouth where the others use sugar, and it is served with no ice. The Sazerac uses rye, Peychaud's bitters and a glass rinsed with absinthe, and you throw the lemon peel away.
