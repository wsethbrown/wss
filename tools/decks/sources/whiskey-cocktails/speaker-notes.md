## Part One: before the night

The Old Fashioned, the Manhattan and the Sazerac. Four drinks, 18 slides, about 45 minutes.

**Running order:** slides 1–3 (7 min) · drink one (5 min) · slides 5–7 (8 min) · drink two (5 min) · slides 9–10 (5 min) · drink three (5 min) · slides 12–13 (4 min) · drink four (5 min) · slides 15–17 (4 min) · close (1 min).

**Before people arrive:** this is a Cocktails Chapter night, so the four pours are four drinks the room makes. Set up one station with a mixing glass, a bar spoon, a strainer, a jigger, a small knife or peeler, and plenty of ice. Put out the four whiskeys in order: Old Overholt Rye, Old Forester 86 Proof, Wild Turkey 101 Rye, Sazerac Rye. You also need white sugar cubes or simple syrup (one part sugar to one part water), Angostura bitters, Peychaud's bitters, a bottle of sweet vermouth (keep it in the fridge once open), a small bottle of absinthe or Herbsaint, an orange and a lemon. Each recipe below makes one full drink. Split each one between two or three people so nobody drinks four full cocktails.

## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] Whiskey Cocktails

Thanks for coming. Tonight is a Cocktails Chapter, so instead of four neat pours we're going to make four drinks and taste them. They're the plain whiskey cocktail, the Old Fashioned, the Manhattan and the Sazerac.

Each one was written down at a particular time and place, and each recipe says something about the whiskey people had to hand. We'll take them in order, from 1806 to about 1900, then look at when the stories about them were written.

## [SLIDE 2] The first printed definition, 1806

The word "cock-tail" turns up in print around 1800. The earliest American use usually cited is in a New Hampshire paper, the Farmer's Cabinet, in April 1803, and it doesn't say what was in the drink. A London paper used the word in 1798.

The definition everyone quotes is from the 13th of May 1806, in a paper called the Balance and Columbian Repository, published in Hudson, New York. The week before, the paper had printed a joke list of what a losing candidate in a local election had spent on drinks, and "cock-tail" was on it. A reader wrote in to ask what a cock-tail was, and the editor, Harry Croswell, answered. He wrote that a cock-tail is "a stimulating liquor, composed of spirits of any kind, sugar, water and bitters". He added that it was vulgarly called bittered sling, and that it was an excellent electioneering potion, because it "renders the heart stout and bold, at the same time that it fuddles the head".

So the first definition we have is four ingredients: spirit, sugar, water, bitters. Every drink tonight is that recipe with something changed. Where the name came from, nobody knows.

## [SLIDE 3] American whiskey in 1806 was young.

American whiskey in 1806 was mostly rye, made on farms and at small distilleries in Pennsylvania, Maryland and New York, with corn whiskey further west in Kentucky.

Most of it was sold young. Barrels were how whiskey was moved and sold, and some ageing happened on the way, but holding whiskey in charred barrels for years on purpose became normal later. Most of what people drank was young.

Bitters were sold as medicine. I should say that "the cocktail was invented to make bad whiskey drinkable" is a guess people make. It's a reasonable one, but nobody in 1806 wrote it down.

## [SLIDE 4] Pour 1 of 4: Plain whiskey cocktail

Let's make the first one. It's as close to 1806 as we can get.

**Recipe:** put 1 sugar cube (or 1 teaspoon of white sugar) in a small tumbler with ¼ oz of water and 2 dashes of Angostura bitters. Stir or crush until the sugar has dissolved. Add 2 oz of Old Overholt Rye and stir. No ice, no garnish.

Taste it at room temperature first. Then drop in one ice cube and taste it again a minute later, because that's the difference the next sixty years made.

Two cheats to admit. Angostura is later: Johann Siegert began selling it in Venezuela in 1824, as a stomach medicine. And Old Overholt is a four-year-old straight rye at 86 proof, far older than most whiskey in 1806. Abraham Overholt began distilling commercially at West Overton, Pennsylvania, in 1810. Beam makes it in Kentucky now.

## [SLIDE 5] 1862: Jerry Thomas prints the recipe.

For the next fifty years the cocktail was something bartenders made rather than something anyone wrote down. That changed in 1862, when a New York bartender named Jerry Thomas published How to Mix Drinks, or The Bon-Vivant's Companion. It's usually called the first American bartender's guide, and it has a short section of cocktails.

His Whiskey Cocktail, as printed, is three or four dashes of gum syrup, two dashes of bitters, a wine glass of whiskey and a piece of lemon peel, with a glass one-third full of fine ice, shaken and strained into "a fancy red wine-glass". The bitters he names are "Bogart's", which is usually read as a misprint for Boker's, a brand of the time.

Compare that with 1806. Syrup has replaced loose sugar, because it mixes into cold liquid, and there's citrus peel. And there's ice, which Thomas takes for granted and the 1806 definition doesn't mention.

## [SLIDE 6] Improved cocktails, and the old-fashioned way

The 1876 edition of Thomas's guide adds an "Improved Whiskey Cocktail", with two dashes of maraschino and one of absinthe.

Not every customer wanted them. In February 1880 the Chicago Tribune mentions "old-fashioned" cocktails, and in 1882 a Chicago barman told the same paper that the old-fashioned cocktails in fashion were made with whiskey, rye more often than bourbon. The name meant the plain recipe, sugar, bitters, whiskey and peel, without the additions. In 1895 George Kappeler, a New York bartender, printed recipes under that name in Modern American Drinks, including an Old-Fashioned Whiskey Cocktail: a small lump of sugar dissolved in a little water, Angostura, ice, a piece of lemon peel and whiskey.

By then the whiskey was different too. In Kentucky, charred barrels and several years of ageing had become normal for bourbon, and some of it was sold in sealed bottles under a brand. Brown-Forman says Old Forester was the first bourbon sold only in sealed bottles, from 1870.

## [SLIDE 7] The Pendennis Club claim is weak.

You'll often be told the Old Fashioned was invented at the Pendennis Club in Louisville, a gentlemen's club founded in 1881. The usual version is that a bartender there made it for Colonel James E. Pepper, a bourbon distiller, who then introduced it at the bar of the Waldorf in New York.

The earliest known source for that is Albert Stevens Crockett's Old Waldorf Bar Days, published in 1931, which says the drink was introduced by, or in honour of, Pepper and "was said to have been" the invention of a Pendennis bartender. The trouble is that the drink is the original cocktail under a new name, and the Chicago paper was calling cocktails old-fashioned in 1880, before the club existed. The club may well have made a good one. There's no evidence it made the first.

## [SLIDE 8] Pour 2 of 4: Old Fashioned

Second drink. I've used Old Forester because it's a Louisville bourbon, from the same town as the Pendennis Club, and because of those early sealed bottles.

**Recipe:** in a rocks glass, dissolve 1 sugar cube (or ¼ oz simple syrup) in a barspoon of water with 2 dashes of Angostura bitters. Add 2 oz of Old Forester 86 Proof and one large piece of ice, or a few cubes. Stir for about twenty seconds. Cut a strip of orange peel, twist it over the glass so the oil goes on the surface, and drop it in.

No muddled fruit and no cherry. We'll get to those later on.

Taste it next to the first one. The differences are the cold, the dilution and a bourbon in place of a rye.

## [SLIDE 9] The Manhattan is printed in a bartenders' guide.

The Manhattan adds one ingredient to the cocktail, which is vermouth: wine fortified and flavoured with herbs.

The earliest known mention of the Manhattan cocktail is in a New York gossip column syndicated to small-town papers in late summer 1882; the Olean Democrat ran it on the 5th of September. The first printed recipes are usually credited to O. H. Byron's The Modern Bartenders' Guide of 1884, and Byron gives two. His first is a pony of French vermouth to half a pony of whiskey, so twice as much vermouth as whiskey, with Angostura and gum syrup. His second is equal parts whiskey and Italian vermouth, with curaçao and Angostura. Both are sweeter than a modern Manhattan.

The whiskey was usually rye. Through most of the nineteenth century the East Coast drank rye from Pennsylvania and Maryland, though an 1883 Boston paper describes a Manhattan made with bourbon.

## [SLIDE 10] The Jennie Jerome story is wrong.

The story you'll hear most often about the Manhattan is that it was created for a party at the Manhattan Club in New York in late 1874. The party was supposedly given by Jennie Jerome, a New Yorker who had married Lord Randolph Churchill that April in Paris, to celebrate Samuel Tilden's election as governor of New York.

This one can be checked, and it's wrong. Tilden was elected on the 3rd of November 1874. Jennie Jerome was in England, and she gave birth to Winston Churchill at Blenheim Palace on the 30th of November. She was not hosting a party in New York that month.

There are other claims. In 1923 William Mulhall, who had tended bar at the Hoffman House, wrote that it was invented in the 1860s by a man named Black who kept a place on Broadway, ten doors below Houston Street. The honest answer is that the drink was being made in New York by 1882 and nobody knows who made it first.

## [SLIDE 11] Pour 3 of 4: Manhattan

Third drink. Wild Turkey 101 Rye, a Kentucky straight rye at 101 proof, made in Lawrenceburg. The higher proof holds up against the vermouth.

**Recipe:** in a mixing glass with plenty of ice, put 2 oz of Wild Turkey 101 Rye, 1 oz of sweet vermouth and 2 dashes of Angostura bitters. Stir for about thirty seconds, until the glass is very cold. Strain into a chilled coupe or cocktail glass. Garnish with a cocktail cherry, or a strip of lemon peel if you'd rather.

If anyone wants to try something closer to Byron's second version, make one with 1½ oz each of rye and sweet vermouth and pass both round.

## [SLIDE 12] Peychaud's bitters and the Sazerac House

The Sazerac comes from New Orleans. Antoine Amédée Peychaud, born around 1803 and usually said to come from Saint-Domingue, now Haiti, was an apothecary on Royal Street who sold his own bitters, redder than Angostura, with more anise. Dates for the bitters range from the 1830s, the company's, to the 1850s.

Sewell Taylor ran the Merchants Exchange Coffee House, with a door on Exchange Alley, and later went into importing a cognac, Sazerac-de-Forge et Fils. In the 1850s, usually given as 1852, the new owner, Aaron Bird, renamed it the Sazerac Coffee House. Around 1870 Thomas Handy, who had clerked for Taylor, took over the bar, and in 1873 or thereabouts he bought the rights to Peychaud's bitters. These dates come from secondary histories and they don't all agree.

The cocktail itself is first named in print in 1899. You will sometimes hear that Peychaud invented the cocktail, serving brandy in egg cups called coquetiers, and that "coquetier" turned into "cocktail". That's a legend. The word was in print in New Hampshire and New York years before anyone was selling Peychaud's bitters.

## [SLIDE 13] Cognac or rye: the printed record starts with whiskey.

The usual story is that the Sazerac was a cognac drink until phylloxera, an insect from America, destroyed most French vineyards in the late nineteenth century. Cognac is distilled from wine, so as the vines died, cognac got scarce, and the bar switched to rye.

That story is mostly told on the authority of Stanley Clisby Arthur's Famous New Orleans Drinks and How to Mix 'Em, from 1937. The record runs the other way. The first published recipe, in William Boothby's The World's Drinks and How to Mix Them in 1908, uses whiskey, and David Wondrich has argued that the Sazerac House sold it as a bottled whiskey cocktail in the 1880s and 1890s. Whether anyone ever made it with cognac first is still argued over. I'd treat the cognac version as possible and unproven.

The other ingredient is absinthe, which coats the glass. The US banned absinthe in 1912. Herbsaint, made in New Orleans from 1934 as a substitute, is still sold, and a lot of local bartenders use it.

## [SLIDE 14] Pour 4 of 4: Sazerac

Last drink, and the most fiddly. Sazerac Rye, a straight rye at 90 proof, is made at Buffalo Trace in Frankfort, Kentucky, by the company that now owns the Sazerac name and Peychaud's bitters.

**Recipe:** chill a rocks glass with ice and water. In a mixing glass, dissolve 1 sugar cube (or ¼ oz simple syrup) with a few drops of water, add 3 dashes of Peychaud's bitters (and 1 dash of Angostura if you like), then 2 oz of Sazerac Rye. Fill with ice and stir for about thirty seconds. Empty the chilled glass, pour in about ¼ teaspoon of absinthe or Herbsaint, swirl it round to coat the inside and tip out the excess. Strain the drink into the glass, with no ice. Twist a strip of lemon peel over the top and throw the peel away.

Smell it before you drink. The absinthe should be mostly a smell.

## [SLIDE 15] Where the muddled fruit came from

National Prohibition ran from January 1920 to December 1933, and for thirteen years most drinkers got whatever bootleggers sold. The photograph is New York in 1921, with police watching agents pour seized liquor into a sewer.

Orange and a cherry turn up in Old Fashioned recipes from 1933, in Julien Proskauer's What'll You Have?, but as a garnish, not muddled in. The usual explanation is that fruit covered the taste of bootleg liquor. That's hard to prove, and some historians date the muddling to after the Second World War.

## [SLIDE 16] The origin stories were written down later.

It's worth noticing when tonight's origin stories were written. Mulhall's man named Black is from 1923. Crockett's Pendennis Club is from 1931. Arthur's cognac Sazerac is from 1937, four years after Repeal. All were set down long after the event. The recipes are older: Thomas in 1862, Byron in 1884, Kappeler in 1895, Boothby in 1908.

After Repeal, blends stretched the short stocks of straight whiskey, and rye lost ground to bourbon. The return started in bars, with bartenders going back to the old books. David Wondrich's Imbibe!, published in 2007, put Jerry Thomas's recipes and the newspaper record back in front of them.

## [SLIDE 17] The four recipes, for home

The plain whiskey cocktail is 2 oz of whiskey, a teaspoon of sugar, a splash of water and 2 dashes of Angostura. The Old Fashioned is the same drink stirred over ice, with orange peel. The Manhattan swaps the sugar for 1 oz of sweet vermouth and is served straight up. The Sazerac uses Peychaud's bitters, a rye, and an absinthe rinse, with the lemon peel thrown away.

If you remember one ratio, make it two of rye to one of vermouth. And keep opened vermouth in the fridge, since it's wine and goes off within a few weeks.
