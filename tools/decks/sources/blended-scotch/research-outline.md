# Blended Scotch

Research outline: 1830 to 1910

1830 to 1910. A guide for anyone who wants to research the subject and build a talk of their own.

---

**Sources.** The facts in this deck were checked in November 2026 against the sources listed under each module: parliamentary records, contemporary newspapers, the London and Edinburgh Gazettes, and named histories, with company accounts used for what the companies make. What could not be confirmed is listed under "Not confirmed" near the end, with what would settle it.

## Module 1. Scotch before the patent still

**The question.** What was Scotch whisky, and how was it sold, around 1830?

### Nailed down

- Scotch was malt whisky from pot stills, sold by distillers by the cask to merchants, grocers and innkeepers.
- The Excise Act 1823 cut the duty to 2s 5d a gallon, more than halving it, and brought in a £10 annual licence and a 40-gallon minimum still.
- George Smith's Glenlivet distillery at Upper Drumin was licensed in November 1824 (9 November by the SMWS account; an estate letter of 10 November has him about to fetch the licence).
- Landseer's _The Illicit Highland Whisky Still_ was shown at the Royal Academy in 1829 and hangs at Apsley House (English Heritage, WM.1532-1948).

### Primary documents

- The Excise Act 1823.
- Parliamentary Papers: reports of the Commissioners of Inquiry into the Excise (1820s–1830s).
- Michael Moss and John Hume, _The Making of Scotch Whisky_ (1981), the standard business history.

### Sources

- SMWS Unfiltered, "A belated bicentenary" (the 1823 Act): https://unfiltered.smws.com/unfiltered-05-2024/the-knowledge-1823-excise-act
- Business and Information History (Glasgow), article on early Glenlivet licensing: https://journals.gla.ac.uk/bih/article/download/598/262/1813
- Wikipedia, "The Glenlivet": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Glenlivet
- Wikipedia, "The Illicit Highland Whisky Still": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Illicit_Highland_Whisky_Still
- Victorian Web, Landseer, The Illicit Whisky Still: https://victorianweb.org/painting/landseer/paintings/19.html

### Traps

- "Smuggling" folklore is plentiful and mostly unsourced. Use the excise prosecutions in the Parliamentary Papers for numbers.

## Module 2. The continuous still

**The question.** Who invented the continuous still, and who adopted it?

### Nailed down

- Aeneas Coffey rose to Inspector-General of Excise in Ireland and resigned from the excise service in 1824. He then ran distilleries in Dublin, including the Dock Distillery, Grand Canal Street.
- The big Dublin distillers rejected the patent still. The first Scottish distillery to install a Coffey still was Grange, in 1834; eleven more had followed by 1850.
- Lowland distillers, the Steins and Haigs foremost, had shipped spirit to London for rectifying into gin since 1777.
- He patented his two-column still in 1830; one source gives the grant as patent no. 5974, 5 February 1831.
- Robert Stein took out two patents: no. 5583 (13 December 1827) and no. 5721 (4 December 1828), the second for heating a continuous supply of wash. His still went first into Kirkliston distillery and then into the Haigs' Cameronbridge distillery in Fife, around 1829–31. It had to be stopped for cleaning.

### Primary documents

- The patent specifications (Coffey 1830; Stein 1827 and 1828), printed in the _Repertory of Patent Inventions_.
- _Dictionary of Irish Biography_, "Coffey, Aeneas".
- Alfred Barnard, _The Whisky Distilleries of the United Kingdom_ (1887), for patent-still distilleries in operation.

### Sources

- Wikipedia, "Aeneas Coffey" (citing the Dictionary of Irish Biography): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneas_Coffey
- Irish Times, "Distilled genius that kick-started the whisky industry": https://www.irishtimes.com/news/distilled-genius-that-kick-started-the-whisky-industry-1.1306727
- Turtle Bunbury, history of the Dock Distillery: https://turtlebunbury.com/?p=18856
- Clackmannanshire Council history, "Robert Stein Column Still Patent": https://clackmannanshire.scot/index.php/history/robert-stein-column-still-patent
- University of Strathclyde thesis on Scottish distilling technology: https://stax.strath.ac.uk/downloads/g732d9306
- Wikipedia, "Column still": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_still
- Difford's Guide, Irish whiskey history: https://www.diffordsguide.com/g/1144/irish-whiskey-uisce-beatha-eireannach/history
- Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails, Coffey still entry: https://www.spiritsanddistilling.com/dictionary/acref-9780199311132-e-1328
- Canmore, Kennetpans distillery: https://canmore.org.uk/insites/153

### Traps

- Coffey is often called the inventor of the column still. Stein, and French and German column designs, came first.
- Coffey's dates of death are given as 1839, 1850 and 1852 in different places. The DIB is the one to trust.

## Module 3. Vatting and the 1860 Spirits Act

**The question.** When did the law allow malt and grain to be mixed before duty was paid, and who took advantage of it?

### Nailed down

- In 1853 a change to the spirit duties law allowed whiskies from the same distillery to be vatted together in bond. Andrew Usher & Co., Edinburgh agents for Smith's Glenlivet, launched Old Vatted Glenlivet the same year.
- Andrew Usher the elder became a spirit merchant in 1813 and died on 17 August 1855, aged 73. Andrew Usher the younger lived 1826–1898 and gave £100,000 for the Usher Hall, Edinburgh, in 1896.
- The Spirits Act 1860 (23 & 24 Vict. c.114, royal assent 28 August 1860) consolidated the law on distilling and dealing in spirits. It allowed spirits from different distilleries, malt and grain among them, to be blended in bond before duty was paid. Gladstone was Chancellor of the Exchequer.

### Primary documents

- The Spirits Act 1860 (23 & 24 Vict. c.114); the Spirits Act 1880 replaced it.
- Hansard, 23 June 1853, Excise Duties on Spirits Bill.
- Edinburgh Post Office directories for the Usher firm.

### Sources

- Wikipedia, "Spirits Act 1860": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirits_Act_1860
- Hansard, HC Deb 23 June 1853: https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1853/jun/23/excise-duties-on-spirits-bill
- Whisky Magazine, "The godfather of blending": https://whiskymag.com/articles/the-godfather-of-blending/
- Wikipedia, "Andrew Usher": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Usher
- Chivas Brothers archive, Glenlivet story: https://history.chivasbrothersarchive.com/glenlivet/our-story/
- Edinburgh Whisky Academy, "The Forbes Mackenzie Act and the story of blended Scotch whisky": https://www.edinburghwhiskyacademy.com/blogs/feature/the-forbes-mackenzie-act-the-story-of-blended-scotch-whisky
- UCL Legacies of British Slavery database, Andrew Usher (the elder): https://lbs.ucl.ac.uk/lbs/person/view/1934603467

- Bonhams, Usher's Old Vatted Glenlivet, late 19th century: https://www.bonhams.com/auction/21887/lot/55/ushers-old-vatted-glenlivet-late-19th-century

### Traps

- "Father of blending" and "first blended whisky" are later titles. Old Vatted Glenlivet was a vatting of one distillery's whisky until the 1860 Act; contemporaries were doing similar things.
- The 1853 change is usually credited to the Forbes Mackenzie Act, which was about pub licensing; the vatting permission came with the 1853 spirit duties legislation. One account says the vatting had to happen after duty was paid; the Chivas archive and others say in bond.

## Module 4. The grocer-blenders

**The question.** Who were the merchants who turned blending into brands?

### Nailed down

- John Walker opened a grocer's shop in Kilmarnock in 1820. His son Alexander built the whisky business. The striding man was drawn by the cartoonist Tom Browne in 1908 (one account says 1909). In 1909 the firm's Old Highland range was renamed Johnnie Walker: Special Old Highland became Red Label and Extra Special Old Highland became Black Label (12 years).
- James Chivas joined a grocery business on King Street, Aberdeen, around 1838. His brother John joined him in 1857. The firm dates its royal warrant to 1843.
- John Dewar set up as a wine and spirit merchant at 111 High Street, Perth, in May 1846.
- George Ballantine opened a grocer's in Edinburgh in 1827 and later opened a larger shop in Glasgow, usually dated 1865 (one profile gives 1869). He retired in 1881.

### Primary documents

- Company archives (read as advocates): Diageo for Walker and Dewar's; Chivas Brothers.
- Local directories for Kilmarnock, Aberdeen, Perth, Edinburgh and Glasgow.
- The London Gazette for royal warrants.

## Module 5. Phylloxera and the English market

**The question.** Did the vine pest make Scotch the drink of England?

### Nailed down

- Phylloxera, an aphid native to North America, was damaging vines in southern France in the 1860s. A commission including Jules-Émile Planchon identified it in 1868.
- The first Cognac vineyards were attacked in 1872. One account gives the Charente vineyard as 283,000 hectares in 1877 and 41,000 in 1893. Grafting onto American rootstock was the cure; replanting took hold in the 1890s.
- Glenfiddich's first spirit ran on Christmas Day 1887. Dewar's Aberfeldy distillery began production in 1898.
- The Punch cartoon is "The Phylloxera, a true gourmet", _Punch_, 6 September 1890, page 110.

### Primary documents

- Cognac production statistics (Bureau National Interprofessionnel du Cognac histories).
- UK import and duty figures for brandy and home consumption of spirits, in the annual Customs and Inland Revenue reports.

### Sources

- Wikipedia, "Great French Wine Blight": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phylloxera_plague
- History Today, "The Great Wine Blight": https://www.historytoday.com/archive/months-past/great-wine-blight
- Decanter, "The root of all evil": https://www.decanter.com/wine-news/the-root-of-all-evil-an-in-depth-look-at-phyloxera-102026/
- Whisky Magazine, "Phylloxera, brandy and whisky": https://www.whiskymag.com/articles/phylloxera-brandy-and-whisky/
- Cognac-ton, on phylloxera in Cognac (a seller's site, used only for the 1872 date and the hectares): https://cognac-ton.nl/en/?p=15752
- Glenfiddich, "Since 1887": https://glenfiddich.com/au/since-1887
- Diffords Guide, John Dewar and Sons history: https://www.diffordsguide.com/producers/857/john-dewar-and-sons-ltd
- Wikimedia Commons, "Phylloxera cartoon.png": https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Phylloxera_cartoon.png

### Traps

- Phylloxera is told as the single cause of Scotch's success in England. Chris Middleton (Whisky Magazine) shows British whisky consumption already far exceeded brandy in 1860. One account gives UK brandy imports falling from about 3.5 million gallons (1872) to under 1.5 million (1890); check it against the Customs reports before quoting it.
- Glenfiddich's own pages say building began in 1887; other histories say the distillery was founded in 1886.

## Module 6. The salesmen

**The question.** Who sold blended Scotch to London and the world?

### Nailed down

- Thomas Robert Dewar (1864–1930) went to London in 1885, aged 21. He wrote _A Ramble Round the Globe_ (1894) about his sales tour. Knighted 1902, created Baron Dewar 1919.
- Dewar's dates its White Label blend to 1899, made by its first master blender, A. J. Cameron.
- James Buchanan (1849–1935) was born in Ontario and brought up in Glasgow. He came to London in 1879 as agent for Charles Mackinlay & Co. and set up on his own in 1884. He supplied the House of Commons from December 1885 (the contract went elsewhere in 1895). The blend was sold as Black & White from 1904. Created Baron Woolavington 1922.
- The Buchanan caricature is "Whisky and Horses", Spy, _Vanity Fair_, 20 November 1907.

### Primary documents

- Dewar, _A Ramble Round the Globe_ (1894), on the Internet Archive.
- _Vanity Fair_, 20 November 1907.

## Module 7. The Pattison crash, 1898

**The question.** What happened to Pattisons Ltd, and what did it do to the industry?

### Nailed down

- Pattisons Ltd of Leith and Edinburgh stopped payment in December 1898; Robert Pattison's estate was sequestrated in March 1899.
- The crash took several firms that had dealt with the Pattisons down with it, left the trade overstocked, and ended the distillery-building boom; the Distillers Company Ltd bought up distressed assets.
- Robert and Walter Pattison were arrested in April 1901 and tried that July on charges of fraud and embezzlement. Robert was convicted on four counts, Walter on two. On 17 July 1901 the Lord Justice-General sentenced Robert to 18 months and Walter to 9 months.

### Primary documents

- Reports of the trial in _The Scotsman_, July 1901.
- _Wine Trade Review_ and _Wine and Spirit Trade Record_ for 1898–99.
- Edinburgh Gazette notices of sequestration.

### Sources

- The Scotsman, "The Pattison Bros: the men who nearly brought down the Scotch whisky industry": https://foodanddrink.scotsman.com/drink/pattison-bros-men-nearly-brought-scotch-whisky-industry/
- Papers Past, Otago Daily Times, 20 July 1901 (sentences): https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/ODT19010720.2.33.1
- Papers Past, Evening Star, 19 July 1901: https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/ESD19010719.2.62

- Sources call the firm Leith-based and Edinburgh-based; it had both.

## Module 8. "What is whisky?", 1905–1909

**The question.** How did British law decide that grain spirit was whisky?

### Nailed down

- On 12 September 1905 an inspector's agent bought "Irish whiskey" at a pub in Hazelville Road, Islington. On 26 February 1906, at North London Police Court, the magistrate Edward Snow Fordham held that patent-still spirit could not be sold as Irish or Scotch whisky. The appeal at Clerkenwell Sessions was postponed and ended without a decision.
- The Royal Commission on Whiskey and Other Potable Spirits was appointed by warrant of 18 February 1908, chaired by Henry, Baron James of Hereford (1828–1911), Gladstone's Solicitor General (1873) and Attorney General (1873–74, 1880–85). It sat 37 days between March 1908 and May 1909 and heard 116 witnesses. The interim report came in July 1908.
- The final report (Cd. 4796), with conclusions dated 28 July 1909 and published in August 1909, defined whiskey as spirit distilled from a mash of cereal grains saccharified by the diastase of malt, and did not restrict the type of still.
- President Taft's decision on the meaning of "whisky" is dated 27 December 1909.
- The Immature Spirits (Restriction) Act 1915, s.1(1), barred spirits from home consumption until warehoused at least three years. Two years figured only in the debates and in Customs treatment of blends in 1915.

### Primary documents

- Royal Commission on Whiskey and Other Potable Spirits, Interim Report (1908) and Final Report (Cd. 4796, 1909) with minutes of evidence.
- Medical Officer of Health reports for Islington, 1905–06 (Wellcome Library, London's Pulse).
- The Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009 (SI 2009/2890), regulation 3: five categories (single malt, single grain, blended malt, blended grain, blended); at least three years in oak casks of no more than 700 litres, in Scotland; nothing added but water and plain caramel (E150A).
