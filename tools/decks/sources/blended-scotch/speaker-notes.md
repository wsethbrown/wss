## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] Blended Scotch

Thanks for coming. Tonight is about blended Scotch. Most of the Scotch ever drunk has been blended.

Around 1830 Scotch was a local drink. By 1910 it was sold all over the world, and almost all of it was blended. A blend is malt whisky and grain whisky mixed. The four whiskies tonight are a grain whisky, a malt whisky and two blends.

The painting on the slide is The Illicit Highland Whisky Still by Edwin Landseer, from 1829. The copper pot still is behind the girl on the right.

## [SLIDE 2] Scotch in 1830: malt, sold by the cask

Around 1830 Scotch was malt whisky. It was made from malted barley in a pot still. A lot of it had been made illegally until a few years before. In 1823 a new law cut the tax and brought in a £10 licence, and many Highland distillers took one. George Smith at Glenlivet got his in 1824.

Distilleries bottled very little whisky. They sold it by the cask to merchants, innkeepers and grocers, who sold it on by the jar or the glass. Every cask was different, even from the same distillery. The whisky you bought from your grocer one month could taste quite different the next.

## [SLIDE 3] 1830: Aeneas Coffey patents a continuous still

Aeneas Coffey was an Irishman. He had been Inspector-General of Excise in Ireland, so he had spent years catching illegal distillers. He left that job in 1824 and started distilling in Dublin. In 1830 he patented a still with two tall columns.

It's in the diagram. Steam goes in at the bottom of the left column, which is called the analyser. The wash is a kind of rough beer. It is warmed in pipes that run through the right column, then let in at the top of the analyser. It falls through a series of plates with holes in them, and the rising steam takes the alcohol out of it. The vapour then crosses to the right column, the rectifier, where it gets stronger. The spirit is drawn off near the top.

A pot still is filled, boiled and emptied, one batch at a time. A Coffey still keeps running as long as you keep feeding it. It makes a much stronger and lighter spirit, and it can use cheaper grains than malted barley.

Coffey wasn't the first. Robert Stein, from a Lowland distilling family, had patented a continuous still a few years earlier, and his relatives the Haigs used it at Cameronbridge in Fife. Coffey's worked better and replaced it. The big Dublin distillers didn't want Coffey's still. The first Scottish distillery to put one in was Grange, in 1834.

## [SLIDE 4] Pour 1 of 4: Haig Club Clubman

_Pour glass 1 now._

This is what that kind of still makes. It's Haig Club Clubman, a single grain whisky from Cameronbridge in Fife. That's the Haig distillery that ran Stein's still. Today it uses column stills. It's 40%.

Single grain means it comes from one distillery, and it can be made from grains other than malted barley. Most grain whisky goes into blends, so you don't often get to taste it by itself.

Drink it neat. It's light and sweet.

## [SLIDE 5] Malt whisky and grain whisky

By the 1840s Scotland was making two different drinks, and both were called whisky.

Malt whisky was made the old way, from malted barley, in a pot still, in small batches. It had a lot of flavour and it varied a lot. Grain whisky was cheap and there was plenty of it. It was so light that it didn't sell well by itself as whisky. The big Lowland distillers had been sending their spirit to London since the 1770s, to be made into gin.

## [SLIDE 6] Andrew Usher vats Glenlivet, 1853

Andrew Usher of Edinburgh usually gets the credit for mixing malt whisky and grain whisky. The Ushers were the Edinburgh agents for Smith's Glenlivet. In 1853 the law changed to let a merchant mix whiskies from the same distillery before paying the tax on them. That year Usher started selling Old Vatted Glenlivet. Vatting meant mixing young and old casks from one distillery, so that every bottle tasted about the same.

Now a customer could ask for a whisky by name and get the same thing twice. Later, when the law allowed it, Usher's firm started mixing grain whisky into the malt. People call him the father of blending for that. Other merchants were doing much the same at the time.

## [SLIDE 7] Pour 2 of 4: The Glenlivet 12 Year Old

_Pour glass 2 now._

This is The Glenlivet, the malt Usher was vatting. It's the 12 Year Old, made by Chivas Brothers, at 40%. In the US the label may say "12 Year Old Double Oak".

A single malt is still a mix of casks. This bottle is many casks of Glenlivet, all at least twelve years old, mixed so that it tastes the same every year. That's what Usher did.

_Ask: "Taste it next to the grain whisky. Which one feels heavier in your mouth?"_

You should get more fruit and more of the grain's own taste in the malt. The blenders had both of these to work with.

## [SLIDE 8] 1860: the Spirits Act

In 1860 the tax law changed again. The Spirits Act was passed in August that year, when Gladstone was Chancellor of the Exchequer. It let merchants mix whiskies from different distilleries, malt and grain together, before the tax was paid. That's what "in bond" means on the slide.

Tax was a large part of the price of whisky. Before 1860 a merchant had to pay it before he blended, and then his money sat in vats until the whisky sold. After 1860 he could blend a lot of whisky and pay the tax when he sold it.

The Act also meant a blender could mix cheap grain whisky with expensive malt and sell the bottle as whisky. For the next hundred years, most of the money in Scotch went to the firms that owned the blends and the brand names.

## [SLIDE 9] The first big blenders were grocers

Grocers and wine merchants were already buying whisky by the cask. Four of the first big blending firms were started by them.

John Walker opened a grocery in Kilmarnock in 1820 and sold whisky along with everything else. His son Alexander built up the whisky side. James Chivas joined a grocery business on King Street in Aberdeen around 1838. The firm says it held a royal warrant to supply Queen Victoria from 1843. His brother John joined him in 1857. John Dewar set up as a wine and spirit merchant on the High Street in Perth in 1846. George Ballantine opened a grocer's in Edinburgh in 1827, and later a bigger shop in Glasgow.

People often say grocers were good at blending whisky because they already blended tea. None of the four is on record saying so. They did have shops, regular customers, and credit with the distillers.

## [SLIDE 10] Pour 3 of 4: Johnnie Walker Black Label

_Pour glass 3 now._

This is Johnnie Walker Black Label. It's a blended Scotch, twelve years old, made by Diageo, at 40%.

The Walkers sold their blends as Old Highland Whisky, and customers asked for them by the colour of the label. In 1909 the firm renamed them Johnnie Walker. The one called Extra Special Old Highland became Black Label. The striding man on the label was drawn by a cartoonist, Tom Browne, the year before. The square bottle and the slanted label are older than that. The company dates the bottle to 1860.

This is malt and grain together. Diageo says some of the malt comes from the islands.

_Ask: "Can you find the fruit from the Glenlivet in this? The lightness of the Clubman? Any smoke?"_

## [SLIDE 11] 1860s: phylloxera reaches the vineyards of France

In the 1860s vines in the south of France started to wither and die. The cause was phylloxera, a tiny insect that feeds on the roots. It came from North America, probably on vines brought over for study. French scientists identified it in 1868. American vines could resist it and European vines couldn't.

It spread across France over the next twenty years. It reached the first Cognac vineyards in 1872. By the 1880s most of the vineyards there had been destroyed. The cure was to graft French vines onto American roots. Replanting in Cognac didn't really get going until the 1890s.

The cartoon is from Punch, in September 1890. It's called "The Phylloxera, a true gourmet".

## [SLIDE 12] From brandy and soda to whisky and soda

In Victorian England a gentleman drank brandy, often with soda. Cognac got scarce and expensive, and people looked for something else. The blenders had a light Scotch that tasted the same every time and could be drunk the same way, with soda.

People often say phylloxera made Scotch popular in England. But Britain already drank far more whisky than brandy in 1860, before the vines started dying. Scotch did boom in the 1880s and 1890s, and those were the years when brandy was hardest to get.

The distillers built new distilleries to keep up. Glenfiddich made its first spirit on Christmas Day 1887. Dewar's opened Aberfeldy in 1898 to make malt for its blend.

## [SLIDE 13] The salesmen of the 1890s

Two Scots in London did the most to sell blended Scotch in the 1890s.

Tommy Dewar was John Dewar's younger son. He went to London in 1885, aged 21, to sell the family's whisky, and he was good at publicity. Then he went round the world to find buyers abroad. He wrote a book about the trip, A Ramble Round the Globe, which came out in 1894.

James Buchanan was born in Canada and brought up in Glasgow. He came to London in 1879 as an agent for a Leith whisky firm and started his own business in 1884. From 1885 he supplied whisky to the House of Commons, and he made sure people knew it. His bottle had a black and white label. People started asking for "Black & White", and from 1904 that was its name.

Dewar became Lord Dewar in 1919, and Buchanan became Lord Woolavington in 1922. The caricature of Buchanan is from Vanity Fair in 1907. It's titled "Whisky and Horses", because he owned racehorses.

## [SLIDE 14] Pour 4 of 4: Dewar's White Label

_Pour glass 4 now. Have the soda water, ice and tall glasses ready._

This is Dewar's White Label, made by John Dewar and Sons. Bacardi owns the firm now. It's a blended Scotch at 40%. The company says its first master blender, A. J. Cameron, made the White Label blend in 1899.

Try this one two ways. Have a little neat first. Then put some in a tall glass with ice and soda water. A lot of it was drunk that way in London in the 1890s.

_Ask: "Go back to the Clubman. Can you pick out the grain whisky in the Dewar's now?"_

## [SLIDE 15] 1898: Pattisons of Leith fails

The boom ended in 1898. Robert and Walter Pattison ran a blending firm in Leith. In the mid-1890s they grew it fast, sold shares in it, borrowed a lot and spent a lot on publicity. There's a story that they gave away hundreds of parrots trained to say "Drink Pattisons' Whisky". Nearly every account of the firm repeats it, but it may just be a story.

The firm stopped paying its debts in December 1898. The brothers were arrested in April 1901 and tried in Edinburgh that July. Both were found guilty of fraud. Robert got eighteen months and Walter got nine.

Several firms that had dealt with the Pattisons went down with them. Lenders got wary of the whisky trade. The industry had far more whisky than it could sell, and the building of new distilleries stopped.

## [SLIDE 16] 1908–09: a Royal Commission decides what whisky is

The malt distillers had never liked the blenders using the word whisky for a drink that was mostly grain spirit from a Coffey still. In 1905 Islington Borough Council in London had whisky bought from local pubs and merchants, and took the sellers to court. The council said it wasn't whisky, because it had grain spirit in it. In February 1906 the magistrate agreed with the council. The sellers appealed, and the appeal ended without a decision.

So in February 1908 the government set up a Royal Commission to answer the question "What is whisky?" Its chairman was Lord James of Hereford. That's him on the slide, in a Vanity Fair caricature from 1874, when he was Sir Henry James.

The Commission reported in 1909, and it sided with the blenders. It said whisky is a spirit made from grain, where malt is used to turn the starch into sugar. The kind of still didn't matter. So grain whisky was whisky.

The Commission didn't set a minimum age. That came in 1915, when a new law required three years in bond. It's still three years today.

## [SLIDE 17] Reading a Scotch label

The rules for Scotch labels were set in 2009, and they still use the 1909 definition.

Single malt comes from one distillery and is made only from malted barley, in pot stills. Single grain comes from one distillery but can use other grains and column stills, like our first pour. Blended malt is single malts from more than one distillery, with no grain whisky. Blended Scotch is malt and grain whisky mixed, like the last two pours. An age statement gives the age of the youngest whisky in the bottle.

All Scotch has to be aged in oak casks in Scotland for at least three years. Caramel colouring is allowed in all of them. No other colouring is.
