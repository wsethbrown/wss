## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] Peated Scotch

Thanks for coming. "Peat reek" is the old Scots phrase for peat smoke, the smell of a house with a peat fire in it, and tonight is about how that smell ended up in whisky and why some distilleries kept it when most dropped it.

We'll follow four distilleries, one per glass. A mainland distillery that gave up peat in the nineteenth century, an Islay distillery that never did, an Islay distillery that was rebuilt in the 1970s to feed the blending trade, and one that was closed for most of the 1980s and restarted in 1997 under new owners.

## [SLIDE 2] Peat, the fuel of a country with few trees

Peat is plant material that never finished rotting. In wet, cold, acid ground, mostly sphagnum moss along with heather and sedges, the dead plants sit under water where there isn't enough oxygen for them to break down, and they pile up. NatureScot puts the average rate of growth at about a millimetre a year, so a bank of peat as tall as you are has taken well over a thousand years to build, and some of Scotland's deepest bogs are around ten thousand years old.

In much of the Highlands and nearly all of the islands there were very few trees, so for centuries peat was what people burned. It was cut from the face of a bank with a narrow spade called a tusker, *tairsgeir* in Gaelic, usually by two people, one cutting and one throwing the slabs out onto the moss to dry. When they were dry enough they were stacked and carried home. A freshly cut peat is mostly water, and it has to lose most of that before it will burn.

## [SLIDE 3] Drying malt over a peat fire

To make malt whisky you first malt the barley. You soak the grain, spread it out until it starts to sprout, and then dry it to stop it growing. The drying happens in a kiln, a room with a perforated floor and a fire underneath, and the fire was whatever fuel was cheap locally. Across the Highlands and islands, that was peat.

The smoke gets into the malt most easily while the grain is still damp. Peat smoke carries a family of chemicals called phenols, and the wet surface of the sprouted barley picks them up. Once the grain has dried out it takes up very little more. So when maltsters peat a batch on purpose now, they burn the peat at the start of the kilning, while the barley is wet, and finish the drying with hot air. Even then, only part of what it takes up survives the kiln.

Peat smoulders and gives off a lot of smoke for the heat it produces, so a distiller in 1800 who dried his malt over it had smoke in his whisky whether he wanted it or not.

## [SLIDE 4] Glenmorangie kept burning peat long after the railway came

Our first distillery is Glenmorangie, in Tain, on the Dornoch Firth north of Inverness. William Matheson, a farmer, applied for a licence there in 1843, on a farm where there had been a brewery since the eighteenth century, and the first spirit ran in 1849.

The railway reached Tain on the first of June 1864. You'll often hear that the railways ended peat on the mainland by bringing in coal and coke. They did make coke available, and some distilleries began mixing it into their kilns, but the change was slow. When the writer Alfred Barnard toured Britain's distilleries in the mid-1880s, twenty years after the trains reached Tain, he recorded that the only fuel used at Glenmorangie was peat, and one reading of his survey is that nearly every Speyside distillery was still drying its malt over peat as well. A history of Glenmorangie says it used peat in its kilns until about 1940.

Meanwhile the blenders were growing. In 1860 the law began to allow whisky to be blended in bonded warehouses before the duty was paid, and blended Scotch, a mix of malt whisky and lighter grain whisky, became what most of the world bought.

The bigger break came after the Second World War. As output rose in the 1960s, distilleries closed their floor maltings and bought from large mechanical maltings, and Scottish Malt Distillers, the Distillers Company's malt arm, stopped using lightly peated malt at all its distilleries except those on Islay and Skye. A 2019 paper in the Journal of the Institute of Brewing describes that as the end of the lightly peated mainland style, with blenders using more Islay malt to put the smoke back.

Glenmorangie followed the same road. It closed its own maltings in 1977 (one source says 1980) and now buys malt specified at no more than 2 parts per million of phenols. The trade calls that unpeated.

## [SLIDE 5] Pour 1 of 4: Glenmorangie The Original

So let's start with a mainland whisky made from unpeated malt. This is Glenmorangie The Original from Tain. It was a ten-year-old for decades and became a twelve-year-old in 2024.

Treat it as the zero point for the evening. Smell it, taste it, and notice that there's no smoke at all, or so little you'd need to be told. Remember that when Barnard visited, this distillery was burning nothing but peat.

Keep a little in the glass if you can, for comparison with the last pour.

## [SLIDE 6] Islay kept burning peat

Now to Islay, the southernmost island of the Inner Hebrides, off the west coast. Islay distilleries carried on burning peat long after the mainland stopped, and the usual explanations are practical ones.

The island has a great deal of peat, much of it close to the distilleries, and it had no railway. Coal had to come in by boat, which made it expensive. And once the blending trade had grown, the blenders had a use for Islay's smoky malts. A small amount of a heavily peated malt in a blend gives it depth, and companies were glad to buy it.

There's also a romantic story that the island's peat tastes of the sea because of salt spray and seaweed in it. Researchers have found real differences between peats from different places, but how much of that survives into the whisky is still argued over, so I'd take the seaweed story as a story.

Bowmore, in the middle of the island on Loch Indaal, is first recorded in 1779, and it calls itself the oldest working distillery on Islay.

## [SLIDE 7] Bowmore still turns its own malt

Bowmore is one of a handful of Scotch distilleries that still malt some of their own barley the old way, on a floor. Laphroaig, along the south coast, is another.

The barley is soaked, then spread across the floor and turned by hand with wooden shovels while it sprouts. Then it goes to the kiln, where Islay peat is burned underneath it for the first part of the drying.

Floor malting is slow and needs a lot of hands, so even Bowmore makes only part of the malt it uses. Published estimates run from about a quarter to nearly half. The rest is bought from a commercial maltster, made to the same smoke level, which is usually given as about 25 parts per million.

## [SLIDE 8] Pour 2 of 4: Bowmore 12

Second pour, Bowmore 12.

Bowmore is usually described as medium peated. You should find the smoke is clearly there now but it isn't the first thing you notice. On a lot of people's palates it reads as a softer, sweeter smoke than the two that follow.

Compare it with the Glenmorangie. Both are twelve years old and bottled at 40 percent, so the smoke is the most obvious difference, though the stills and the casks differ as well.

## [SLIDE 9] The ppm figure measures the malt

Before the next part of the story, a word about the number people quote for peat. The letters ppm stand for parts per million of phenols, and it's a measurement of the malt, taken in a laboratory before anything is distilled. A maltster will sell you barley at whatever level you specify.

The number in your glass is lower, and the bottle's figure doesn't tell you by how much. A good share of the phenols stay behind in the still. The heavier phenols come over late in the distillation, so a stillman who keeps collecting spirit for longer catches more of them, and a distillery that cuts early can make a lighter spirit from the same malt. Then years in a cask soften the smoke further, which is why a young peated whisky can taste smokier than an older one from the same distillery.

For scale, Ardbeg's malt is usually quoted at around 50 to 55 ppm and its new spirit at somewhere between 17 and 24.

## [SLIDE 10] 1973: the Port Ellen maltings open on Islay

In 1973 the Distillers Company Limited, the giant of the industry then and the forerunner of today's Diageo, opened a large drum maltings beside its Port Ellen distillery on the south coast of the island. It was built to supply heavily peated malt to the company's own Islay distilleries. Sources differ on whether to date it to 1973, when it was commissioned, or 1974, when full production began.

Caol Ila and Lagavulin take much or all of their malt from there; one account says all, another says over half for Caol Ila. Over the years most of the other distilleries on the island have bought some of their malt there too, peated and unpeated. That means a lot of the smoke in Islay whisky starts in one building, with the differences coming from each distillery's specification and its stills.

The maltings are still running today.

## [SLIDE 11] Caol Ila, rebuilt 1972–74

Caol Ila sits on the north-east coast of Islay, near Port Askaig, looking across the Sound of Islay to the island of Jura. It was founded in 1846 by Hector Henderson, a Glasgow businessman. DCL owned it by the 1970s, and between 1972 and 1974 they knocked most of it down and rebuilt it, going from two stills to six.

They didn't rebuild it to sell Caol Ila under its own name. Most of it went into blends, Johnnie Walker among them, and most of it still does.

## [SLIDE 12] Pour 3 of 4: Caol Ila 12

Third pour, Caol Ila 12, from malt made at Port Ellen.

What people often notice is that the smoke is cleaner and drier, with less sweetness behind it. That's probably more about the stills and the cut than about the malt.

The malt is usually given at about 35 parts per million, a little above Bowmore's. If you drink Johnnie Walker, see whether you can recognise anything of this in it.

## [SLIDE 13] Closures on Islay, 1981–1996

Back to the story. In the early 1980s the industry was in trouble. Production through the 1960s and early 1970s had run far ahead of demand, and the warehouses were full; the trade called it the whisky loch. In 1983 DCL closed eleven of its forty-five malt distilleries, and it closed more in 1985.

Port Ellen distillery was one of them. It shut in 1983, and the maltings next door carried on working.

Ardbeg, a few miles east along the south coast, had already stopped. Its owners, Hiram Walker, halted production in March 1981, and its own floor maltings, which had made Ardbeg's malt until then, closed for good. It started again at a low level in 1989 and stopped again late in 1996, and its owners by then, Allied Domecq, put it up for sale.

## [SLIDE 14] 1997: Glenmorangie buys Ardbeg

In 1997 Glenmorangie bought Ardbeg, the distillery and its stock of whisky, for £7 million. So the company from Tain, which made its own whisky from unpeated malt, also owned one of the most heavily peated distilleries in Scotland.

They restarted production that year. The stock they bought had gaps in it, because of the years when the distillery had been closed or barely working, and that shaped what Ardbeg could release for the next decade. Ardbeg dates itself to 1815, when John MacDougall took out a licence, though whisky was probably made there before that.

Ardbeg Ten became the main bottling. Glenmorangie, and Ardbeg with it, was bought by the French luxury group LVMH at the end of 2004, and the two are still sister distilleries today.

## [SLIDE 15] Peat in the malt, in ppm

Around the same time drinkers started looking for smoke. By the late 1990s heavily peated Islay malts had gone from the bit you added to a blend to something people sought out on their own.

Distilleries began to compete on peat levels. Bruichladdich, on the other side of Loch Indaal from Bowmore, was reopened in 2001 by a group of investors led by Mark Reynier and Simon Coughlin, and in 2008 it released the first Octomore, edition 01.1, from malt peated to 131 ppm. The next year Ardbeg released Supernova, from malt peated to just over 100 ppm. Later Octomores went much higher: edition 08.3, released in 2017, was given as 309 ppm.

The figures for the four distilleries we're tasting are the ones usually published or reported, and they change between batches. Ardbeg's in particular comes from reviewers and retailers, who give anything from 42 to 70. On this chart the four whiskies we're drinking tonight sit well below the extreme bottlings.

## [SLIDE 16] Pour 4 of 4: Ardbeg Ten

Last pour, Ardbeg Ten, the bottling that came out of the 1997 restart.

The malt spec is usually given at around 55 ppm, the highest of tonight's four, and since the restart all of it has come from Port Ellen. Taste it, then go back to the Glenmorangie you kept. The two distilleries have the same owner, and their whiskies are a fair way apart in age and strength, so not every difference you notice is the peat.

Ardbeg is also bottled at 46 percent and isn't chill-filtered, so a few drops of water will change it a fair amount. Try it both ways. When you've finished, I'll ask who'd buy which bottle for the house.

## [SLIDE 17] Reading peat on a Scotch label

To finish, a few things to remember when you're looking at a bottle.

"Peated", "heavily peated" and "smoky" have no legal definition for Scotch. They're the producer's description.

A ppm figure, if you find one, is a measurement of the malt before distillation. The whisky in the bottle will be lower, and you can't tell how much lower from the label.

Region is a weak guide. Several Islay distilleries make unpeated whisky, the Port Ellen maltings sell unpeated malt as well as peated, and there are peated whiskies made on the mainland.

Age matters. Smoke fades in the cask, so a younger whisky from a given distillery usually tastes smokier than an older one.
