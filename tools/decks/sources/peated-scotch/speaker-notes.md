## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] Peated Scotch

Thanks for coming. Tonight is about peat smoke in Scotch whisky. The old Scots name for it is "peat reek". We'll look at how the smoke got into whisky, and why some distilleries kept it when most gave it up.

There are four distilleries and four glasses. The first is on the mainland and uses almost no peat now. The second is on Islay and never stopped using it. The third is on Islay too. It was rebuilt in the 1970s to make whisky for blends. The fourth was closed for most of the 1980s and started again in 1997 with new owners.

## [SLIDE 2] Peat, the fuel of a country with few trees

Peat is dead plants that never finished rotting. It forms in wet, cold, acid ground. The plants are mostly moss, with some heather. They sit under water where there isn't enough oxygen for them to break down, so they pile up. Peat grows about a millimetre a year. A bank of peat as tall as you are took well over a thousand years to form. Some of Scotland's deepest bogs are about ten thousand years old.

Much of the Highlands and nearly all of the islands had very few trees. For centuries people there burned peat. They cut it from the face of a bank with a narrow spade called a tusker. Usually two people worked together. One cut the slabs and the other threw them out onto the ground to dry. When the slabs were dry enough, they were stacked and carried home. Fresh peat is mostly water, and it has to lose most of that water before it will burn.

## [SLIDE 3] Drying malt over a peat fire

To make malt whisky you first have to malt the barley. You soak the grain and spread it out until it starts to sprout. Then you dry it to stop it growing. The drying is done in a kiln, which is a room with holes in the floor and a fire underneath. Distillers burned whatever fuel was cheap where they lived. In the Highlands and islands that was peat.

Peat smoke has chemicals in it called phenols. The barley takes them up while it is still damp. Once the grain is dry it takes up very little more. So today, a maltster who wants smoky malt burns the peat at the start, while the barley is wet, and finishes the drying with hot air. Even then, only some of the smoke stays in the malt.

Peat gives off a lot of smoke for the heat it makes. A distiller in 1800 who dried his malt over peat got smoke in his whisky whether he wanted it or not.

## [SLIDE 4] Glenmorangie kept burning peat long after the railway came

The first distillery is Glenmorangie. It's in Tain, on the Dornoch Firth, north of Inverness. A farmer called William Matheson applied for a licence there in 1843. The farm had had a brewery on it since the 1700s. The distillery made its first spirit in 1849.

The railway reached Tain in June 1864. People often say the railways ended peat on the mainland, because they brought in coal and coke. Some distilleries did start mixing coke into their kilns, but the change was slow. A writer called Alfred Barnard toured Britain's distilleries in the mid-1880s, twenty years after the trains reached Tain. He wrote that the only fuel used at Glenmorangie was peat. One account says the distillery went on using peat until about 1940.

In those same years blended Scotch took over. A blend is malt whisky mixed with lighter grain whisky. From 1860 the law made blending easier, and blends became the Scotch most of the world bought.

The bigger change came after the Second World War. In the 1960s distilleries were making more whisky. They closed their own maltings and bought malt from large malting plants. The largest company, the Distillers Company, stopped using lightly peated malt at all its distilleries except the ones on Islay and Skye. That was the end of the lightly smoky mainland style. Blenders who wanted smoke used more Islay malt.

Glenmorangie did the same. It closed its own maltings in about 1977. Now it buys malt with no more than 2 parts per million of phenols. In the whisky business that counts as unpeated.

## [SLIDE 6] Islay kept burning peat

Islay is the most southerly island of the Inner Hebrides, off the west coast of Scotland. Its distilleries went on burning peat long after the mainland stopped. There were practical reasons for that.

The island has a lot of peat, and much of it is close to the distilleries. It had no railway, so coal had to come by boat, and that made it expensive. The blenders also wanted Islay's smoky malts. A small amount of a very smoky malt adds a lot of flavour to a blend, and they were glad to buy it.

You'll also hear that Islay peat tastes of the sea, because of salt spray and seaweed in it. Peat from different places is different. Nobody has shown how much of that you can taste in the whisky. Treat the seaweed as a story.

Bowmore is in the middle of the island, on Loch Indaal. It is first recorded in 1779, and it calls itself the oldest working distillery on Islay.

## [SLIDE 7] Bowmore still turns its own malt

Bowmore is one of a few Scotch distilleries that still malt some of their own barley the old way, on a floor. Laphroaig, on the south coast, is another.

The barley is soaked and spread across the floor. People turn it by hand with wooden shovels while it sprouts. Then it goes to the kiln, and Islay peat is burned under it for the first part of the drying.

Malting on a floor is slow and takes a lot of people. Bowmore makes only part of its malt this way, somewhere between a quarter and a half. It buys the rest from a malting company, made to the same level of smoke. That level is usually given as about 25 parts per million.

## [SLIDE 8] Pour 2 of 4: Bowmore 12

_Pour glass 2 now._

This is Bowmore 12.

Bowmore is usually described as medium peated. You can taste the smoke now, but it isn't the first thing you notice. A lot of people find it softer and sweeter than the smoke in the next two glasses.

Taste it next to the Glenmorangie. Both are twelve years old and both are 40 percent. The smoke is the biggest difference between them. The stills and the casks are different too.

_Ask: "Where do you notice the smoke first, on the nose or after you swallow?"_

## [SLIDE 9] The ppm figure measures the malt

People quote a number for peat, in ppm. That stands for parts per million of phenols. It measures the malt, and it is tested in a laboratory before any whisky is made. A maltster will make malt at whatever level the distillery asks for.

The whisky in your glass has less than that number, and the bottle doesn't tell you how much less. A good share of the phenols stay behind in the still. The heavier ones come through late in the run. A distiller who keeps collecting spirit for longer gets more of them. A distiller who stops early gets a lighter spirit from the same malt. Years in a cask take away more smoke. That's why a young peated whisky can taste smokier than an older one from the same distillery.

Ardbeg is an example. Its malt is usually quoted at about 50 to 55 ppm. Its new spirit is somewhere between 17 and 24.

## [SLIDE 10] 1973: the Port Ellen maltings open on Islay

In 1973 the Distillers Company opened a large maltings on Islay. The Distillers Company was the biggest firm in Scotch at the time, and it later became part of Diageo. The maltings stand beside the company's Port Ellen distillery, on the south coast. They were built to make heavily peated malt for the company's own Islay distilleries.

Caol Ila and Lagavulin get most or all of their malt from there. Over the years most of the other distilleries on the island have bought some malt there too, both peated and unpeated. So a lot of the smoke in Islay whisky comes from one building. The whiskies differ because each distillery orders its own level of smoke and has its own stills.

The maltings are still running today.

## [SLIDE 11] Caol Ila, rebuilt 1972–74

Caol Ila is on the north-east coast of Islay, near Port Askaig. It looks across the water to the island of Jura. A Glasgow businessman, Hector Henderson, founded it in 1846. By the 1970s the Distillers Company owned it. Between 1972 and 1974 the company knocked most of it down and rebuilt it. It went from two stills to six.

The company didn't rebuild it to sell Caol Ila under its own name. Most of the whisky went into blends, including Johnnie Walker. Most of it still does.

People often find the smoke cleaner and drier than the Bowmore, with less sweetness. That probably has more to do with the stills than with the malt.

The malt is usually given as about 35 parts per million, a little more than Bowmore's.

## [SLIDE 13] Closures on Islay, 1981–1996

In the early 1980s the whisky industry was in trouble. In the 1960s and early 1970s it had made far more whisky than people were buying, and the warehouses were full. In 1983 the Distillers Company closed eleven of its forty-five malt distilleries. It closed more in 1985.

Port Ellen distillery was one of the eleven. It shut in 1983. The maltings next door kept working.

Ardbeg is a few miles east of Port Ellen on the same coast. It had already stopped. Its owner, Hiram Walker, stopped production in March 1981. Ardbeg had made its own malt until then, and its maltings closed for good. The distillery started again at a low level in 1989 and stopped again late in 1996. By then the owner was Allied Domecq, and it put Ardbeg up for sale.

## [SLIDE 14] 1997: Glenmorangie buys Ardbeg

In 1997 Glenmorangie bought Ardbeg for £7 million. That price included the distillery and its stock of whisky. Glenmorangie made its own whisky from unpeated malt. Now it also owned one of the most heavily peated distilleries in Scotland.

It started Ardbeg up again that year. The stock had gaps in it, from the years when the distillery was closed or hardly working. That limited what Ardbeg could release for the next ten years. Ardbeg Ten became the main bottle.

Ardbeg gives its own founding date as 1815, when John MacDougall took out a licence. Whisky was probably made there before that.

At the end of 2004 the French luxury group LVMH bought Glenmorangie, and Ardbeg with it. The same company still owns both.

## [SLIDE 15] Peat in the malt, in ppm

Around the same time, drinkers started looking for smoke. Heavily peated Islay malt used to be something you added to a blend. By the late 1990s people were buying it to drink by itself.

Distilleries started to compete on how much peat they used. Bruichladdich is on the other side of Loch Indaal from Bowmore. A group of investors reopened it in 2001. In 2008 it released the first Octomore, made from malt peated to 131 ppm. The next year Ardbeg released Supernova, from malt at just over 100 ppm. Later Octomores went much higher. One released in 2017 was given as 309 ppm.

The numbers for tonight's four are the ones usually published, and they change from batch to batch. Ardbeg's is the least certain. You'll see anything from 42 to 70. All four are a long way below the bottles at the bottom of the chart.

## [SLIDE 16] Pour 4 of 4: Ardbeg Ten

_Pour glass 4 now._

This is Ardbeg Ten, the main bottle since the distillery restarted in 1997.

The malt is usually given as about 55 ppm, the highest of tonight's four. Since the restart all of it has come from Port Ellen.

Taste it, then go back to the Glenmorangie you kept. The two distilleries have the same owner. The two whiskies are different ages and strengths, so the peat isn't the only difference between them.

Ardbeg is bottled at 46 percent and isn't chill-filtered. A few drops of water will change it quite a lot. Try it both ways.

_Ask: "Which of the four would you buy a bottle of?"_

## [SLIDE 17] Reading peat on a Scotch label

Here are a few things to remember when you look at a bottle.

"Peated", "heavily peated" and "smoky" have no legal meaning for Scotch. They are the producer's own description.

If the label gives a ppm figure, it was measured in the malt. The whisky in the bottle has less, and the label doesn't tell you how much less.

The region doesn't tell you much. Several Islay distilleries make unpeated whisky. The Port Ellen maltings sell unpeated malt as well as peated. There are peated whiskies made on the mainland.

Age matters. Smoke fades in the cask, so a younger whisky usually tastes smokier than an older one from the same distillery.
