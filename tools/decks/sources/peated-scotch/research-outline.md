# Peated Scotch

Research outline: Peat smoke in Scotch whisky

Peat smoke in Scotch whisky. A guide for anyone who wants to research the subject and build a talk of their own.

---

**Sources.** The facts in this deck were checked in November 2026 against the sources listed under each module: distillery and owner histories, whisky reference works and magazines, railway and archaeological records. What could not be confirmed is listed under "Not confirmed" near the end; the deck leaves it off the slides or says in the script that it is uncertain.

## Module 1. What peat is, and how it was cut

**The question.** What is peat, how fast does it form, and how did people in the Highlands and islands get it out of the ground?

### Nailed down

- Peat is partly decayed plant matter that builds up in waterlogged, acid, low-oxygen ground.
- NatureScot gives an average accumulation rate of about 1 mm a year; some of Scotland's deepest bogs are around 10,000 years old. A study of 28 European peatlands found a mean of about 1.2 mm a year with a wide range (0.05–4.5 mm).
- The traditional cutting spade is the tusker (Gaelic _tairsgeir_). Cutting was a two-person job, a cutter and a thrower who cast the slabs onto the moss to dry.

### Primary documents

- The _Statistical Account of Scotland_ (1790s) and _New Statistical Account_ (1830s–40s), parish by parish, which describe fuel and peat-cutting (free, online).
- NatureScot and James Hutton Institute publications on Scottish peatlands.

### Traps

- Growth rates vary widely by site and depth. Give an average, not a precise rate.
- The seasonal calendar (cut in spring, dry over summer) is widely repeated but was not confirmed; the deck does not give seasons.

### Sources

- NatureScot / Peatland ACTION, "Carbon facts and figures" leaflet (2019): https://nnr.nature.scot/sites/default/files/2019-04/Peatland%20Action%20-%20COMMS%20-%20Materials%20-%20LEAFLET%20-%20Carbon%20Facts%20and%20Figures%20leaflet%20screen%20-%20with%20full%20reference%20list%20-%202019%20UPDATE.pdf

## Module 2. Kilning and phenols

**The question.** How does peat smoke get into malt, and why only at a certain stage?

### Nailed down

- Malting is steeping, germination and kilning; the kiln dries the green malt to stop germination.
- Peat is burned at the start of kilning, before the "break", because green malt takes up phenols while its surface is wet. Uptake falls off as the grain dries; sources put the threshold variously between about 15% and 25% moisture.
- Part of the absorbed phenol is lost during kilning, so only some of it remains in the finished malt.

### Primary documents

- Maltsters' technical literature (for example the Institute of Brewing & Distilling's publications on malting).

### Traps

- Do not quote a single moisture cut-off; sources disagree.
- Do not claim that every Scotch was smoky before 1850. Say "across the Highlands and islands".

### Sources

- Difford's Guide, "Single malt Scotch whisky production 2: malting": https://www.diffordsguide.com/g/1168/single-malt-scotch-whisky-production/malting
- ScienceDirect Topics, Kilns: https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/kilns
- scotchwhisky.com, "Phenol fables: peat's secrets uncovered": https://scotchwhisky.com/magazine/features/9293/phenol-fables-peat-s-secrets-uncovered/

## Module 3. Glenmorangie, railways and the blenders

**The question.** Why did mainland distilleries stop peating, when, and where does Glenmorangie fit?

### Nailed down

- William Matheson, a farmer, founded Glenmorangie at Tain in 1843 (licence application that year); production began in November 1849. A brewery is recorded on the site in the eighteenth century.
- The railway reached Tain on 1 June 1864, opened by the Inverness and Aberdeen Junction Railway (which had absorbed the Inverness and Ross-shire Railway in 1862).
- Railways made coal and coke available, and Lowland and Speyside distilleries were the first to use them; but the change was slow. Alfred Barnard, visiting in the mid-1880s, recorded peat as the only fuel used at Glenmorangie.
- In 1860 the law allowed spirits to be blended in bonded warehouses before duty was paid.
- The decisive change came after the Second World War: as output rose in the 1960s, floor maltings closed and distillers bought from centralised mechanical maltings. Scottish Malt Distillers (DCL's malt arm) stopped using lightly peated malt at all its distilleries except on Islay and Skye, ending the lightly peated mainland style; blenders used more Islay malt to compensate (Bathgate, J. Inst. Brewing, 2019).
- Glenmorangie closed its own floor maltings in 1977 (two sources; one gives 1980). It buys malt specified at a maximum of 2 ppm phenols (in practice often 1–1.4), described as unpeated.

### Primary documents

- Alfred Barnard, _The Whisky Distilleries of the United Kingdom_ (1887), which records kiln fuel distillery by distillery (free, scanned).
- G. N. Bathgate, "The influence of malt and wort processing on spirit character: the lost styles of Scotch malt whisky", _Journal of the Institute of Brewing_ 125 (2019), 200–213.
- The Excise on Spirits Act 1860 (statute text).

### Traps

- "The railway killed peat on the mainland" is too simple: Barnard found peat in mainland kilns twenty years after the railway reached Tain. The bigger break is the post-war closure of floor maltings.
- Several sources call Glenmorangie "very lightly peated"; the specification is 2 ppm or less. Say what the number is.
- Brand histories credit the 1860 Act with "creating" blended Scotch. Merchants blended before it.

## Module 4. Islay and Bowmore

**The question.** Why did Islay keep peating, and who still malts there?

### Nailed down

- Bowmore's first recorded mention is 1779; it is the oldest working distillery on Islay. The founder's name is given variously as John or David Simson (or Simpson).
- Bowmore keeps a working floor maltings and kiln and makes part of its own malt; published estimates run from about 25% to nearly half, the rest bought from a commercial maltster (Simpsons has been named) to the same specification.
- Bowmore's malt is usually given at about 25 ppm (reported range 18–30).
- Laphroaig also floor-malts part of its barley.

### Primary documents

- Barnard (1887) on Bowmore and the other Islay distilleries.
- Company histories, read as company histories.

### Traps

- The claim that Islay peat tastes of seaweed or iodine because of sea spray is a marketing staple. Call it a story.
- "Islay" is not a synonym for "peated": the Port Ellen maltings supply unpeated as well as peated malt.

## Module 5. ppm

**The question.** What does a ppm figure measure, and why is the glass different?

### Nailed down

- ppm is phenols in the malt, in parts per million, measured before distillation. It tells you how much peat went in, not what comes out.
- Many peat phenols have high boiling points and come off late in the spirit run; an earlier cut keeps fewer of them, a later cut more, and a good share is lost to the feints. Benromach makes its smokier Peat Smoke by moving the cut point alone.
- Ardbeg is quoted at about 50–55 ppm in the malt and 17–24 ppm in the new make.

### Primary documents

- Scotch Whisky Research Institute publications on phenol measurement.

### Traps

- Do not quote a fixed percentage lost in distillation.
- Published peating levels are widely repeated figures, not guarantees, and many come from retailers.

### Sources

- scotchwhisky.com, "The truth about peated whisky and phenols": https://scotchwhisky.com/magazine/ask-the-professor/16459/the-truth-about-peated-whisky-and-phenols/
- The Whiskey Wash, "Flavor development, episode 3: phenols": https://thewhiskeywash.com/lifestyle/flavor-development-episode-3-defining-sources-of-flavor-in-whisky-phenols/
- Torabhaig, Dr Mike Billett on peat: https://torabhaig.com/blogs/news/peat-exploring-phenols

## Module 6. Port Ellen maltings and Caol Ila

**The question.** How did one maltings come to supply most of Islay?

### Nailed down

- The Distillers Company Limited built drum maltings beside Port Ellen distillery, built 1972 and commissioned 1973 (one source gives 1974 for full production), to supply heavily peated malt to its Islay distilleries. The maltings now supply peated and unpeated malt to most of the island's distilleries.
- Caol Ila and Lagavulin take much or all of their malt from Port Ellen (one source: all; another: over half for Caol Ila).
- Caol Ila was founded in 1846 by Hector Henderson, a Glasgow businessman, near Port Askaig, and rebuilt by DCL in 1972–74, going from two stills to six. Most of its output goes to blends, Johnnie Walker among them.
- Caol Ila's malt is usually given at about 35 ppm (one source 35–38).

### Primary documents

- DCL annual reports, 1970s.
- Diageo's distillery histories.

### Traps

- DCL became part of Guinness in 1986 and then Diageo in 1997. Use the owner's name for the date you're talking about.

## Module 7. Closures and Ardbeg

**The question.** What happened in the 1980s, and how did Ardbeg come back?

### Nailed down

- Production in the 1960s and early 1970s ran far ahead of demand (the "whisky loch"). In 1983 DCL closed eleven of its 45 malt distilleries, including Port Ellen (May 1983) and Brora; Glenesk and Millburn followed in 1985. Counts differ slightly between sources (eleven or twelve in 1983).
- The Port Ellen maltings continued; the 1987 Concordat of Islay Distillers committed the island's distilleries to take malt from them.
- Ardbeg dates from 1815, when John MacDougall was licensed (whisky was probably made there from about 1794).
- Ardbeg, then owned by Hiram Walker, halted production in 1981 (25 March is given), and its own floor maltings closed for good. Production resumed at a low level in 1989 and continued until late 1996, when the owners, Allied Domecq, put the distillery up for sale. Since the 1997 restart its malt has come from Port Ellen (third-party sources; about 50–55 ppm).
- Glenmorangie plc bought Ardbeg, the distillery and stock, in 1997 for £7 million (reported in the US as $11.4 million).
- LVMH acquired Glenmorangie plc, and with it Ardbeg, on 28 December 2004.

### Primary documents

- DCL annual reports and press coverage of the 1983 and 1985 closures.
- Campbeltown Courier, 7 March 1997, on the sale.

### Traps

- Some sources call the 1981 shutdown mothballing; there were still people on site and casks in the warehouses.

## Module 8. The peated revival

**The question.** When did drinkers start seeking out heavy peat, and how high did the numbers go?

### Nailed down

- Bruichladdich was bought in December 2000 by investors led by Mark Reynier and Simon Coughlin and ran its first spirit on 29 May 2001.
- Bruichladdich released the first Octomore, edition 01.1, in 2008, at 131 ppm (distilled 2001 or 2002; sources differ).
- Ardbeg Supernova was released in 2009 (58.9% ABV) from malt peated to just over 100 ppm (101 is given). Some accounts mention an earlier Committee release.
- Octomore 08.3 was released in 2017 at 309 ppm (309.1 is given). One retailer calls it a 2018 release.
- Ardbeg's standard malt is usually given at about 55 ppm (reported figures run from 42 to 70).

### Primary documents

- Producers' release notes and press releases.

### Traps

- Very high malt ppm does not mean proportionally smokier whisky in the glass. See Module 5.

## The four pours

- **Pour 1. Glenmorangie The Original (12 years, 40%).** A mainland distillery that burned peat into the twentieth century and now uses malt at 2 ppm or less.
- **Pour 2. Bowmore 12 (40%).** An Islay distillery that never stopped; floor maltings.
- **Pour 3. Caol Ila 12 (43%).** Rebuilt by DCL for the blenders; malt largely from Port Ellen.
- **Pour 4. Ardbeg Ten (46%, non-chill-filtered).** Closed in the 1980s, bought and restarted by Glenmorangie in 1997.

Glenmorangie The Original became a 12-year-old in 2024 at the same 40% and price; older 10-year-old stock may still be on shelves. Bowmore 12 is 40% on US listings (K&L, ReserveBar). Caol Ila 12 is 43% on Diageo's US page. Prices vary too much by region to quote.

## Not confirmed

- The seasonal peat-cutting calendar (cut in spring, dried over summer). Searched peat-cutting sources; found the tool and method but not the seasons. Settle with the New Statistical Account or a Hebridean folk-life study. The deck gives no seasons.
- When exactly Scottish Malt Distillers stopped using lightly peated malt on the mainland. The Bathgate paper describes the decision but the search results did not give the year. Settle with the full text of the paper. The deck says "after the Second World War".
- Barnard on Speyside generally ("nearly every Speyside distillery still dried over peat"). One secondary source (Edinburgh Whisky Academy); a second search did not find it. Settle with Barnard's text. The script calls it "one reading of his survey".
- Glenmorangie peating until about 1940. One distillery history (whisky-news.com). Off the slides; the script attributes it.
- Ardbeg's malt specification and supplier from Ardbeg itself. Third-party sources agree on Port Ellen and about 50–55 ppm; no company statement found.
- Seaweed and sea-spray in Islay peat. Kept as a story in the script.
