# American Rye

Research outline: 1790s to now

1790s to now. A guide for anyone who wants to research the subject and build a talk of their own.

---

**Sources.** The facts in this deck were checked in November 2026 against the sources listed under each module. Most are the brand owners' and Mount Vernon's own published statements, court and settlement reporting, and the established whiskey press. What could not be confirmed is listed under "Not confirmed" near the end, with what would settle it.

## Module 1. Why rye, and why the mid-Atlantic

**The question.** Why did farmers in Pennsylvania and Maryland grow rye and distil it, rather than sell grain?

### Nailed down

- Rye is the most winter-hardy of the small grains and tolerates acid, infertile and sandy soils better than wheat, oats or barley.
- Pennsylvania German farmers grew rye: Benjamin Rush (1789) wrote that they sold their wheat and ate their rye or corn. Lewis Evans (1753) wrote that rye "thrives pretty well" in Pennsylvania.
- Western Pennsylvania was settled largely by Scots and Ulster Scots, who brought distilling with them.
- On the western Pennsylvania frontier hard currency was scarce and whiskey took the place of money in trade.
- Distilling turned surplus grain into a denser, more valuable, keeping product.
- The federal excise of 1791, payable in scarce hard money, provoked the Whiskey Rebellion (another deck covers it).

### Primary documents

- Tench Coxe, _A View of the United States of America_ (1794), and the 1810 census of manufactures, which counts stills and output by county.
- Benjamin Rush, _An Account of the Manners of the German Inhabitants of Pennsylvania_ (1789).

### Traps

- The often-quoted comparison of how much grain a pack horse could carry as grain versus as whiskey varies between sources. Do not use a figure without a period source.
- "Rye was used as money" is an approximation; whiskey was a common medium of exchange, not legal tender.

## Module 2. Washington's distillery at Mount Vernon

**The question.** What did Washington's distillery make, how much, and how do we know?

### Nailed down

- In 1797 Washington hired James Anderson, a Scottish farm manager, who urged him to distil beside the gristmill. Distilling began in February 1797 with two stills in the mill's cooperage.
- Washington then built a stone still house for five copper pot stills (total capacity 616 gallons), in operation by spring 1798.
- The 1799 ledgers record more than eighty sales totalling 10,942 gallons, valued at $7,674. Mount Vernon rounds this to "almost 11,000 gallons" and calls the distillery the largest in America at the time. Note that 10,942 is gallons sold, not a production count.
- The mash bill, 60% rye, 35% corn, 5% malted barley, was reconstructed from the distillery ledgers of 1798 and 1799.
- Washington died on 14 December 1799. His nephew Lawrence Lewis inherited the mill and distillery; distilling continued until about 1808, and the distillery burned in 1814.
- An archaeological survey in 1997 found the footprint; excavation followed (1999 or 2000 by different accounts).
- The still house was reconstructed on its original foundations, helped by a $2.1 million grant from the Distilled Spirits Council of the United States, and opened in 2007.

### Primary documents

- Washington's farm and distillery ledgers, held by Mount Vernon (Fred W. Smith National Library) and published in the _Papers of George Washington_, Retirement Series.
- Mount Vernon's archaeological reports on the distillery excavation.

### Traps

- "Largest distillery in America" has no national survey behind it. Mount Vernon makes the claim; say that it is theirs.
- The mash bill is a reconstruction from ledger entries, not a written recipe. One magazine prints "15% malted barley", a typo (the figures would not add up).

## Module 3. Monongahela and Maryland

**The question.** Were Pennsylvania and Maryland rye really two styles, and what was the difference?

### Nailed down

- Beam currently sells a rye called A. Overholt Monongahela Mash, made in Kentucky: the regional name now describes a style, not a place.
- The style descriptions on slide 4 are presented in the deck as later writers' descriptions, not as established fact.

### Primary documents

- Annual Reports of the Commissioner of Internal Revenue (HathiTrust), production by collection district.
- Period trade papers, such as _Bonfort's Wine and Spirit Circular_.
- Surviving labels and advertisements in museum and library collections.

### Traps

- "Monongahela was all rye, Maryland was softer with corn" is mostly a twentieth-century description. Few period mash bills survive.
- Brands used "Monongahela" and "Maryland" as quality words, not only as places of origin.

### Sources

- VinePair, review of A. Overholt Monongahela Mash Straight Rye: https://vinepair.com/review/a-overholt-monongahela-mash-straight-rye-whiskey

## Module 4. Overholt

**The question.** How did a farm distillery become a national brand, and who owned it?

### Nailed down

- Abraham Overholt (1784–1870), Westmoreland County, Pennsylvania. The brand dates itself to 1810 at West Overton and is described as America's oldest continually maintained whiskey brand.
- His grandson Henry Clay Frick took over the business in the 1880s (1881 in some accounts) with Andrew Mellon and Charles W. Mauck as partners, each with a third. Mauck adopted "Old Overholt" as the name in 1888, with Abraham's portrait as the logo.
- Frick died in December 1919 and left his share to Mellon.
- Old Overholt held one of the few Prohibition medicinal licenses. Mellon's stake was politically controversial while he was Treasury Secretary, and he sold it in 1925 (Sotheby's and a second account agree on the year; one names the New York grocers Park & Tilford as buyer).
- Broad Ford reopened after Repeal; distilling there ended in 1951 under National Distillers.
- After 1951 the whiskey is believed to have been made elsewhere in Pennsylvania; the record is thin. In 1987 Beam bought National Distillers' spirits business, including Old Overholt, and moved production to Kentucky.
- Andrew Mellon was Secretary of the Treasury from 1921 to 1932.

### Primary documents

- West Overton Village and Museum archives (the Overholt homestead).
- Frick and Mellon papers; Westmoreland and Fayette county deed and business records.

### Traps

- The size of Mellon's stake by the 1920s is disputed: Sotheby's calls him a minority partner, VinePair the majority owner. Do not give a figure.
- Whether Mellon personally steered the medicinal license to Overholt is asserted by one source (Bourbon Veach) and loosely by others. The deck says only that the brand held one and that his stake drew criticism.

## Module 5. Maryland and Pikesville

**The question.** What happened to Maryland's distilleries after Repeal?

### Nailed down

- Pikesville Rye began in the 1890s (1895 in several accounts) with the Winand family's distillery at Scott's Level, near Pikesville, north-west of Baltimore. Prohibition closed it.
- The brand was revived in the 1930s (one account: bought by Baltimore businessman Andrew Merle in 1936) and made under contract at Baltimore distilleries, latterly Majestic.
- Pikesville was last distilled in Maryland in 1972; stocks lasted until 1982, when the brand and recipe were sold to Heaven Hill of Bardstown, Kentucky.
- Heaven Hill relaunched it in 2015 as Pikesville Straight Rye: six years old, 110 proof (55% ABV), made at the Bernheim distillery.
- Heaven Hill does not publish the mash bill. It is widely reported as 51% rye, 39% corn, 10% malted barley, the same as Rittenhouse.

### Primary documents

- Maryland state and Baltimore city business directories; Internal Revenue distillery registrations.

### Traps

- Accounts disagree on who owned the brand at the 1972 closure (Majestic, or Standard Distillers Products) and on the date of the explosion at the Monumental plant (late 1930s or 1942).
- Difford's Guide gives a different mash bill (65% rye). Most sources give 51%. Say "reported".
- "The last Maryland rye distillery" is stronger than the sources; the deck says only that Pikesville's last Maryland batch was in 1972.

## Module 6. Prohibition and after

**The question.** Why did rye fail to recover after 1933 when bourbon did?

### Nailed down

- National Prohibition began on 17 January 1920 and ended with the ratification of the Twenty-first Amendment on 5 December 1933.
- Six companies held medicinal whiskey licenses (American Medicinal Spirits, Schenley, James Thompson and Brother, Frankfort, Brown-Forman, A. Ph. Stitzel; lists vary slightly). Old Overholt was a medicinal brand.
- Late in the 1920s the licensed companies were allowed to distil again to replenish stocks. Sources give 1928 or 1929, and different volumes.
- In 1942 the War Production Board ordered distilleries to stop making beverage alcohol and make industrial alcohol. The restriction ended with the war; full beverage production resumed in 1945–46.
- Congress agreed to S. Con. Res. 19, recognizing bourbon as a distinctive product of the United States, on 4 May 1964. It is a resolution, not a statute.
- Bomberger's Distillery at Schaefferstown made Michter's and closed in 1989 after Michter's bankruptcy (one source says 1990). It is a National Historic Landmark (1980). The current Michter's brand in Kentucky has no direct link to it.

### Primary documents

- Bureau of Prohibition records on medicinal licenses.
- War Production Board orders on distilleries, 1942–1945.
- Senate Finance Committee Report 88-496 on the bourbon resolution.
- National Register nomination for Bomberger's Distillery (NPS, 75001649).

### Traps

- Explanations for rye's decline (taste for blends, Canadian whisky, vodka) are reasonable but rarely backed by figures. The deck says so. Use sales data by category if you can find it.

## Module 7. The Kentucky holdouts

**The question.** Which ryes survived until the revival, and who made them?

### Nailed down

- Before the revival the remaining ryes came from four Kentucky distillers: Beam (Old Overholt, Jim Beam Rye), Heaven Hill (Pikesville, Rittenhouse), Wild Turkey (Wild Turkey Rye) and Buffalo Trace (Sazerac Rye).
- Rittenhouse began in 1934 at Continental Distilling, Philadelphia. Heaven Hill bought it in 1999 (Distiller says 1993; most sources say 1999).
- A Heaven Hill-related trade piece says the company distilled rye only one or two days a year in the 1990s.

### Traps

- Sazerac Rye's introduction date was not found; the deck gives none.
- One writer remembers a point when only three ryes were left (Jim Beam, Wild Turkey 101, Old Overholt). Lists depend on the year.

## Module 8. The standard of identity

**The question.** What does the law require of rye whisky?

### Nailed down

- 27 CFR 5.143: rye whisky is from a mash of at least 51% rye, distilled at not more than 160 proof and stored at not more than 125 proof in charred new oak containers. Whisky of this kind stored two years or more may be called straight.
- 27 CFR 5.74: whisky aged under four years must carry an age statement.
- 27 CFR 5.66(f): the label of American rye (and other listed whisky types) must show the state of original distillation. The bottler's address satisfies this when it is in that state, which is why in practice the state appears separately when it differs.

### Primary documents

- Title 27, Code of Federal Regulations, Part 5 (ecfr.gov), and the TTB Beverage Alcohol Manual.

### Traps

- The pre-2022 rule (§ 5.36(d)) required the state only when it differed from the address; the current § 5.66(f) is worded as a general requirement with the address as one way to meet it.

### Sources

- Cornell LII, 27 CFR 5.143: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/27/5.143
- Cornell LII, 27 CFR 5.66: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/27/5.66
- eCFR, 27 CFR Part 5, Subpart E: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-27/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-5/subpart-E

## Module 9. Lawrenceburg, Indiana, and sourced rye

**The question.** How did one Indiana distillery come to supply so many rye brands?

### Nailed down

- Founded as the Rossville distillery in 1847. Seagram bought it in 1933.
- After Seagram's break-up it went to Pernod Ricard (2001; some sources date the beverage split to 2000). Pernod sold it in 2007 to CL Financial, the Trinidad parent of Angostura, and it ran as Lawrenceburg Distillers Indiana (LDI). A 2008 trade report dates the Angostura deal's completion to that June.
- MGP Ingredients agreed the purchase in October 2011 and closed it on 27 December 2011 for about $11 million (MGP 10-K for 2011). It now trades as Ross & Squibb and sells aged and new whiskey to other brands.
- The 95% rye, 5% malted barley mash was developed under Seagram as a blending whiskey; accounts credit Larry Ebersold, who began there in 1972, and differ on when it was settled (VinePair places it in the 1990s).
- George Dickel Rye is MGP 95% rye; High West has used MGP rye in its blends.
- Bulleit 95 Rye (launched 2011) is made from that mash at Lawrenceburg. When Bulleit launched its bourbon-and-rye blend Bulleit '87 in 2026, coverage of the release stated that the rye component was distilled and aged by MGP in Lawrenceburg, while the bourbon is distilled in Shelbyville, Kentucky.
- Templeton Rye was distilled at Lawrenceburg by MGP on a recipe shared with other brands. Class actions were filed in 2014 (_Aliano v. Templeton Rye Spirits_, Cook County, Illinois, No. 2014-CH-15667; _McNair_, moved to federal court in September 2014; a third in Polk County, Iowa). Templeton denied wrongdoing and settled in 2015: refunds of up to $2.5 million, "distilled in Indiana" added to the label, "Prohibition Era Recipe" and "small batch" removed. The suits targeted its Al Capone and Prohibition-era marketing.
- Templeton opened a $35 million distillery in Templeton, Iowa, in August 2018. Its first whiskey made entirely in Iowa, released in 2024, was a bourbon (Fortitude).

### Primary documents

- MGP Ingredients annual reports (SEC filings) for the acquisition and its distilled-spirits business.
- Court filings in _Aliano_ and _McNair_; the settlement notice.
- TTB COLA Public Registry, for the approved Bulleit 95 Rye back label.

### Traps

- Brands rarely announce sourcing and it changes. Check each brand's current label for its state of distillation before naming it.
- "Sourced" is not a fault in itself. The complaint against Templeton was about the story on the label.

## Module 10. The revival

**The question.** When and why did rye come back?

### Nailed down

- Mount Vernon's reconstructed still house opened in 2007 (Module 2 sources).
- Old Forester Rye (2019) and Pikesville's relaunch (2015) are examples of established distillers adding higher-priced ryes.

### Primary documents

- Distilled Spirits Council annual economic briefings, which report rye volume.

### Traps

- Growth percentages for rye in the 2010s are often quoted without a base year. Give volumes, not percentages, if you can.

## The four pours

- **Pour 1. Old Overholt.** Label facts confirmed: straight rye, 4 years old, 86 proof. Beam, Kentucky. Was 3 years and 80 proof; 86 proof from about 2020, 4 years from 2022. Mash bill not published. Why it is in the flight: a Pennsylvania name, made in Kentucky.
- **Pour 2. Pikesville Straight Rye.** Label facts confirmed: 6 years old, 110 proof. Heaven Hill. Mash reported 51% rye. Why it is in the flight: a Maryland name, made in Kentucky; at the legal minimum of rye.
- **Pour 3. Old Forester Rye.** Label facts confirmed: label "Kentucky Straight Rye Whisky", 100 proof (50% ABV). Published mash 65% rye, 20% malted barley, 15% corn. Launched 2019. Why it is in the flight: a published mash bill in the middle of the range.
- **Pour 4. Bulleit 95 Rye.** Label facts confirmed: straight rye, 95% rye and 5% malted barley, 90 proof (45% ABV). Distilled by MGP in Lawrenceburg, Indiana; bottled by Bulleit in Shelbyville, Kentucky. Why it is in the flight: the 95% rye mash from Indiana.

Pour sources: Beam Distilling, Old Overholt (https://www.beamdistilling.com/old-overholt/whiskies/old-overholt-original); Distiller, Old Overholt 4 Year 86 Proof (https://distiller.com/spirits/old-overholt-4-year-straight-rye-whiskey-86-proof); The Manual, "Old Overholt rye whiskey update" (https://themanual.com/food-and-drink/old-overholt-rye-whiskey-update); The Liquor Barn, Old Overholt 3 Year listing (discontinuation note, https://theliquorbarn.com/products/old-overholt-3-year-rye-750ml); Old Forester, 100 Proof Rye Whisky (https://www.oldforester.com/products/old-forester-100-proof-rye-whisky/) and "Old Forester introduces Rye Whisky" (https://www.oldforester.com/old-forester-introduces-rye-whisky/); Heaven Hill and Bulleit sources as in Modules 5 and 9.

Prices vary too much by region to quote.

## Not confirmed

- **Bulleit 95 Rye's exact back-label wording.** Retailer listings give "Distilled in Indiana and bottled by the Bulleit Distilling Co., Shelbyville", and the 2026 '87 launch confirms the rye is still distilled in Indiana, but no label image or COLA was seen. The slide says "distilled in Indiana", which is confirmed. Settle with the TTB COLA Public Registry.
- **Sazerac Rye's introduction date.** Searched Buffalo Trace reviews and histories; none gave a year. The deck gives no date. Settle with Buffalo Trace's own release history.
- **The 95/5 recipe's date.** Searched Seagram/MGP histories; accounts differ (Ebersold from 1972, VinePair says the 1990s). The deck gives no date. Settle with Seagram-era records or an interview with Ebersold.
- **Post-Repeal taste shift in figures.** Not searched for sales data; the script says plainly that it has none. Settle with DISCUS category data.
