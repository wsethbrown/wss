## Part One: before the night

### The short version

Most of the words on a bourbon label have a legal meaning, and a few have none. Tonight's four bourbons each carry one of the words that matter: bourbon, bonded, straight with an age, and barrel proof. The rules behind "bonded" and "straight" were written because a lot of nineteenth-century whiskey was not what its label said.

### What the night is

A beginner's night. Nobody needs to know anything before they sit down. One glass at a time, lowest proof first, and each glass comes with the word it teaches and where that word came from.

Be fair to every bottle. All four are good whiskey at an ordinary price, and none of them is here to lose.

### How many bottles

Four. Past five, palate fatigue does your thinking for you.

### Cast the roles, then find the bottles

- **The Bourbon (glass 1).** A standard bourbon made with wheat as its second grain. It teaches the basic rules. Tonight: Maker's Mark.
- **The Bonded One (glass 2).** A bottled-in-bond bourbon made with plenty of rye. It teaches "bonded" and gives the room rye to compare with wheat. Tonight: Old Grand-Dad Bonded.
- **The One With a Number (glass 3).** A straight bourbon with an age printed on the label. Tonight: Knob Creek 9 Year.
- **The Barrel Proof (glass 4).** A bourbon bottled at the strength it left the barrel. Tonight: Wild Turkey Rare Breed.

## Part Two: the script

_SET UP: four empty glasses in front of everyone, marked 1 to 4. Water on the table. All four bottles in view._

## [SLIDE 1] Bourbon Basics

Good evening, and welcome.

Tonight is four bourbons and the words printed on their labels. Most of those words have a legal meaning. A distiller who prints "bourbon" or "straight" or "bonded" has to meet a federal rule to do it. By the end of the night you will know what each of those words promises, and which words promise nothing.

You do not need to know anything about whiskey to follow this. We start by pouring one.

## [SLIDE 2] Glass 1: Maker's Mark

_Pour glass 1 now. Half an ounce each is plenty. Keep talking while you pour._

This is Maker's Mark, from Loretto, Kentucky. It is 90 proof, which is 45% alcohol.

Have a look at the front label. It says "Kentucky Straight Bourbon Whisky". We are going to take that line apart tonight, one word at a time, starting with "bourbon".

Don't drink it all. You will want some left to compare with glass 2.

_Ask: "Before I tell you anything about it, what do you smell?" Take two or three answers, then move on._

## [SLIDE 3] How to taste it

There is no wrong answer when you say what you smell or taste. You have been smelling and tasting food your whole life, and that is all the training this takes.

Here is a method from the whiskey writer Lew Bryson. Lift the glass slowly until you can smell the whiskey, and stop there. Breathe normally. Ask yourself what else smells like this.

Take a small sip and hold it on your tongue for a few seconds before you swallow. Then take a second sip. Wild Turkey's own tasting guide says the first sip gets your mouth used to the strength and the second gives you more detail.

If it is too hot, add a few drops of water. That is not cheating. The people who blend whiskey for a living do it.

_Give them a minute to try it with glass 1._

## [SLIDE 4] What "bourbon" means

"Bourbon" is defined in federal regulations. To print the word on a label, the whiskey has to meet all of these.

_Walk the list on the slide. Take it slowly._

It is made in the United States. The grain is at least 51% corn. It comes off the still at no more than 160 proof. It goes into new, charred oak barrels at no more than 125 proof. It is bottled at 80 proof or more. And nothing is added for colour or flavour.

So the colour in your glass came from the barrel. It went into the barrel clear.

One thing is missing from that list, and that is a minimum age. Plain bourbon has none. The words that tell you about age are "straight" and "bonded", and the number on glass 3. We will get to those.

## [SLIDE 5] It does not have to come from Kentucky

Nothing in the federal rule mentions Kentucky. Bourbon can be made in any state. The regulation's own example of a label is "New York Bourbon Whisky".

When a state's name is printed beside the word "bourbon", the whiskey was distilled and aged in that state. Kentucky's own law adds that it must be aged there at least a year before it can use the name.

In 1964 Congress passed a resolution calling bourbon "a distinctive product of the United States". Its purpose was to stop imported whiskey being sold as bourbon. It was a resolution and not a law, and it did not define bourbon. The definition was already in the regulations.

> **IF SOMEONE ASKS WHERE THE NAME COMES FROM**
> Historians disagree. Some say Bourbon County, Kentucky, and point to newspaper advertisements from the 1820s. Michael Veach argues for Bourbon Street in New Orleans and calls it his theory. Say that it is not settled.

## [SLIDE 6] The second grain

The rule says at least 51% corn. The rest is the distiller's choice. There is nearly always some malted barley, and then a second grain, which is rye in most bourbons and wheat in a few.

Distillers describe the difference like this. Wheat makes a softer, sweeter bourbon. Rye adds spice: pepper and cinnamon.

Maker's Mark publishes its recipe: 70% corn, 16% wheat, 14% malted barley, and no rye.

The next glass has no wheat. Its maker does not publish the recipe, but writers who cover the distillery report it as about 27% rye, and the label says "High Rye Mash Bill".

## [SLIDE 7] Glass 2: Old Grand-Dad Bonded

_Pour glass 2 now._

This is Old Grand-Dad Bonded. It is made by Jim Beam in Kentucky and it is 100 proof.

Smell it next to glass 1 before you taste it. Then taste glass 1, then glass 2.

_Ask: "What is different about the second one?" Let them answer before you say anything about rye or proof._

Two things changed between the glasses. The second grain went from wheat to rye, and the proof went up by ten.

Now look at the label. It says "Bonded". That word has the oldest rule of any on the table, and it dates from 1897. To explain why it was written, I need to tell you what whiskey was like before it.

## [SLIDE 8] Before the rules

In the 1800s a distiller sold whiskey by the barrel. The drinker bought it from a saloon or a merchant, by the glass or in a plain bottle. Between the two stood a trade called rectifying. Rectifiers bought spirit, then blended it, coloured it and flavoured it. The trade was legal, licensed and taxed.

Their manuals were published, and you can still read them. One from 1853 gives a recipe for "Old Bourbon Whiskey" with no bourbon in it: neutral spirit, water, strong tea and oil of wintergreen, coloured with cochineal and burnt sugar.

Another, from 1885, lists eleven grades of whiskey by price. The cheapest is spirit, water and colouring. The next one up adds something called "Oil of Bourbon", and is sold as bourbon.

The author of that second book was in the trade himself. He wrote that a man who orders whiskey, even at a first-class bar, "does not know what he drinks".

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> Not every rectifier was a fraud. The best grade in the 1885 manual is a blend of good straight whiskeys. And the stories about tobacco in the barrel were called "a popular error" by the 1853 manual itself.

> **WHO DID THE WORK**
> Optional. The whiskey writer Fred Minnick records that before the end of slavery the biggest names in Kentucky whiskey used enslaved labour. The surviving records show enslaved people rolling barrels and gathering grain. Minnick says the records do not show whether they also did the distilling.

## [SLIDE 9] 1897: bottled in bond

On the 3rd of March 1897 Congress passed "An Act to allow the bottling of distilled spirits in bond". Historians credit a Kentucky distiller, Colonel Edmund Haynes Taylor Junior, and the Treasury Secretary, John G. Carlisle, with getting it through.

A distiller who wanted to use it had to follow these rules. The whiskey came from one distillery and one distilling season. Nothing went into it except water, to bring it to 100 proof. It was at least four years old. It was bottled at the distillery with a government officer watching. And a stamp was pasted over the cork, giving the real name of the distiller.

For the first time, a drinker could buy a sealed bottle and know who made the whiskey, how old it was and how strong it was.

The Act was repealed in 1979, but the rule is still in the regulations. A label that says "bonded" or "bottled in bond" today means one distillery, one season, at least four years old, and exactly 100 proof.

> **SAY THE CAVEAT OUT LOUD**
> The Act did not promise the whiskey was good. It promised the label was true. The rectifiers' lawyer said so in 1906: with a bonded bottle you know who made it and how old it is, and nothing about whether it is fit to drink.

## [SLIDE 10] What "straight" means

Bottling in bond was voluntary. Everything else could still be labelled however the seller liked.

In 1906 Congress passed the Pure Food and Drugs Act, and the argument began over what could be called whiskey. The government's chief chemist, Harvey Wiley, mixed a whiskey in front of a House committee out of alcohol, caramel and flavouring. He told them, "I am a rectifier now; I have taken out my license."

The question went to the President. In December 1909 William Howard Taft ruled that any spirit distilled from grain could be called whiskey. But the words "straight", "bourbon" and "rye" were kept for whiskey that was aged and unmixed, and whiskey made from neutral spirit had to say so on the label.

Wiley thought the rectifiers had won. The straight distillers kept the words.

Today "straight" on a bourbon label means at least two years in the barrel. If the whiskey is less than four years old, the label must give its age. And when a label gives an age, it is the age of the youngest whiskey in the bottle.

## [SLIDE 11] Glass 3: Knob Creek 9 Year

_Pour glass 3 now._

This is Knob Creek. It says "Kentucky Straight Bourbon Whiskey", and it has a large 9 on the front: "Aged Nine Years". Every drop in the bottle is at least nine years old.

It is 100 proof, the same as glass 2, and it is made by the same company, Jim Beam, in Clermont, Kentucky. Glass 2 is at least four years old. This one is at least nine.

_Ask: "Same proof as the last one. What did five more years do?" Let them compare glasses 2 and 3._

A straight bourbon over four years old does not have to print its age, so the number is the distiller's choice. Knob Creek took the 9 off the label in 2016 and put it back in 2020. Maker's Mark has no age on its label.

## [SLIDE 12] What the barrel does

Whiskey comes off the still clear. Bourbon cannot have colour added, so all of the colour in these four glasses came from the wood.

The barrel is charred on the inside before it is filled. The heat breaks down the wood under the char, and that is where the vanilla and caramel flavours come from. The char itself works as a filter.

Some whiskey is lost every year. Heaven Hill, a Kentucky distillery, puts it at about 6% in the first year and 3% a year after that. Distillers call it the angel's share.

The proof changes in the barrel too. On the hot upper floors of a Kentucky warehouse, water leaves faster than alcohol and the proof goes up. On the cool lower floors it goes down.

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> A figure often quoted is that 70% of the flavour comes from the barrel. Nobody has measured it. The estimates from people in the trade run from half to 70%. "Most of it" is safe.

## [SLIDE 13] Words with rules, and words without

So far every word we have looked at has a rule behind it: bourbon, straight, bonded, and the age.

Some words on a label have no federal definition. "Small batch" is one. So are "reserve" and "handmade". "Single barrel" is not defined either. A label is not allowed to mislead, but no rule says how small a small batch is.

Pick up the Knob Creek. "Small Batch" is moulded into the glass. It may well be true. The regulations do not say what it means.

There is one more word tonight with a rule behind it, and it is on the last bottle.

## [SLIDE 14] Glass 4: Wild Turkey Rare Breed

_Pour glass 4 now. Pour a little less than before. Make sure the water is within reach._

This is Wild Turkey Rare Breed, from Lawrenceburg, Kentucky. The label says "Barrel Proof".

That has a rule. The whiskey must be bottled within two degrees of proof of what it was when it came out of the barrel. Most whiskey has water added before bottling to bring it down to a set strength.

This bottle says 116.8 proof, which is 58.4% alcohol. There is no age on the label. The company says it is a mix of six, eight and twelve year old bourbon.

Take a small sip as it is. Then add three or four drops of water, give it a moment, and taste it again.

_Ask: "What changed when you added the water?"_

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> A 2017 study is often quoted as proof that water improves whiskey. It was a computer simulation of one flavour molecule in alcohol and water. Nobody tasted anything. Trust the room's answer over the study.

## [SLIDE 15] Read the shelf

Put the four bottles side by side and read them again.

Maker's Mark: "Kentucky Straight Bourbon Whisky". Made and aged in Kentucky, at least 51% corn, new charred oak, at least two years old, nothing added.

Old Grand-Dad: "Bonded". One distillery, one season, at least four years old, 100 proof.

Knob Creek: "Aged Nine Years". Nothing in the bottle is younger than nine.

Rare Breed: "Barrel Proof". The strength it came out of the barrel.

The next time you are in a shop, pick up a bottle you have never tried and read the label before you look at the price.

_Ask: "Which of the four would you buy again?" Let this one run._

## [SLIDE 16] Sláinte

Raise whichever glass you liked best.

To the people who made these four bourbons, and to the ones who made the labels tell the truth.

Sláinte.
