## Part One: before the night

### The short version

Most of the words on a bourbon label have a legal meaning, and a few have none. Tonight's four bourbons each carry one of the words that matter: bourbon, bonded, straight with an age, and barrel proof. The rules behind "bonded" and "straight" were written because a lot of nineteenth-century whiskey was not what its label said.

### What the night is

A night for beginners. Nobody needs to know anything about whiskey before they sit down. You pour four bourbons one at a time, lowest proof first. For each one you explain one term on its label and where the rule behind it came from.

All four are good bourbons at ordinary prices. The night does not rank them.

### How many bottles

Four.

### Cast the roles, then find the bottles

- **Glass 1: a standard bourbon made with wheat as its second grain.** You use it to explain the word "bourbon". Suggested bottle: Maker's Mark.
- **Glass 2: a bottled-in-bond bourbon made with a lot of rye.** You use it to explain "bonded", and the room compares rye with wheat. Suggested bottle: Old Grand-Dad Bonded.
- **Glass 3: a straight bourbon with its age printed on the label.** Suggested bottle: Knob Creek 9 Year.
- **Glass 4: a barrel proof bourbon.** It is bottled at the strength it came out of the barrel. Suggested bottle: Wild Turkey Rare Breed.

## Part Two: the script

_SET UP: four empty glasses in front of everyone, marked 1 to 4. Water on the table. All four bottles in view._

## [SLIDE 1] Bourbon Basics

Good evening, and welcome.

Tonight is four bourbons and the words printed on their labels. Most of those words have a legal meaning. A distiller who prints "bourbon" or "straight" or "bonded" has to meet a federal rule to do it. A few other words on a label have no rule behind them.

You do not need to know anything about whiskey to follow this.

## [SLIDE 2] Glass 1: Maker's Mark

_Pour glass 1 now. Half an ounce each is plenty. Keep talking while you pour._

This is Maker's Mark, from Loretto, Kentucky. It is 90 proof, which is 45% alcohol.

Have a look at the front label. It says "Kentucky Straight Bourbon Whisky".

Don't drink it all. Keep some to compare with glass 2.

_Ask: "Before I tell you anything about it, what do you smell?" Take two or three answers, then move on._

## [SLIDE 3] How to taste it

There is no wrong answer when you say what you smell or taste. You have been smelling and tasting food all your life.

Here is a simple way to do it. Lift the glass slowly until you can smell the whiskey, and stop there. Breathe normally. Ask yourself what else smells like this.

Take a small sip and hold it on your tongue for a few seconds before you swallow. Then take a second sip. The first sip gets your mouth used to the strength. The second one gives you more detail.

If it is too hot, add a few drops of water. People who blend whiskey for a living add water too.

_Give them a minute to try it with glass 1._

## [SLIDE 4] What "bourbon" means

"Bourbon" is defined in federal regulations. To print the word on a label, the whiskey has to meet all of these.

_Walk the list on the slide. Take it slowly._

It is made in the United States. The grain is at least 51% corn. It comes off the still at no more than 160 proof. It goes into new, charred oak barrels at no more than 125 proof. It is bottled at 80 proof or more. And nothing is added for color or flavor.

The color in your glass came from the barrel. The whiskey was clear when it went in.

There is no minimum age on that list. A whiskey can meet every one of those rules without spending any set time in the barrel. A label says something about age only when it carries another word, "straight" or "bonded", or prints an age such as "Aged Nine Years".

## [SLIDE 5] It does not have to come from Kentucky

Nothing in the federal rule mentions Kentucky. Bourbon can be made in any state. The regulation's own example of a label is "New York Bourbon Whisky".

When a state's name is printed beside the word "bourbon", the whiskey was distilled and aged in that state. Kentucky's own law adds that it must be aged there at least a year before it can use the name.

In 1964 Congress passed a resolution calling bourbon "a distinctive product of the United States". Its purpose was to stop imported whiskey being sold as bourbon. The resolution changed no rule. The definition of bourbon was already in the regulations.

> **IF SOMEONE ASKS WHERE THE NAME COMES FROM**
> Nobody knows for sure. Some historians say it comes from Bourbon County, Kentucky. Another theory is Bourbon Street in New Orleans. It has not been settled.

## [SLIDE 6] The second grain

The rule says at least 51% corn. The rest is the distiller's choice. There is nearly always some malted barley, and then a second grain, which is rye in most bourbons and wheat in a few.

Distillers describe the difference like this. Wheat makes a softer, sweeter bourbon. Rye adds spice: pepper and cinnamon.

Maker's Mark publishes its recipe: 70% corn, 16% wheat, 14% malted barley, and no rye.

Glass 2 is Old Grand-Dad Bonded, and its second grain is rye. Its maker does not publish the recipe. It is usually reported as about 27% rye, and the label says "High Rye Mash Bill".

## [SLIDE 7] Glass 2: Old Grand-Dad Bonded

_Pour glass 2 now._

This is Old Grand-Dad Bonded. It is made by Jim Beam in Kentucky and it is 100 proof.

Smell it next to glass 1 before you taste it. Then taste glass 1, then glass 2.

_Ask: "What is different about the second one?" Let them answer before you say anything about rye or proof._

Two things changed between the glasses. The second grain went from wheat to rye, and the proof went up by ten.

The label says "Bonded". The rule behind that word dates from 1897.

## [SLIDE 8] Before the rules

Before 1897 a distiller sold whiskey by the barrel. The drinker bought it from a saloon or a merchant, by the glass or in a plain bottle. In between there was a trade called rectifying. Rectifiers bought spirit, then blended it, colored it and flavored it. The trade was legal, licensed and taxed.

Rectifiers had printed manuals. One from 1853 gives a recipe for "Old Bourbon Whiskey" with no bourbon in it: neutral spirit, water, strong tea and oil of wintergreen, colored with cochineal and burnt sugar.

Another, from 1885, lists eleven grades of whiskey by price. The cheapest is spirit, water and coloring. The next one up adds something called "Oil of Bourbon", and is sold as bourbon.

Its author was Joseph Fleischman. He wrote that a man who takes a glass of whiskey, even at a first-class bar, "does not know what he drinks".

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> Not every rectifier was a fraud. The best grade in the 1885 manual is a blend of good straight whiskeys. And the stories about tobacco in the barrel were called "a popular error" by the 1853 manual itself.

## [SLIDE 9] 1897: bottled in bond

On the 3rd of March 1897 Congress passed "An Act to allow the bottling of distilled spirits in bond". A Kentucky distiller, Colonel Edmund Haynes Taylor Junior, usually gets the credit for pushing it, along with the Treasury Secretary, John G. Carlisle.

A distiller who wanted to use it had to follow these rules. The whiskey came from one distillery and one distilling season. Nothing went into it except water, to bring it to 100 proof. It was at least four years old. It was bottled at the distillery with a government officer watching. And a stamp was pasted over the cork, giving the real name of the distiller.

A drinker who bought a bonded bottle knew who made the whiskey, how old it was and how strong it was.

The Act was repealed in 1979, but the rule is still in the regulations. A label that says "bonded" or "bottled in bond" today means one distillery, one season, at least four years old, and exactly 100 proof.

> **IF SOMEONE ASKS**
> The Act said nothing about whether the whiskey was good. It only covered what was on the label. The rectifiers' lawyer made that point in 1906. He said a bonded bottle tells you who made the whiskey and how old it is, and does not tell you whether it is fit to drink.

## [SLIDE 10] What "straight" means

Bottling in bond was voluntary. Everything else could still be labeled however the seller liked.

In 1906 Congress passed the Pure Food and Drugs Act, and the argument began over what could be called whiskey. The government's chief chemist, Harvey Wiley, mixed a whiskey in front of a House committee out of alcohol, caramel and flavoring. He told them, "I am a rectifier now; I have taken out my license."

The question went to the President. In December 1909 William Howard Taft ruled that any spirit distilled from grain could be called whiskey. But the words "straight", "bourbon" and "rye" were kept for whiskey that was aged and unmixed, and whiskey made from neutral spirit had to say so on the label.

Ten years later Wiley wrote that Taft had "decided all questions in favor of the rectifiers".

Today "straight" on a bourbon label means at least two years in the barrel. If the whiskey is less than four years old, the label must give its age. And when a label gives an age, it is the age of the youngest whiskey in the bottle.

## [SLIDE 11] Glass 3: Knob Creek 9 Year

_Pour glass 3 now._

This is Knob Creek. It says "Kentucky Straight Bourbon Whiskey", and it has a large 9 on the front: "Aged Nine Years". Every drop in the bottle is at least nine years old.

It is 100 proof, the same as glass 2, and it is made by the same company, Jim Beam, in Clermont, Kentucky. Glass 2 is at least four years old. This one is at least nine.

_Ask: "Same proof as the last one. What did five more years do?" Let them compare glasses 2 and 3._

A straight bourbon over four years old does not have to print its age, so the number is the distiller's choice. Knob Creek took the 9 off the label in 2016 and put it back in 2020. Maker's Mark has no age on its label.

## [SLIDE 12] What the barrel does

Whiskey comes off the still clear. Bourbon cannot have color added, so all of the color in these four glasses came from the wood.

The barrel is charred on the inside before it is filled. The heat breaks down the wood under the char, and that is where the vanilla and caramel flavors come from. The char itself works as a filter.

Some whiskey is lost every year. Heaven Hill, a Kentucky distillery, puts it at about 6% in the first year and 3% a year after that. Distillers call it the angel's share.

The proof changes in the barrel too. On the hot upper floors of a Kentucky warehouse, water leaves faster than alcohol and the proof goes up. On the cool lower floors it goes down.

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> A figure often quoted is that 70% of the flavor comes from the barrel. Nobody has measured it. The estimates from people in the trade run from half to 70%. "Most of it" is safe.

## [SLIDE 13] Words with rules, and words without

Bourbon, straight, bonded and a printed age each have a federal rule behind them.

Some words on a label have no federal definition. "Small batch" is one. So are "reserve" and "handmade". "Single barrel" is not defined either. A label is not allowed to mislead, but no rule says how small a small batch is.

Pick up the Knob Creek. "Small Batch" is molded into the glass. No regulation says what that means.

"Barrel proof" does have a rule. It is on the label of glass 4.

## [SLIDE 14] Glass 4: Wild Turkey Rare Breed

_Pour glass 4 now. Pour a little less than before. Make sure the water is within reach._

This is Wild Turkey Rare Breed, from Lawrenceburg, Kentucky. The label says "Barrel Proof".

Under the rule for that term, the whiskey must be bottled within two degrees of proof of what it was when it came out of the barrel. Most whiskey has water added before bottling to bring it down to a set strength.

This bottle says 116.8 proof, which is 58.4% alcohol. There is no age on the label. The company says it is a mix of six, eight and twelve year old bourbon.

Take a small sip as it is. Then add three or four drops of water, give it a moment, and taste it again.

_Ask: "What changed when you added the water?"_

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> A 2017 study is often quoted as proof that water improves whiskey. It was a computer simulation of one flavor molecule in alcohol and water. Nobody tasted anything. Go by what the room tastes.

## [SLIDE 15] What the four labels say

Put the four bottles side by side and read them again.

Maker's Mark: "Kentucky Straight Bourbon Whisky". Made and aged in Kentucky, at least 51% corn, new charred oak, at least two years old, nothing added.

Old Grand-Dad: "Bonded". One distillery, one season, at least four years old, 100 proof.

Knob Creek: "Aged Nine Years". Nothing in the bottle is younger than nine.

Rare Breed: "Barrel Proof". The strength it came out of the barrel.

_Ask: "Which of the four would you buy again?" Let this one run._

## [SLIDE 16] Sláinte

Raise whichever glass you liked best.

To the people who made these four bourbons. Sláinte.
