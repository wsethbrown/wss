# Bourbon Basics

Research outline: what the words on a bourbon label promise

_This is the other half of the speaker notes. It does not tell you what to say; it tells you where to look, what is settled, what is still argued about, and which widely repeated claims will trip you up. Facts were checked in November 2026 against the documents listed at the end, each marked with how much of it was read. Prices, proofs, label wording and who owns which brand move quickly: re-check anything about a current bottle against the bottle in your hand._

> **THE SPINE OF THE STORY**
> Whiskey leaves the distillery in barrels and reaches the drinker through wholesalers and licensed rectifiers, who stretch, color and flavor it; trade manuals print the recipes (1853, 1885) → Congress lets a distiller bottle under government supervision and seal the bottle with a stamp naming the maker, the season and the proof (Bottled-in-Bond Act, 3 March 1897) → the Food and Drugs Act (30 June 1906) starts a fight over what may be called whisky: Roosevelt sides with the straight distillers (10 April 1907), Taft widens "whisky" again but keeps "straight", "bourbon" and "rye" for the unmixed article (27 December 1909) → after Prohibition, federal regulations tie "straight" and age claims to charred new oak (whiskey made from 1 July 1936) and then the word "bourbon" itself, with 51 percent corn and 160 proof (1 March 1938) → Congress resolves that bourbon is "a distinctive product of the United States" (4 May 1964) → the 1897 Act is repealed (1979) and "bonded" survives as a labeling rule; "barrel proof" is defined by ruling (79-9) and then by regulation (2022) → today each word is a line in 27 CFR part 5, and some words on the same label are in no rule at all.

## How to use this

Each module gives the question worth answering, the facts that are nailed down, the sources, threads worth pulling, and the traps. Modules 1 and 2 cover the first pour, 3 and 4 the second, 5 and 6 the third, 7 the fourth; Module 8 is the bottles. The current law is quoted from the regulation as it stood on 19 November 2026. The history comes from the statutes, two rectifiers' manuals, the 1906 hearings, Taft's decision and the Federal Register of the 1930s, all free online, with named historians as the second source. Bottle facts come from the makers' pages and front labels, named reviewers, and two readable price lists.

One caution about the story itself. "These rules exist because whiskey used to be faked" is well supported for "bonded" and "straight". It is weaker for the rest: the regulator's stated reason for new charred oak was uniform quality, the 1964 resolution was about imports and exports, and "barrel proof" was settled by a 1979 ruling. Where a claim rests on one secondary source, or on a source that could not be read, it is in **Not confirmed** at the end, with how a script should carry it.

## Module 1. What "bourbon" promises

_What does the law make a distiller do before the word "bourbon" can go on a label, and what does it leave out?_

### What's established

- **Where the rule is.** 27 CFR 5.143, "Whisky". Paragraph (a) defines the class; the table in paragraph (c), row (2), defines the type "Bourbon Whisky". Until 11 March 2022 the bourbon standard was 27 CFR 5.22(b)(1)(i).
- **Whisky, the class.** A distillate "from a fermented mash of any grain distilled at less than 95 percent alcohol by volume (190° proof)", "stored in oak barrels" and "bottled at not less than 40 percent alcohol by volume (80° proof)". The 80 proof floor comes from here.
- **Bourbon, the type.** "Fermented mash of not less than 51%" corn; distilled at "160° or less"; stored in "Charred new oak barrels at 125° or less"; neutral spirits permitted: "No"; coloring, flavoring and blending materials: "Yes, except for bourbon whisky."
- **Made in the United States.** "The word 'bourbon' may not be used to describe any whisky or whisky-based distilled spirits not distilled and aged in the United States" (5.143(b)). Section 5.1 defines the United States as the 50 States, the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico.
- **No minimum age.** The bourbon row names a barrel and an entry proof and no period of time. The only periods in the whisky table are the two years in the "straight" rows. TTB's 2024 worked example lists what makes a product bourbon with no age, then says it is straight bourbon because it was also aged "for no less than 2 years". The point rests on the regulation's silence plus that example, not on a sentence saying "no minimum".
- **Nothing added.** Most spirits may carry coloring, flavoring or blending materials up to 2.5 percent by volume (5.155(b)(2)). The whisky table carves bourbon out of that.
- **The barrel.** "Charred new oak barrels": no species, no country, no size. Before 2022 the word was "containers". In 2018 TTB proposed defining an oak barrel as a drum "of approximately 50 gallons"; after almost 700 opposing comments it dropped the idea in 2020. Part 5 has no definition of "oak barrel".
- **A second barrel.** A bourbon finished in another kind of barrel is no longer plain bourbon (5.155(c)(6)). TTB's 2024 example: "BOURBON WHISKY FINISHED IN SHERRY BARRELS", a distilled spirits specialty.
- **"Blended" changes everything.** "Blended Bourbon Whisky" is a separate type: at least 51 percent straight bourbon, up to 49 percent neutral spirits, coloring and flavoring allowed.
- **Kentucky is not required.** The regulation's own example is another state: "'New York Bourbon Whisky' must be distilled and aged in the State of New York" (5.143(b)).
- **What a state name means.** Every bourbon distilled in the United States must show its state of distillation (5.66(f)). A state name directly beside the type, "such as 'Kentucky bourbon whisky'", is allowed only if the whiskey was both distilled and aged there. The other ways: a "distilled by" statement with a single location, a "bottled by" address in the same state, or a separate "Distilled in" line.
- **Kentucky's own law.** Kentucky Revised Statutes 244.370: whiskey made in Kentucky must be aged at least one full year in oak before it is bottled there or leaves the state, unless "Kentucky" stays off the package. That is state law, not the federal standard.
- **4 May 1964.** Senate Concurrent Resolution 19, 88th Congress, printed at 78 Stat. 1208 under the heading "Bourbon whiskey designated as distinctive product of U.S." It is a concurrent resolution. The House Historian: it "was not a law and did not require the President's signature."
- **What the resolution says.** That "'Bourbon whiskey' is a distinctive product of the United States", and that it is "the sense of Congress" that agencies should "take appropriate action to prohibit the importation into the United States of whisky designated as 'Bourbon whiskey'."
- **What it does not say.** No "native spirit". No Kentucky. No recipe, proof or barrel: it points at the standard of identity already in the regulations. The Senate Finance Committee report (12 September 1963) explains the aim: an international trade federation had granted bourbon an appellation of origin in November 1960 but could not enforce it until the United States had a law or regulation of its own.
- **Who moved it.** Rep. John C. Watts of Kentucky introduced the House version on 24 January 1963; Sen. Thruston Morton of Kentucky reported the Senate's. Reid Mitenbuler's account (Slate, 2015, from his book): the force behind it was Lewis Rosenstiel of Schenley, who had overproduced for a Korean War shortage that never came, formed the Bourbon Institute, won the 1958 Forand Act (tax due at 20 years in bond, not eight) and wanted export markets.
- **"America's Native Spirit".** The phrase is in Senate Resolution 294 of 2 August 2007 (National Bourbon Heritage Month), which says Congress declared it in 1964. The 1964 text does not.

- › _S. Con. Res. 19, 78 Stat. 1208, on govinfo.gov: one page. Read it before saying what Congress did in 1964._

### Threads worth pulling

- Who spoke for and against the resolution on the floor? The Congressional Record for 1963 and 4 May 1964 was not read. Mitenbuler mentions a New York opponent acting for two heiresses with royalties from a Mexican "bourbon".
- Look at the back label of each bottle on the table and find the state of distillation: which of the four ways did the bottler use?

> **TRAPS**
> "Bourbon must come from Kentucky": no federal rule says so. "Bourbon must be aged two years": that is "straight". "Aged in new charred American white oak, 53 gallons": the rule says "Charred new oak barrels" and nothing more; makers' pages that say "white oak" are describing their own barrels. "The 1964 Act of Congress": it was a resolution, it defined nothing, and it never says "native spirit". "The only spirit distinctive to the United States": the regulation calls rye, wheat, malt, corn, straight and blended whiskies "distinctive products of the United States" too; what is unique to bourbon is the flat ban on the word for anything not distilled and aged here.

## Module 2. The second grain: wheat and rye

_After the corn, what does the second grain do, and who actually tells you the recipe?_

### What's established

- **The law only counts the corn.** At least 51 percent. The rest is the distiller's choice, and no rule requires the recipe to be published. Whisky Advocate notes that wheated bourbon is not an officially defined style. Bernie Lubbers calls rye and wheat the flavoring grains.
- **How wheat is described.** Maker's Mark: a soft, rounded character, fresh bread, sweetness at the front of the palate. Heaven Hill: wheat in place of rye makes a rounder, less spicy bourbon. Whisky Advocate: softer, slightly sweeter, less peppery. Lubbers adds the careful version: wheat is not a sweeter grain; it is less assertive than rye, so more of the corn and the vanilla shows through.
- **How rye is described.** Wild Turkey: a spicy counterpoint to the corn's sweetness. Lubbers: pepper, nutmeg, clove, caraway, cinnamon, and the taste of rye bread. Old Grand-Dad's maker: more rye for a spicy flavor and a peppery finish. Breaking Bourbon puts Old Grand-Dad's cinnamon spice down to its rye.
- **These are descriptions, not chemistry.** Every line above is how distillers and writers describe the two styles. Nothing read measured it.
- **"High rye" has no rule.** Lubbers says he and several distillers would draw the line at 18 percent rye or more. Old Grand-Dad prints "High Rye Mash Bill" on its label.
- **Maker's Mark: 70 percent corn, 16 percent soft red winter wheat, 14 percent malted barley.** Stated by the maker on its own site; Breaking Bourbon lists the same.
- **Old Grand-Dad: 63 percent corn, 27 percent rye, 10 percent malted barley.** Stated by Breaking Bourbon (2020); Michael Veach gives "about 27% rye" (2017). The maker says only "high-rye". The percentages are writers' figures.
- **Knob Creek: 75 percent corn, 13 percent rye, 12 percent malted barley.** Stated by Breaking Bourbon. The maker does not publish it. Beam's own page for Jim Beam Black gives 77, 13 and 10, which does not match.
- **Wild Turkey: 75 percent corn, 13 percent rye, 12 percent malted barley.** Stated by Breaking Bourbon, which says the distillery uses one recipe for all its bourbons. The maker names the grains and gives no percentages.
- **The Maker's Mark wheat story, as the company tells it.** Bill Samuels, Sr. wanted a whisky that was never bitter or sharp, chose wheat instead of the usual rye, and tested recipes by baking bread rather than distilling and aging each one. The company's timeline: the distillery started at its present site in 1952; in 1953 he burned the old family recipe and Margie Samuels created the name and the bottle design; the first bottle was finished and hand-dipped in 1958.
- **The Old Grand-Dad rye story, as the company tells it.** The brand is named for Basil Hayden Sr. by his grandson, Colonel R. B. Hayden, and was first bottled in 1882. The maker's own wording on the rye is hedged: he "perhaps may have preferred rye". The link is the brand's tradition, not a documented recipe.

### Go to the source

- › _Maker's Mark, "How It's Made" and "Our Story", makersmark.com: the only maker in the lineup that publishes its recipe._
- › _Bernie Lubbers, "The Master Distiller's 'Rule of Thumb' on Flavor", Distillery Trail, 19 October 2015: the plainest account of what the small grains do._
- › _Nick Beiter, "Old Grand-Dad Bonded Bourbon", Breaking Bourbon, 5 March 2020, and Michael Veach, "Tasting Notes: Old Grand Dad Bonded", 9 August 2017: the source of the 27 percent._
- › _Chuck Cowdery, "That Old Story About Pappy, Maker's Mark And Old Fitzgerald", 27 September 2019: a writer tracing a story back to himself._

### Threads worth pulling

- Taste pours 1 and 2 side by side before anyone is told which is which. The words to listen for are soft, sweet and bready on one side, pepper, cinnamon and spice on the other.
- Did Pappy Van Winkle give Bill Samuels Sr. the Old Fitzgerald recipe? Cowdery heard it from a friend who had worked at Stitzel-Weller, says Bill Samuels Jr. largely confirmed it, and says he himself may be the story's only original source.

> **TRAPS**
> Only one of the four recipes comes from its maker. Say "reported by" for the other three. Don't say wheat "is sweeter" or rye "is spicy" as chemistry: say that is how distillers describe them. And the two pours differ in more than grain: 90 proof against 100, different distilleries, different ages.

## Module 3. Before the rules: the rectifiers

_What was in the glass before a label had to tell the truth, and how much of the horror story is documented?_

### What's established

- **1853.** Pierre Lacour's trade manual, "The Manufacture of Liquors, Wines and Cordials Without the Aid of Distillation", prints recipes for "Old Bourbon Whiskey" with no bourbon in it. One (p. 107): "Starch filtered clean spirit, one hundred gallons; water, twenty-five gallons; strong tea, one gallon; tincture grains of paradise, one gallon; thirty drops wintergreen oil, dissolved in one ounce alcohol." "Old Bourbon for Bottling" adds honey, peach juice, "sulphuric acid, one ounce", and is colored with cochineal and burnt sugar.
- **1853, creosote.** Lacour documents it for imitation Scotch and Irish: "Oil of tar, or creasote, is used for flavoring malt whiskey, or well cleaned corn whiskey, in imitation of Irish or Scotch whiskeys" (p. 46).
- **1853, tobacco.** Lacour himself calls it a legend: "A popular error of the day has it that the above-mentioned articles are used in the manufacture of liquors for giving an artificial strength" (p. 198; the chapter heading lists tobacco, caustic potassa, red pepper, aqua fortis and oil of vitriol).
- **1885.** Joseph Fleischman's "The Art of Blending and Compounding Liquors and Wines" gives the rectifier's price ladder in eleven grades. Grade 1 is spirits, water, sugar coloring and beading oil. Grade 2 adds "Oil of Rye, or Oil of Bourbon, making the result Rye Whiskey, or Bourbon, as the case may be" (pp. 22 to 23). "The first five of the foregoing grades are simply spirits." Grades 6 to 10 blend spirits with rising shares of named Kentucky whiskeys. Grade 11, "the finest of all grades, as it contains no spirits", is a blend of straight whiskeys with prune juice.
- **1885, the additives.** Prune juice, peach juice, tea extract and raisin extract "are all used in every rectifying establishment" and are "harmless" (p. 17). The cheap chemical flavors are another matter: "Bourbon Oil" is distilled from fusel oil, sulphuric acid, sulphate of copper and other salts, and he calls such compounds "poisonous in their character" but "frequently used". Beading oil, which fakes the bead of a strong whiskey, is "deleterious".
- **1885, the drinker.** "the thirsty individual who takes his glass of whiskey at any of even the first-class public bars, saloons or parlors, does not know what he drinks" (p. 7). "The moment a barrel of liquor leaves the bonded warehouse, the first thing thought of, and done, is to reduce its cost" (pp. 7 to 8).
- **It was lawful.** The revenue act of 1 July 1862 defined a rectifier, including anyone who mixes spirits "with any other materials for sale under the name of whisky", and charged for the license (quoted by Wiley, 1919). Rectifying was a licensed, taxed trade.
- **The scale.** President Taft, 1909, after reading the hearing record: neutral-spirits whisky "now constitutes and for thirty years last passed has constituted, perhaps 75 per cent of all the whisky sold". It is his estimate, not a count.
- **Barrel versus bottle.** The distiller's package was the barrel (Veach). The Kentucky Historical Society: distillers sold in barrels, casks and kegs, and drinkers got bottled whiskey from bars and saloons that bought wholesale and filled generic bottles. Whoever held the barrel between distillery and glass decided what was in it.
- **Old Forester, the company's claim.** Its timeline says that in 1870 George Garvin Brown batched bourbon from three distilleries (Mattingly, Mellwood, Atherton), sealed it only in glass bottles, and made the "First Bottled Bourbon". By that account Brown was a merchant batching other distillers' whiskey.
- **Old Forester, the documents.** The earliest advertisement found is in the Dallas Daily Herald, 5 July 1881, p. 5: "Old Forester Bourbon, Kentucky's finest whisky, is bottled by us only", signed Brown, Thompson & Co., Louisville. Drew Hannush reports no newspaper mention before 1881 and an 1896 lawsuit claiming use of the name for "over twenty years". No 1870 document was found.
- **Old Forester, the historians.** Veach: bourbon was bottled before 1870, and what Old Forester can claim is to be the first bourbon sold only by the bottle. The Kentucky Historical Society dates the sealed bottles to "the 1870s".

### The people the brochure leaves out

- **Enslaved workers.** Fred Minnick (2017): before emancipation the biggest names in Kentucky whiskey benefited from slave labor. Elijah Craig owned 32 enslaved people by the 1800 tax records; at the site of today's Woodford Reserve the Pepper family held 13 men and 12 women. Minnick states the limit of the record himself: it suggests enslaved people rolled barrels and gathered grain, and it is only assumed they did more; who did the distilling is not yet shown. He also reports that sellers at auctions noted distiller-trained people, and that the skill raised the price.
- **Catherine Carpenter.** Minnick: Catherine Spears Carpenter Frye wrote the earliest known sour mash recipe, dated 1818 and held at the Kentucky Historical Society, where her family's tax lists include an enslaved man, "Little Bob", valued at $350. The earliest known written sour mash recipe is a woman's, and her household held a man as property.
- **Nearest Green** belongs to Tennessee whiskey, not bourbon: an enslaved distiller in Lincoln County who taught Jack Daniel, by History.com's summary of later research. Known here at second hand only.

### Threads worth pulling

- Kentucky newspaper advertisements naming enslaved distillers: a search found none in the time available. The Kentucky Historical Society's Carpenter papers are the place to start.
- "A Plot Against the People" (1911) is the rectifiers' side at book length, on the Internet Archive. It was not read.

> **TRAPS**
> "Rectifiers poisoned people with tobacco, iodine and creosote." Documented in the manuals: burnt sugar, prune and peach juice, tea, honey, glycerine, cochineal, wintergreen oil, a little sulphuric acid, fusel-oil "Bourbon Oil" and beading oil. Creosote is documented for fake Scotch and Irish. Tobacco is called a "popular error" by the 1853 manual itself, and survives as a recipe Veach says he has seen and does not cite. Iodine was not found anywhere. And "rectified" covered everything from fraud to honest blending: Fleischman's best grade is simply good straight whiskeys mixed.

## Module 4. 1897: bottled in bond

_What exactly did the 1897 Act put on the bottle, who wanted it, and what does "bonded" promise now?_

### What's established

- **3 March 1897.** "An Act To allow the bottling of distilled spirits in bond", chapter 379, 29 Stat. 626 to 628. "Approved, March 3, 1897."
- **Supervised bottling.** Tax-stamped spirits in a distillery's bonded warehouse could be bottled there "under the supervision of a United States storekeeper". A named federal employee stood in the room.
- **One distillery, one season, nothing added.** Spirits "produced at the same distillery by the same distiller" may be mingled, "but nothing herein shall authorize or permit any mingling of different products, or of the same products of different distilling seasons, or the addition or the subtraction of any substance or material". Spring runs "from January to July", fall "from July to January".
- **100 proof.** The distiller may reduce "by the addition of pure water only to one hundred per centum proof" for domestic use.
- **The stamp.** Over the mouth of every bottle, an "engraved strip stamp" bearing the proof, the registered distillery number, the State and district, "the real name of the actual bona fide distiller", the year and season of entry into bond, and the date of bottling.
- **The maker's name.** "no trade-marks shall be put upon any bottle unless the real name of the actual bona fide distiller shall also be placed conspicuously on said bottle."
- **Four years, by cross-reference.** The Act never says "four years". It bars bottling until the regauge period in section 50 of the Act of 28 August 1894 has run, and that section sets it at four years from entry.
- **Green is not in the statute.** The color comes from advertising a few years later. Old Taylor, 1909: "GUARANTEED BY ITS LITTLE GREEN STAMP over the cork of each bottle of OLD TAYLOR whiskey, BOTTLED IN BOND, four essential facts": the distiller's name, the strength, the full measure and the age.
- **Who pushed it.** Veach: the straight distillers saw their chance when a Kentuckian, John G. Carlisle, became Secretary of the Treasury under Grover Cleveland, with E. H. Taylor Jr. and James E. Pepper for the Act and George Garvin Brown and Isaac Wolfe Bernheim against. Cowdery calls Taylor the father of the Act. A hostile witness agrees on the motive: W. M. Hough, counsel for the wholesalers and rectifiers, told the House in 1906 that the law passed when some distillers were trying "to wipe out the middleman", and that Carlisle "was interested in the welfare of the Kentucky distillers". In 1909 Carlisle and Edmund W. Taylor appeared together for the straight distillers.
- **What it did not promise.** Hough, 1906: with a bonded bottle you know whose it is and how old, but "you know absolutely nothing about whether it is fit to drink or unfit to drink". Cowdery, from the other side: a guarantee of quality was never the intent; it became one in practice.
- **It was slow.** Veach: about another decade passed before drinkers valued bonded whiskey, and after 1898 many rectifiers bought distilleries to have one of their own.
- **1979.** TTB (2018 notice): the Act "was repealed by the Distilled Spirits Tax Revision Act of 1979", and the predecessor agency kept the labeling rules "because consumers continued to place value on these terms on labels."
- **Today, 27 CFR 5.88.** "Bond", "bonded", "bottled in bond" or "aged in bond" may be used only if the spirits are of the same kind; "Distilled in the same distilling season" "by the same distiller at the same distillery"; "Stored for at least 4 years in wooden containers"; unaltered except by filtration, chill proofing or other physical treatment; reduced only with pure water; and "Bottled at 50 percent alcohol by volume (100 degrees of proof)."
- **A season is half a year.** 1 January to 30 June, or 1 July to 31 December (5.1).
- **What the label must carry.** The "real name of the distillery or the trade name under which the distiller distilled and warehoused the spirits", and the numbers of the distilled spirits plants where it was distilled and bottled (5.88(c)).
- **Not only whiskey.** Section 5.88 speaks of "distilled spirits" and has storage rules for vodka and gin.

- › _Chuck Cowdery, "Happy Birthday, Bottled-in-Bond Act", 3 March 2020, and "Bottled-in-Bond: An Old Law for Modern Times", 25 January 2023._

### Threads worth pulling

- Find the DSP numbers on the back of pour 2. They say which plant distilled it and which bottled it, which nothing else on the bottle does.
- The Congressional Record for the 54th Congress and the Louisville Courier-Journal of March 1897 would show who sponsored the bill and whether anyone then called it consumer protection.

> **TRAPS**
> "The first consumer protection law in the United States": the FDA's chronology lists a vaccine law in 1813 and the Drug Importation Act of 1848 against adulterated drugs. The Act's own title is a revenue title. Cowdery's narrower wording, "first 'truth in labeling' legislation", can be said with his name on it. "Bonded means made in one year": one season. "At least 100 proof": exactly 100. "Aged in a government-supervised warehouse": true in 1897, not in the rule today. "Old Forester's founder fought for pure whiskey law": Veach puts George Garvin Brown on the rectifiers' side. And Taylor's own part rests on historians, not on a document found.

## Module 5. "Straight", from Wiley and Taft to the 1938 standards

_How did "straight" become a protected word, and when were today's rules first written down?_

### What's established

- **February 1906.** At the House hearings on the pure-food bills Dr. Harvey Wiley mixed an "aged" whisky from alcohol, caramel and flavoring in front of the committee: "I am a rectifier now; I have taken out my license". He also said that many people prefer such goods "and they ought to have them". His demand was about the name on the label.
- **30 June 1906.** The Food and Drugs Act. It makes a food misbranded if offered "under the distinctive name of another article", with an exception for labeled compounds, imitations and blends. It does not mention whisky.
- **1 December 1906.** Food Inspection Decision 45: a mix of bourbon and neutral spirits "can not legally be labeled either 'blended whiskies' or 'blended whisky'".
- **10 April 1907.** Theodore Roosevelt, adopting his Attorney General's opinion: "Straight whisky will be labeled as such. A mixture of two or more straight whiskies will be labeled 'Blended whisky' or 'whiskies.'" Whisky mixed with ethyl alcohol is a compound, and "Imitation whisky will be labeled as such." This is the high-water mark for the straight distillers.
- **1907 to 1909.** Two federal district courts uphold that position; the rectifiers ask for a rehearing; Taft's Solicitor General takes a record of more than 1,200 printed pages from 8 April 1909.
- **27 December 1909.** Taft's decision, signed that day and given to the press on the 26th (the Louisville Courier-Journal printed the full text on the 27th).
- **What Taft decided.** "Whisky" is the broad term: for more than a hundred years it "has included all potable liquor distilled from grain", so to limit it to straight whisky is "a perversion of the pure food act". Straight distillers may brand their barrels "bourbon" or "rye" and add that it is aged in wood. Makers of whisky from neutral spirits must "use a brand which shall show exactly the kind of whisky they are selling". A mix of the two is "a blend". Spirit from molasses is rum, not whisky.
- **Why he required it.** "It is undoubtedly true that the liquor trade has been disgracefully full of frauds upon the public by false labels". And: "It injures no man's lawful business, because it only insists upon the statement of the truth in the label."
- **Who won.** Taft's summary: "The straight whisky men are relieved from all future attempt to pass off neutral spirits whisky as straight whisky." Wiley's, ten years later: Taft "decided all questions in favor of the rectifiers". What lasted for a bourbon label is that "straight" and "bourbon" became descriptions a neutral-spirit whisky could not carry.
- **16 February 1910.** Food Inspection Decision 113 turns the decision into labels, and is looser than Taft's text: all unmixed grain spirits are "entitled to the name whisky without qualification". "Imitation Whisky" for non-grain spirit comes from here, not from Taft.
- **29 August 1935.** The Federal Alcohol Administration Act, 49 Stat. 977: the labeling authority the later regulations cite.
- **January 1936.** The Federal Alcohol Administration issues Regulations No. 5. For American whiskey made on or after **1 July 1936**, only whiskey in charred new oak could be called "straight" or claim an age.
- **The stated reason.** Administrator W. S. Alexander, 1 October 1937: "American type whiskey stored in new oak containers matures more satisfactorily and more uniformly than whiskey stored in second-hand barrels".
- **It wrote down existing practice.** Of about 8.9 million barrels in bond on 1 July 1937, "approximately 3 per cent" were post-July-1936 whiskey in reused barrels.
- **1 March 1938.** Amendment No. 5: bourbon whiskey is "distilled at not exceeding 160 proof from a fermented mash of not less than 51%" corn "and, if produced on or after March 1, 1938, stored in charred new oak containers". From that date whiskey in used barrels could not be called bourbon at all.
- **"Straight" today.** The same recipe and proofs, stored "for a minimum of 2 years", with no coloring, flavoring or blending materials (5.143(c), row 5). Straight bourbons from two states mixed together are a different type, "Blended Straight Bourbon Whiskies".
- **"Straight" is optional.** "The use of the word 'straight' is a further designation of a type, and is optional." Before the 2020 rule, policy required it on whiskies aged more than two years.
- **Age statements, 27 CFR 5.74.** Any whisky "aged less than 4 years" must carry one, unless it is bottled in bond. The age is "the age of the youngest whisky in the product". "it is permissible to understate the age of a product, but overstatements of age are prohibited." For bourbon, age means time in charred new oak barrels (5.1); time in a finishing cask does not count.

## Module 6. The barrel and the years

_What does the barrel do to clear spirit, and what does a number of years on the label cost the distiller?_

### What's established

- **New make is clear.** Heaven Hill: the raw distillate gets its flavor, color, character and aroma from years in a charred barrel, and charring creates the compounds that turn clear distillate brown. Wild Turkey: the longer in the barrel, the darker.
- **The color is all barrel.** Nothing may be added to bourbon (Module 1), so the color in every glass on the table came from wood.
- **What char is.** Cowdery (Whisky Advocate, 2018): how long the barrel burns, from a few seconds to a minute or more, sets the char depth, and the charred wood acts as a filter. Whisky Advocate (2024): the char is carbon; most of the flavor sits in the "red layer" just under it. Char levels by burn time: No. 1, up to 15 seconds; No. 2, 20 to 30; No. 3, 30 to 35; No. 4, up to 60, the "alligator" char.
- **On the table.** Maker's Mark says it uses a "Level 3 char"; Wild Turkey says No. 4 on everything.
- **Flavors from oak.** Chris Morris of Brown-Forman (Whisky Advocate, 2017): charring breaks hemicellulose into sugars that caramelize under the char, and lignin breaks down into vanillin. Heaven Hill: water dissolves the wood sugars that carry vanilla and caramel; alcohol extracts tannins, which dry the palate. Safe wording: vanillin gives vanilla, caramelized wood sugars give caramel and toffee, oak lactones are part of the oak character.
- **Evaporation.** Heaven Hill gives its own average: 6 percent in the first year, which it puts down to soakage, then 3 percent every year after. The first-year figure is partly dry wood soaking up spirit. For contrast, Paul Hughes puts Scotland's losses at about 1 to 2 percent a year.
- **Proof can rise.** Hughes: water and alcohol both leave the cask; where the air is humid, alcohol goes faster and the proof falls; where it is dry, water goes faster and the proof rises. He names humidity as the main driver. Heaven Hill: barrels on hot, dry upper floors can reach 150 proof, and barrels on cool lower floors can fall to the low 90s, from an entry proof of 125.
- **Entry proof.** The legal ceiling is 125. Maker's Mark has kept an entry proof of 110 since 1954 (Whisky Advocate, July 2026). Wild Turkey long used 107, raised it to 110 in 2004 and 115 in 2006, and does not disclose today's figure (same article).
- **What an age statement costs: Knob Creek.** The 9 year statement was on the label from 1992 to late 2016. Cowdery, 22 September 2016, quoting master distiller Fred Noe: with demand growing, the number would come off so the taste could stay the same. Cowdery, 13 June 2019: the statement would return on a new label early the next year. The Whiskey Wash, 29 June 2020: the 9 year is back, with the age on the front label. With the number on, every drop must be at least that old (Module 5).
- **"70 percent of the flavour comes from the barrel."** No one was found who measured it. Wild Turkey's site says "up to 70%", crediting unnamed experts. Bernie Lubbers's rule of thumb from talking to distillers is 50 percent for maturation. Michael Veach puts it at 55 to 60 percent and says 70 is true of very old whiskey.

## Module 7. Words that promise something and words that do not

_Which words on the label are defined in law, which are only bound by the rule against lying, and what does a few drops of water do?_

### What's established

- **Proof.** "The ethyl alcohol content of a liquid at 60 degrees Fahrenheit, stated as twice the percentage of ethyl alcohol by volume" (5.1). Alcohol by volume is the mandatory statement; proof "may be stated" beside it (5.65).
- **Barrel proof, 27 CFR 5.87(a).** "The term 'barrel proof' or 'cask strength' may be used to refer to distilled spirits stored in wood barrels only when the bottling proof is not more than two degrees lower than the proof of the spirits when the spirits are dumped from the barrels."
- **Original proof, 5.87(b).** "Original proof", "original barrel proof", "original cask strength" or "entry proof" may be used only if "the proof of the spirits entered into the barrel and the proof of the bottled spirits are the same." A different promise: the strength it went in at, not the strength it came out at.
- **Where the rule came from.** ATF Ruling 79-9 defined "Barrel Proof" as not more than two degrees below the proof at tax gauging. Since 11 March 2022 the definition is in the regulation, measured at dumping, and covers "cask strength" too.
- **Small batch, single barrel, handmade, handcrafted, craft, sour mash.** None of these words appears anywhere in 27 CFR part 5. "Reserve" appears only in "[Reserved]" placeholders. A search of all of Title 27 returns nothing for "small batch", "single barrel", "handmade" or "handcrafted". TTB's 2007 labeling manual and its 2024 chart do not contain them either.
- **How firm that is.** It rests on the silence of the regulation and of TTB's guidance, both read, plus two trade articles. No statement by TTB itself that the terms are undefined was found, and nothing read shows how its label specialists treat a "single barrel" claim.
- **What still binds them.** Labels "may not contain any statement or representation that is false or untrue in any particular" (5.102), and "All claims, whether implicit or explicit, must have a reasonable basis in fact" (5.122(b)(2)). An undefined word is still a factual claim.
- **"Handmade" in court.** A law firm's alert of June 2015 reports that a federal judge in Florida dismissed a suit over "handmade" on Maker's Mark on the ground that no one could believe a bourbon sold at that volume is made by hand, while other courts let similar claims proceed. The orders were not read.
- **On the table.** Maker's Mark prints "Handmade since 1953". Knob Creek has "Small Batch" embossed in the glass. Rare Breed's label says "Barrel Proof" and "crafted in small batches". Only one of those phrases is defined.
- **Water: the 2017 study.** Karlsson and Friedman, "Dilution of whisky - the molecular perspective", Scientific Reports, 17 August 2017. A computer simulation of water, ethanol and one flavor molecule, guaiacol. Up to 45 percent alcohol the molecule sits mainly at the surface of the liquid; at 59 percent or higher it is "driven to the bulk".
- **What it does not show.** No whisky was poured and no one tasted anything. Guaiacol is a smoky phenol that the paper itself says is "much more common in Scottish whiskies than in American or Irish ones". Its stated conclusion is about dilution "prior to bottling"; that other flavor molecules behave the same way is the authors' "may apply". Rare Breed, at 58.4 percent, sits at the paper's cask-strength mark.
- **A simple method.** Lew Bryson, "The Fundamentals of Tasting Whisky" (Whisky Advocate, 2016), in outline:
- Pour half an ounce.
- Lift the glass until you can smell it, hold it there, breathe normally, and ask what smells like this. Rest ten seconds and go back.
- Sip, hold it on the tongue, breathe, swallow. Hot, bitter, sweet? Thin or full?
- Sip again and move it slowly around the mouth.
- If it is too hot, or gives little aroma, add a few drops of water and swirl.
- **Other voices.** Heaven Hill's guide: taste from lowest proof to highest, which this lineup already does. Wild Turkey's guide: roll the glass rather than swirl hard, let the first sip temper the palate, use room-temperature water. Whisky Advocate: try it neat first; cask strength often benefits from water.

### Go to the source

- › _27 CFR 5.87, 5.102 and 5.122 on ecfr.gov, and ATF Ruling 79-9 on ttb.gov: the defined words and the rule that covers the rest._
- › _Karlsson and Friedman, "Dilution of whisky - the molecular perspective", Scientific Reports 7, 6489 (2017), open access on nature.com. Read the abstract and the discussion before quoting it._
- › _Lew Bryson, "The Fundamentals of Tasting Whisky", Whisky Advocate, 1 June 2016._

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> Say "a simulation suggests", never "science proves", about water. Say "no federal definition was found", not "TTB says it means nothing", about small batch. "Barrel proof" does not mean straight from the barrel: the rule allows two degrees. And "full proof" and "114" on other bottles are not barrel proof (Module 8).

## Claims that don't hold up

- **"Bourbon has to be made in Kentucky."** The United States, by 27 CFR 5.143(b). The regulation's example is New York.
- **"Bourbon has to be two years old."** Plain bourbon has no minimum. Two is "straight"; four is bonded, and where the age statement stops being compulsory.
- **"Congress declared bourbon America's Native Spirit in 1964."** The 1964 text says "distinctive product of the United States". The phrase is from a 2007 Senate resolution.
- **"The Bottled-in-Bond Act says four years, and requires a green stamp."** Four years is there by cross-reference to an 1894 Act; the stamp's color is not in the statute.
- **"Bottled in bond was a guarantee of quality."** It guaranteed who made it, when, how strong, and that nothing was added.
- **"Taft saved bourbon."** He widened "whisky" and kept "straight", "bourbon" and "rye" for the unmixed article. The straight distillers' champion thought they had lost.
- **"Elijah Craig invented bourbon."** The claim traces to one unsourced sentence in Collins' History of Kentucky (1874), which names neither Craig nor barrels. Cowdery and Veach both find no contemporary support.
- **"Bourbon is named for Bourbon County"** or **"for Bourbon Street"**, stated flat. Robert Moss argues the county from 1820s advertisements; Michael Veach argues the street and calls it his theory.
- **"Old Forester was the first bottled bourbon, in 1870."** The company's date and phrase. Earliest advertisement found: 1881.
- **"Small batch means under so many barrels."** Any number is a company's own practice.
- **"Barrel proof means nothing was added."** Up to two degrees of proof below the barrel is allowed.
- **"Wiley resigned over the Taft decision."** The decision was December 1909; the FDA says he left in 1912. The link is one historian's reading.
