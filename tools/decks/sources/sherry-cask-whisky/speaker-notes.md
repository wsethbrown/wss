## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] Sherry Cask Whisky

Tonight is about the sherry butt. A butt is a 500-litre oak cask. Sherry used to be shipped from Spain to Britain in butts, and when the wine merchant had bottled the sherry he was left with an empty cask. A lot of those casks were sold to Scottish distilleries. That is how Scotch came to be aged in sherry casks. In the 1980s the casks stopped coming, and distillers had to find another way to get them.

We have four bottles from three distilleries: Glenmorangie, Macallan and GlenDronach. One was aged only in bourbon barrels. One was finished in sherry casks. Two were aged in sherry casks from the start.

## [SLIDE 2] Scotch is aged in casks that held something else first

By law, Scotch has to spend at least three years in oak casks in Scotland. The casks can hold no more than 700 litres. The law doesn't say the casks have to be new, and very few are. Scottish distillers buy casks that have already held something else.

Most come from one of two places. The common one is the American bourbon barrel, which holds about 200 litres. Distillers use it as it is, or take it apart and rebuild it a bit bigger. The bigger one is called a hogshead and holds about 250 litres. The other is the sherry butt, which holds about 500 litres.

Whisky is as clear as water when it comes off the still. All of its natural colour comes from the cask, and so does a lot of its flavour.

## [SLIDE 3] Britain was sherry's biggest customer in the 1800s

Sherry is wine from around the town of Jerez, in the south of Spain. The English have been drinking it since Shakespeare's time, when they called it sack. In the 1800s Britain bought far more of it than anyone else.

You can see that in the names of the sherry companies. Sandeman was founded in London in 1790 by George Sandeman, who was a Scot from Perth. Harvey's was a wine merchant in Bristol. González Byass gets its second name from Robert Blake Byass. He was the company's English agent, and he became a partner. Williams and Humbert was started in Jerez in 1877 by Alexander Williams and his brother-in-law, Arthur Humbert.

## [SLIDE 4] Sherry crossed to Britain in the cask

Sherry was shipped in wooden casks, mostly butts. They went to Bristol, London, Liverpool, Leith and Glasgow. The British wine merchants bottled the sherry themselves and sold it under their own names.

That left the merchant with an empty cask. It cost money to send it back to Spain, so he sold it cheaply to someone nearby. Whisky distillers and blenders were some of the buyers.

These casks were made for shipping. Many were new or nearly new, and the sherry might only have been in them for a few months. Sherry is aged in much older casks at the bodega, which is what a sherry winery is called. Those casks mostly stayed in Spain.

## [SLIDE 5] 1915: whisky must spend two years in bond before sale

For most of the 1800s a lot of Scotch was sold young, often within months of being made. So the cask mattered less than it does now. We also don't know how much of it was aged in sherry casks. Distillers used whatever casks they could get. Some had held rum, port or wine, and the records rarely say which.

During the First World War David Lloyd George was Chancellor of the Exchequer. He thought heavy drinking was slowing down war production. One of the laws that followed was passed in May 1915. It said whisky had to sit in a bonded warehouse for at least two years before it could be sold. In May 1916 that went up to three years, and it is still three years today. From then on every bottle of Scotch had spent years in a cask, so the cask mattered much more.

## [SLIDE 6] From 1938, bourbon goes into new barrels

The other big supply of casks came from America. Prohibition ended in 1933, and the American government then wrote rules for what could be called bourbon. From 1938 the rules said straight bourbon had to be aged in new charred oak barrels. So a bourbon distiller can use a barrel once, and then it has to go somewhere else.

People often say the rule was written to protect coopers and the timber trade. There is no good evidence for that.

American distilleries now had a steady supply of used barrels they couldn't refill. Scottish distillers started buying them. Most Scotch is now aged in bourbon barrels.

## [SLIDE 7] An ex-bourbon barrel and a sherry butt

A bourbon barrel is American white oak. It holds about 200 litres and it is charred on the inside. It has held bourbon for anything from two years to more than ten. American oak tends to give flavours like vanilla, coconut and honey, and a pale gold colour.

A sherry butt is bigger, about 500 litres, so less wood touches each litre of whisky. It is usually toasted, not charred. It is often European oak, which has more tannin. A good many sherry casks are American oak, though, because the bodegas use a lot of it. Whisky from sherry casks tends to be darker. People describe dried fruit, nuts and spice, and a drier feel from the tannin.

None of this is a rule. The age of the cask matters, and so does how many times it has been filled and what whisky went into it.

## [SLIDE 8] Pour 1 of 4: Glenmorangie The Original

First pour. This is Glenmorangie The Original, twelve years old. It comes from Tain, in the northern Highlands. The distillery dates from 1843. The French luxury group LVMH has owned it since 2004.

For decades The Original was ten years old. In 2024 Glenmorangie replaced it with this twelve-year-old. It is still 40 percent.

It is aged only in bourbon barrels. Glenmorangie says it picks its own oak in the Ozark forests of Missouri and has the wood dried in the open air. It then lends the finished barrels to American distillers, who fill them with whiskey before the barrels come to Tain.

Look at the colour, and keep a little in your glass to compare with the other three.

## [SLIDE 9] Sherry must be bottled in the Jerez region

In the 1970s and 80s the sherry producers wanted sherry to be bottled in Spain. That way the bottling work and the money from it stayed there.

It happened in stages. From the early 1970s the body that regulates sherry pushed the companies to bottle it in Spain. In the 1980s it became a rule. Some accounts date the rule to 1981 and others to 1986, so the slide shows both. Sherry was no longer shipped to Britain in butts, and the cheap empty casks stopped coming.

That was a serious problem for the distillers who relied on sherry casks. The bodegas had little reason to sell the old casks they aged their wine in.

## [SLIDE 10] Two ways round the shortage

Distillers dealt with it in two main ways. Glenmorangie Lasanta, pour two, is an example of the first. Macallan 12 Sherry Oak, pour three, is an example of the second.

The first way is to use fewer sherry casks. The whisky spends most of its life in bourbon barrels and is moved into a sherry cask for the last part. That is called a finish. Glenmorangie became known for finishes in the 1990s. Its first was finished in port casks. Bill Lumsden joined Glenmorangie in 1994 and became the person most associated with them. Balvenie did it earlier. David Stewart at Balvenie finished a whisky in oloroso sherry casks in 1982. It went on sale in 1983 as Balvenie Classic, and in 1993 the same idea became Balvenie DoubleWood.

The second way is to go to Jerez and pay to have casks built and filled with sherry for you. Macallan had built its name on sherry casks, and it is the best-known distillery that does this.

## [SLIDE 11] Pour 2 of 4: Glenmorangie Lasanta

Second pour. This is a finish. Glenmorangie Lasanta is twelve years old and comes from the same distillery as the first pour. Glenmorangie says it spends about ten years in bourbon barrels and then two more in oloroso and Pedro Ximénez sherry casks.

Taste it next to the Original. They are the same age and from the same distillery. The Original stayed in bourbon barrels for all twelve years. The Lasanta spent its last two in sherry casks, so most of the difference comes from those two years. Look at the colour first. Then see if you get more dried fruit, and if it feels drier at the end.

## [SLIDE 12] Seasoned casks, made to order in Jerez

To make a cask to order, a cooperage builds a new cask. The distiller chooses European or American oak and how much the inside is toasted. The cask goes to a bodega, which fills it with sherry. That is usually a young oloroso or a Pedro Ximénez, and it stays in the cask for anything from several months to a couple of years. Then the cask is emptied and shipped whole to Scotland. This is called seasoning the cask. A lot of the sherry that comes out is distilled into brandy or made into vinegar.

In 2015 the body that regulates sherry started certifying these casks. A certified "Sherry Cask" has to hold real sherry from the region for at least twelve months. Its president has said the average is closer to eighteen months.

For much of the 1900s some casks were treated with paxarette, which is a dark, sweet wine concentrate. It gave the cask a sherry character quickly. That stopped around the end of the 1980s.

## [SLIDE 13] A solera cask and a seasoned cask

People say "sherry cask" for two different things.

A solera cask belongs to a bodega. Sherry is aged in rows of casks. The wine for bottling is taken from the oldest row. That row is topped up from the next oldest, and so on back to the youngest. The casks stay in use for decades, sometimes much longer. By then the wood is soaked through and has little oak flavour left to give. Bodegas rarely sell them, and they cost a lot when they do.

A seasoned cask was built to be sold to a distiller. It usually held sherry for a year or two, and the wood still has plenty of oak flavour to give. Nearly every sherry cask filled with Scotch today is a seasoned cask.

## [SLIDE 14] Pour 3 of 4: Macallan 12 Sherry Oak

Third pour. Macallan 12 Sherry Oak. The distillery is at Easter Elchies, near Craigellachie on the River Spey. A farmer called Alexander Reid took out the licence in 1824.

Macallan has its casks made to order. The company says this whisky is aged in "predominantly sherry seasoned European oak casks". Older bottles said the casks were all sherry casks. Macallan says it chooses the oak in northern Spain and has the casks built and seasoned in Jerez.

It is the most expensive bottle tonight, often over $70. Compare it with the Lasanta. This one has been in sherry casks all its life. The Lasanta was in them for two years. Look at the colour first. Scotch is allowed to have caramel colouring added. Macallan says the colour of this one is all natural, so what you see comes from the casks.

## [SLIDE 15] Oloroso and Pedro Ximénez

Oloroso is made from the Palomino grape. All the sugar is fermented out, so it is dry. Then spirit is added to bring it up to at least 17 percent alcohol. Some sherries age under a layer of yeast, and that yeast can't grow at 17 percent. So oloroso ages in contact with the air and turns brown and nutty.

Pedro Ximénez is a different grape. The bunches are laid out in the sun until they are nearly raisins. The wine made from them is almost black, thick and very sweet. It is often called PX for short.

In whisky, oloroso casks are usually said to give nuts and dry spice. PX casks are said to give something darker and sweeter, more like raisins. It varies with the distillery and the age of the cask.

## [SLIDE 16] GlenDronach, 1826 to now

GlenDronach is near Forgue in Aberdeenshire. James Allardice founded it in 1826.

In 1960 the company behind Teacher's blended whisky bought it, and much of what it made went into Teacher's. Teacher's later became part of a bigger group called Allied. Allied closed GlenDronach in 1996 and reopened it in 2002.

In 2008 it was sold to a company led by Billy Walker. He made GlenDronach a sherry cask distillery, using oloroso and Pedro Ximénez casks. In 2016 it was sold to Brown-Forman, the Kentucky company that makes Old Forester and Woodford Reserve.

## [SLIDE 17] Pour 4 of 4: GlenDronach 12

Last pour. GlenDronach 12, at 43 percent. The distillery says it is aged for at least twelve years in Pedro Ximénez and oloroso casks, with no caramel added.

Like the Macallan, this has been in sherry casks all its life. Some of them are PX casks. Put it next to the Macallan. Both are twelve years old and 43 percent, and both were aged in sherry casks. See if you find this one darker or sweeter.

## [SLIDE 18] Reading the cask on a Scotch label

"Ex-bourbon" means barrels that held American whiskey. "American oak" only tells you the wood. It doesn't tell you what the cask held before.

"Sherry cask" or "sherry oak" today almost always means a seasoned cask. It doesn't tell you if the oak was European or American.

"Matured in" means the whisky spent all or most of its life in that kind of cask. "Finished" or "extra matured" means it was moved there for the last part.

"Oloroso" and "PX" tell you which sherry was in the cask.

"Natural colour" means the producer says no caramel was added. Scotch is allowed to have caramel colouring, so a dark Scotch that doesn't say "natural colour" may have been coloured.
