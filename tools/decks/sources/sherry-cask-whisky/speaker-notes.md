## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] Sherry Cask Whisky

Tonight is about one kind of cask: the sherry butt, a 500-litre oak cask that used to arrive in Britain full of Spanish wine and leave the wine merchant's cellar empty. Many ended up in Scottish distilleries, and how that happened, and why it stopped, explains why some Scotch is pale gold and some is the colour of strong tea.

We have four bottles from three distilleries, Glenmorangie, Macallan and GlenDronach. Each made a different choice about sherry casks at a different point in the story, and we'll taste them as we reach them.

## [SLIDE 2] Scotch matures in casks that held something else first

By law, Scotch has to spend at least three years in oak casks in Scotland, in casks of no more than 700 litres. The law doesn't say the casks have to be new, and in practice very few are. Scottish distillers buy casks that have already held something.

There are two main sources. The common one is the American bourbon barrel, about 200 litres. Distillers use it as it is, or take it apart and rebuild it with extra staves as a hogshead of about 250 litres. The other is the sherry butt, about 500 litres, which is the one tonight is named after.

The cask gives the whisky all of its natural colour and a large share of its flavour. New spirit runs off the still as clear as water.

## [SLIDE 3] Britain was sherry's biggest customer in the 1800s

Sherry is wine from the corner of Andalusia around Jerez de la Frontera, with El Puerto de Santa María and Sanlúcar de Barrameda making up what's called the sherry triangle. The English had been drinking it since Shakespeare's time, when it was called sack, and in the nineteenth century Britain was its biggest market by a long way.

You can see that in the names of the shipping houses. Sandeman was founded in London in 1790 by George Sandeman, a Scot from Perth. Harvey's was a Bristol wine merchant. González Byass takes its second name from Robert Blake Byass, the English agent who became Manuel María González's partner. Williams & Humbert was started in Jerez in 1877 by Alexander Williams and his brother-in-law Arthur Humbert.

British sherry drinking is usually said to have peaked around the 1870s and fallen away after, with cheap imitations and fashion blamed. Treat both the date and the causes as unproven.

## [SLIDE 4] Sherry crossed to Britain in the cask

The detail that matters for us is how it travelled. Sherry was shipped in wooden casks, mostly butts, to Bristol, London, Liverpool, Leith and Glasgow, and British wine merchants bottled it themselves under their own names.

Once a cask was empty it had no obvious use. Sending it back to Spain cost money, so merchants sold the empties locally and cheaply, and whisky distillers and blenders were among the buyers.

These transport casks were built for the voyage, often from new or nearly new oak, and they might have held sherry for only a few months at sea and in a cellar. The old casks in the bodegas, where sherry is actually aged, stayed in Spain.

## [SLIDE 5] 1915: whisky must spend two years in bond before sale

For most of the 1800s a lot of Scotch was sold young, often within months of being distilled, so the cask it sat in mattered less than it does now. It's also hard to say how much was aged in sherry casks. Distillers used whatever casks they could get, including ones that had held rum, port and wine, and the records rarely say which. The claim that Victorian Scotch was routinely matured in sherry is repeated a lot, and the evidence for it is thin.

The First World War changed this. David Lloyd George, Chancellor of the Exchequer when the bill went through, believed heavy drinking was slowing war production. He is said to have wanted prohibition and settled for a set of smaller measures. One of them was the Immature Spirits (Restriction) Act, passed in May 1915. Whisky had to be kept in a bonded warehouse for at least two years before sale, and in May 1916 the minimum went up to three, where it still is. From then on every bottle of Scotch had spent years in a cask, and which cask it was began to matter much more.

## [SLIDE 6] From 1938, bourbon goes into new barrels

The other great supply of casks came from America. After Prohibition ended in 1933, the federal government wrote standards of identity for American whiskey, and from 1938 they said that straight bourbon had to be aged in new charred oak containers. Sources give March or July 1938 for when the rule took effect. A bourbon distiller can use a barrel once, and then it has to go somewhere else.

You'll often hear that the new-barrel rule was put in to protect coopers and the timber trade. That story is told a lot and I've never seen good evidence for it, so treat it as a legend.

Whatever the reason, American distilleries now produced a steady supply of used barrels that no American distiller could use. Scottish distillers started buying them, and the ex-bourbon barrel became the ordinary Scotch cask. The big shift is usually put in the 1950s and 60s, which I couldn't confirm. Most Scotch is now matured in them.

## [SLIDE 7] An ex-bourbon barrel and a sherry butt

An ex-bourbon barrel is American white oak, about 200 litres, charred inside, and it has held bourbon for anything from two years to ten or more. American oak tends to give flavours people describe as vanilla, coconut and honey, and a pale gold colour.

A sherry butt is bigger, about 500 litres, so less wood touches each litre of whisky. It's usually toasted rather than charred. It is often European oak, which has more tannin, though a good many sherry casks are American oak, because the bodegas have used a lot of it. Sherry-cask whisky tends to be darker, with flavours described as dried fruit, nuts and spice, and a drier grip from the tannin.

These are tendencies. The cask's age, how often it has been filled and the spirit put into it count as much as what it held before.

## [SLIDE 8] Pour 1 of 4: Glenmorangie The Original

First pour. This is Glenmorangie The Original, twelve years old, from Tain on the Dornoch Firth in the northern Highlands. The distillery dates from 1843 and has belonged to the French luxury group LVMH since 2004.

For decades The Original was a ten-year-old; in 2024 Glenmorangie replaced it with this twelve-year-old, still at 40 percent. Glenmorangie is here because it went further into American oak than almost anyone. This whisky is matured entirely in ex-bourbon casks. The company says it chooses its own oak in the Ozark forests of Missouri, has the staves air-dried, and lends the finished barrels to American distillers to fill with whiskey before they come to Tain.

Look at the colour, and keep a little of this in your glass. It's the reference for everything else tonight.

## [SLIDE 9] Sherry must be bottled in the Jerez region

Back to Jerez. In the 1970s and 80s the sherry producers wanted more control over their wine. They wanted sherry bottled in Spain, so that the bottling trade and its profits stayed there.

It happened in stages. From the early 1970s the sherry regulator, the Consejo Regulador, pushed the shippers to bottle at source, and the regulator's own history says that at the start of the 1980s it decided wine carrying the name could no longer be bottled outside the region. Several whisky writers give 1981; others, including Whisky Magazine, give 1986. I couldn't find the text of the rule, so the slide shows the range. Either way, bulk shipments in cask came to an end, and so did the supply of cheap transport butts to Britain.

For distillers who depended on sherry casks this was a serious problem, because the bodegas had little reason to sell their old casks.

## [SLIDE 10] Two ways round the shortage

Distillers answered it in roughly two ways, and our next two bottles show one each.

The first was to use sherry casks sparingly. You mature the whisky for most of its life in ex-bourbon barrels and move it into a sherry cask for the last part. That's called a finish, and one sherry cask can serve many batches. Glenmorangie made finishing its speciality in the 1990s, starting with a Port Wood finish, and Bill Lumsden, who joined in 1994, became the face of it. Balvenie got there first: its malt master David Stewart finished a whisky in oloroso casks in 1982, released it as Balvenie Classic in 1983, and the same idea became DoubleWood in 1993.

The second was to go to Jerez and have casks built and filled with sherry to order. Macallan, which had built its name on sherry casks, is the best-known distillery to take that route.

## [SLIDE 11] Pour 2 of 4: Glenmorangie Lasanta

Second pour, and this is the first approach. Glenmorangie Lasanta, twelve years old, from the same distillery as the first pour. Glenmorangie says it spends about ten years in ex-bourbon casks and is then moved into oloroso and Pedro Ximénez sherry casks for two more.

Taste it next to the Original. The two are now the same age, and the spirit and most of the maturation are shared, so the difference you notice is close to what the sherry casks added in the last stretch. Look at the colour, then see whether there's more dried fruit, and whether the finish is drier.

## [SLIDE 12] Seasoned casks, made to order in Jerez

A cooperage builds a new cask to the distiller's order, with the oak, European or American, and the toast chosen by the customer. The cask goes to a bodega and is filled with sherry, usually a young oloroso or a Pedro Ximénez, for anything from several months to a couple of years. Then it's emptied and shipped whole to Scotland. Much of the sherry that comes out is distilled into brandy or made into vinegar.

In 2015 the sherry regulator, the Consejo Regulador, registered a "Sherry Cask" mark and began certifying casks seasoned with real sherry from the region, for at least twelve months. Its president has said the average is nearer eighteen months.

There's an older practice you should know about. For much of the twentieth century some casks were treated with paxarette, a dark, sweet wine concentrate, to give them a sherry character quickly. It ended in the late 1980s or around 1990; I've seen both dates, and the record of exactly who stopped it is thin.

## [SLIDE 13] A solera cask and a seasoned cask

People use "sherry cask" for two different things.

A solera cask belongs to a bodega. Sherry is aged in rows of casks: wine for bottling is drawn from the oldest row, which is topped up from the next oldest, and so on back to the youngest. The casks stay in the system for decades, sometimes much longer. They are soaked right through and have little oak flavour left to give. Bodegas rarely sell them, and when they do the price is high.

A seasoned cask was built to be sold to a distiller. It usually held sherry for a year or two, and the wood is still fresh enough to give plenty of oak flavour. Nearly every sherry cask filled with Scotch today is a seasoned cask.

## [SLIDE 14] Pour 3 of 4: Macallan 12 Sherry Oak

Third pour. Macallan 12 Sherry Oak, from Easter Elchies near Craigellachie on the Spey. The distillery was licensed in 1824 to a farmer called Alexander Reid.

This is the second approach. Macallan now describes this whisky as matured in "predominantly sherry seasoned European oak casks"; older bottles said exclusively sherry casks. The company has published a good deal about choosing oak in northern Spain and having the casks built and seasoned in Jerez to its order.

It's the most expensive bottle tonight, often over $70. Compare it with the Lasanta: this one has been in sherry casks all its life, and the Lasanta only for its last part. Look at the colour first. Scotch law allows caramel colouring, and Macallan says the colour of this one is entirely natural, so what you see comes from the casks.

## [SLIDE 15] Oloroso and Pedro Ximénez

Oloroso is made from the Palomino grape and fermented dry. It's fortified to at least 17 percent alcohol, which stops the layer of yeast called flor from growing on it, so the wine ages in contact with the air and turns brown and nutty. It is dry.

Pedro Ximénez is a different grape. The bunches are laid out in the sun until they're nearly raisins, and the wine made from them is almost black, thick and very sweet.

In whisky, oloroso casks are usually said to give nuts and dry spice, and PX casks something darker, sweeter and more like raisins. The distillery and the age of the cask can blur that.

## [SLIDE 16] GlenDronach, 1826 to now

GlenDronach is near Forgue in Aberdeenshire, and it was founded in 1826 by James Allardice.

In 1960 it was bought by William Teacher and Sons, and much of what it made went into Teacher's blended whisky. Teacher's later became part of the Allied group, which mothballed GlenDronach in 1996 and reopened it in 2002.

In 2008 Pernod Ricard sold it to the BenRiach Distillery Company, led by Billy Walker, who made GlenDronach a sherry-cask distillery, matured in oloroso and Pedro Ximénez casks. In June 2016 the company was sold to Brown-Forman, the Kentucky firm that makes Old Forester and Woodford Reserve.

## [SLIDE 17] Pour 4 of 4: GlenDronach 12

Last pour. GlenDronach 12, labelled The Original, at 43 percent. The distillery says it is matured for at least twelve years in Pedro Ximénez and oloroso casks, with no caramel added.

This is full sherry maturation again, but with PX in the mix. Put it beside the Macallan. Both are sherry-matured twelve-year-olds at 43 percent, so the differences come mostly from the two distilleries' spirit and from the casks: Macallan's sherry-seasoned oak against GlenDronach's mix of oloroso and PX. See whether you find this one darker or sweeter.

When you've finished, I'd like a show of hands for which of the four you would buy.

## [SLIDE 18] Reading the cask on a Scotch label

"Ex-bourbon" means barrels that held American whiskey. "American oak" on its own describes only the wood, so it may or may not mean the same thing.

"Sherry cask" or "sherry oak" today almost always means a seasoned cask built for the purpose, and it says nothing about whether the oak was European or American.

"Matured in" means the whisky spent all or most of its life in that kind of cask. "Finished" or "extra matured" means it was moved there for the last part.

"Oloroso" and "PX" name the sherry that seasoned the cask.

"Natural colour" is the producer's statement that no caramel was added. Scotch law allows caramel colouring, so a dark Scotch without that phrase may have been coloured.
