## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] Grocers and Grain

The painting on the cover is Edwin Landseer's Illicit Highland Whisky Still, shown at the Royal Academy in 1829. The copper pot still is behind the girl on the right.

Thanks for coming. Tonight is about blended Scotch, which is most of the Scotch that has ever been drunk.

Between about 1830 and 1910 Scotch went from a local drink to one sold all over the world, almost all of it as blends. We'll taste a grain whisky, a malt whisky and two blends made from both, so you can taste the parts before you taste the result.

## [SLIDE 2] Scotch in 1830: malt, sold by the cask

Start with what Scotch was around 1830. It was malt whisky, made from malted barley in a pot still, and the trade had only recently become respectable. An Excise Act in 1823 cut the duty and brought in a £10 licence, and a lot of Highland distillers who had been working illegally went legal in the years after. George Smith at Glenlivet was licensed in November 1824.

Very little whisky was bottled by the distillery. A distiller sold whisky by the cask to merchants, innkeepers and grocers, and they sold it on by the jar or the glass. Every cask was different, even from the same distillery, so what you bought from your grocer one month might taste quite unlike the next.

## [SLIDE 3] 1830: Aeneas Coffey patents a continuous still

The first change came from Ireland. Aeneas Coffey had been Inspector-General of Excise in Ireland, so he spent years catching distillers. He resigned in 1824 and went into distilling himself in Dublin, and in 1830 he patented a design with two tall columns.

You can see it in the diagram. Steam goes in at the bottom of the left column, the analyser. The wash, which is a kind of rough beer, is first warmed in pipes running through the right-hand column, then let in at the top of the analyser. It comes down through a series of perforated plates and the rising steam strips the alcohol out of it. The vapour then goes across to the right-hand column, the rectifier, where it's concentrated further, and the spirit is drawn off near the top.

The point is that it runs continuously. A pot still is filled, boiled and emptied, one batch at a time. A Coffey still keeps going as long as you keep feeding it, it makes a much stronger and lighter spirit, and it can use cheaper grains than malted barley.

Robert Stein, from a Lowland distilling family, had patented a continuous still in 1827 and 1828. His relatives the Haigs used it at Cameronbridge in Fife. Coffey's design worked better and replaced it. The big Dublin distillers turned the patent still down. The Scots took it up: the first Scottish distillery to install a Coffey still was Grange, in 1834.

## [SLIDE 4] Pour 1 of 4: Haig Club Clubman

So let's taste what that still makes. This is Haig Club Clubman, a single grain whisky from Cameronbridge, the same Haig distillery in Fife that ran Stein's still. Today it uses column stills. It's at 40%.

Single grain means one distillery, and grains other than malted barley are allowed. Most grain whisky goes into blends, so this is one of the few ways to try it by itself.

Have it neat and notice how light it is. Look for sweetness rather than weight.

## [SLIDE 5] Malt whisky and grain whisky

By the 1840s Scotland was making two different things, both called whisky.

Malt whisky was what it had always been: malted barley, a pot still, small batches, a lot of flavour, and a lot of variation. Grain whisky was cheap and plentiful, but it was so light that on its own it didn't sell well as whisky. The big Lowland distillers had been shipping spirit south to the London gin rectifiers since the 1770s.

## [SLIDE 6] Andrew Usher vats Glenlivet, 1853

The man usually credited with starting that is Andrew Usher of Edinburgh. The Ushers were the Edinburgh agents for Smith's Glenlivet. In 1853 a change in the duty law allowed whiskies from the same distillery to be vatted together in bond, and that year Usher began selling Old Vatted Glenlivet. Vatting meant mixing casks of one distillery's whisky, young and old together, so that every bottle tasted roughly alike.

A customer could now ask for something by name and get the same thing twice. Usher's firm went on to mix grain whisky into the malt once the law allowed it, which is why he gets called the father of blending. I'd be careful with that title, because other merchants were doing similar things and most of the detail comes from later company histories. There were also two Andrew Ushers, father and son, and the stories tend to merge them.

## [SLIDE 7] Pour 2 of 4: The Glenlivet 12 Year Old

Second pour, and it's the Glenlivet, the malt Usher was vatting. This is the 12 Year Old, made by Chivas Brothers, at 40%. In the US you may find it labelled "12 Year Old Double Oak".

A single malt is still a vatting, of course. This bottle is many casks of Glenlivet of at least twelve years old, mixed together so it tastes the same every year, much as Usher did.

Taste it against the grain whisky. It should feel heavier in the mouth, with more fruit and more of the cereal. That difference is what the blenders were working with.

## [SLIDE 8] 1860: the Spirits Act

The step that made blending a real industry was a change in the tax law. The Spirits Act of 1860, passed in August that year while Gladstone was Chancellor of the Exchequer, allowed whiskies from different distilleries, malt and grain among them, to be mixed together while still in bond, which meant before the duty was paid.

That mattered for cash. Duty was a large part of the price of whisky, and paying it before blending tied up a lot of money in stock sitting in vats. Under bond, a merchant could blend in quantity and pay the duty when he sold.

It also let the blender put cheap grain alongside expensive malt and sell the bottle as whisky. For the next century most of the money in Scotch went to the firms that owned the blends and brand names.

## [SLIDE 9] The first big blenders were grocers

And the people best placed to do that were the grocers and wine merchants who were already buying whisky by the cask. Four of the names you know started this way.

John Walker opened a grocery in Kilmarnock in 1820, selling whisky among everything else. His son Alexander turned the whisky side into a business. In Aberdeen, James Chivas joined a grocery business on King Street around 1838, and the firm says it held a royal warrant to supply Queen Victoria from 1843. His brother John joined him in 1857. John Dewar set up as a wine and spirit merchant on the High Street in Perth in 1846. George Ballantine opened a grocer's in Edinburgh in 1827 and later opened a larger shop in Glasgow, usually dated 1865.

You'll hear it said that grocers took to blending whisky because they already blended tea, and knew how to make a product taste the same from one chest to the next. It's a neat explanation and I suspect there's something in it, but none of them is on record saying so. The more certain point is that they had shops, customers who came back, and credit with the distillers.

## [SLIDE 10] Pour 3 of 4: Johnnie Walker Black Label

Third pour, Johnnie Walker Black Label, a blended Scotch with a twelve-year age statement, made by Diageo at 40%.

The Walkers sold their blends as Old Highland Whisky, and customers already asked for them by the colour of the label. In 1909 the firm renamed them Johnnie Walker, and the Extra Special Old Highland became Black Label. The striding man was drawn by the cartoonist Tom Browne the year before. The square bottle and the slanted label are older. The company dates the bottle to 1860.

This is malt and grain together. Try to find the Glenlivet-type fruit and the lightness of the Clubman in it. Look for a little smoke as well; Diageo says island malts go into it.

## [SLIDE 11] 1860s: phylloxera reaches the vineyards of France

Now the insect. In the 1860s vines in the south of France began to wither and die. The cause was phylloxera, a tiny aphid that came from North America, probably on vines imported for study, and fed on the roots. French scientists identified it in 1868. American vines had resistance to it; European vines didn't.

It spread across France over the next twenty years. The first Cognac vineyards were hit in 1872, and by the 1880s most of the vineyards there had been destroyed. The eventual cure was grafting French vines onto American rootstocks, and replanting in Cognac only got going in earnest in the 1890s.

The cartoon is from Punch, 6 September 1890, titled "The Phylloxera, a true gourmet". The insect sits at table among the wine it has ruined.

## [SLIDE 12] From brandy and soda to whisky and soda

This is where the story becomes about England. The fashionable drink for a respectable Victorian gentleman was brandy, often as brandy and soda. When cognac became scarce and expensive, people looked for a replacement, and the blenders were ready with a light, consistent Scotch that could be drunk the same way, with soda water.

It's usually told as if phylloxera alone made Scotch popular in England, and I'd hold that loosely. The whisky historian Chris Middleton points out that Britain already drank far more whisky than brandy in 1860, before the vines began to die. But the Scotch boom of the 1880s and 1890s does run alongside the worst of the brandy shortage.

The distillers responded by building. Glenfiddich's first spirit ran on Christmas Day 1887, and Dewar's opened Aberfeldy in 1898 to supply malt for its blend.

## [SLIDE 13] The salesmen of the 1890s

The blends needed selling, and two London-based Scots did most to sell them in the 1890s.

Tommy Dewar was John Dewar's younger son. He went to London in 1885, aged 21, to sell the family's whisky, and had a gift for publicity. He then went round the world to open export markets and wrote a book about it, A Ramble Round the Globe, published in 1894.

James Buchanan was born in Canada and brought up in Glasgow. He came to London in 1879 as an agent for the Leith firm Charles Mackinlay and Co., and started his own business in 1884. From 1885 he supplied whisky to the House of Commons, which he made full use of. The bottle had a black and white label, people started ordering "Black & White", and from 1904 the firm used the name.

Dewar became Lord Dewar in 1919 and Buchanan became Lord Woolavington in 1922. The 1907 Vanity Fair caricature of Buchanan is titled "Whisky and Horses", after his racehorses.

## [SLIDE 14] Pour 4 of 4: Dewar's White Label

Last pour, Dewar's White Label, made by John Dewar and Sons, which Bacardi owns now. It's a blended Scotch at 40%. The company dates the White Label blend to 1899, when its first master blender, A. J. Cameron, made it.

I'd like you to try this one two ways. Have a little neat first. Then put some in a tall glass with ice and soda water, which is how a lot of it was drunk in London in the 1890s.

Once you've done that, go back and taste the Clubman again. You should be able to pick out the grain in the Dewar's now.

## [SLIDE 15] 1898: Pattisons of Leith fails

The boom ended badly. Robert and Walter Pattison ran a blending firm in Leith, and in the mid-1890s they expanded quickly, floated the company, borrowed heavily and spent lavishly on publicity. There's a story that they gave out hundreds of parrots trained to say "Drink Pattisons' Whisky". It's told everywhere and I haven't seen a contemporary source for it, so I'd call it a legend.

What isn't in doubt is that the firm stopped payment in December 1898. The brothers were arrested in April 1901 and tried in Edinburgh that July. Robert was found guilty on four charges of fraud and embezzlement and Walter on two; Robert got eighteen months and Walter nine.

Lenders became wary of the whisky trade, several firms that had dealt with the Pattisons went down with them, and the industry was left with far more whisky than it could sell. The building boom of the 1890s was over.

## [SLIDE 16] 1908–09: a Royal Commission decides what whisky is

The last part of the story is a legal fight over the word itself. The malt distillers had never liked the blenders using the name whisky for something that was mostly grain spirit from a Coffey still. In 1905 Islington Borough Council in London had samples bought from local publicans and merchants and prosecuted them for selling whisky that, the council said, wasn't whisky, because it contained patent-still spirit. In February 1906 the magistrate, Edward Fordham, agreed with the council. The appeal was adjourned and ended without a decision.

In February 1908 a Royal Commission was appointed to answer the question "What is whisky?", chaired by Lord James of Hereford. As Sir Henry James he had been Gladstone's Attorney General; the caricature is from Vanity Fair in 1874.

Its final report, signed in July 1909 and published that August, came down on the blenders' side. Whisky was a spirit made from a mash of cereal grains saccharified by the diastase of malt, and the type of still didn't matter. So grain whisky was whisky. The Commission didn't set a minimum age; that came in 1915, when the Immature Spirits Act required three years in bond, and it's still three years today. Across the Atlantic, President Taft reached a similar answer for American whiskey that December.

## [SLIDE 17] Reading a Scotch label

To finish, here's how to read a Scotch label today. These definitions are set by the Scotch Whisky Regulations of 2009, which still use the 1909 idea of a grain mash converted by malt.

Single malt is from one distillery, made only from malted barley in pot stills. Single grain is from one distillery but can use other cereals and column stills, like our first pour. Blended malt is single malts from more than one distillery, with no grain whisky. Blended Scotch is malt and grain whisky mixed, like the last two pours. An age statement gives the age of the youngest whisky in the bottle.

All Scotch must be matured in oak casks in Scotland for at least three years. Any colouring other than plain caramel is banned, and caramel colouring is allowed in every category.
