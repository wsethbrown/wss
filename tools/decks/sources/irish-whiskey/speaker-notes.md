## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] Irish Whiskey

Tonight is about Irish whiskey over the last 140 years.

In the 1880s Irish whiskey had the best reputation of any whiskey, and a lot of it was made in Dublin. A hundred years later there were two working distilleries left in the whole of Ireland, and one company owned both. Today there are dozens again. I'll tell you how that happened, in order. We'll taste four whiskeys as we go.

The photograph is Locke's distillery at Kilbeggan. It comes up later.

## [SLIDE 2] Dublin's four big distillers in the 1880s

We start in Dublin in the 1880s. Four firms made most of the whiskey there. John Jameson was at Bow Street, north of the river. William Jameson was a separate firm at Marrowbone Lane. John Power was at John's Lane. George Roe was at Thomas Street.

The picture is the Bow Street distillery. It was drawn for a journalist called Alfred Barnard, who visited the distilleries of Britain and Ireland in the 1880s and published a book about them in 1887.

These were big factories in the middle of the city. They made whiskey in copper pot stills and sold it by the cask to merchants, who bottled it. It was sold all over Britain, the Empire and America.

## [SLIDE 3] Single pot still: malted and unmalted barley

The whiskey these distilleries made is now called single pot still.

It is made from barley. Some of the barley is malted and some is not. Malting means soaking the grain and letting it start to sprout, which turns its starch into sugar. The Dublin distillers malted part of their barley and used the rest raw. Then they distilled it in big copper pot stills, usually three times.

The usual explanation is tax. From 1785 malted barley was taxed in Ireland, so a distiller who used less malt paid less tax. Distillers were already using some raw barley before then, so the tax is probably only part of the reason.

Raw barley makes a different whiskey from malt. People describe it as oily or creamy, with some spice.

## [SLIDE 4] Pour 1 of 4: Redbreast 12 Year Old

_Pour glass 1 now._

This is Redbreast 12. It is made at Midleton, in County Cork, by Irish Distillers. It is a single pot still whiskey. That means malted and unmalted barley, made in pot stills at one distillery.

Redbreast started as a merchant's brand. Gilbey's were wine and spirit merchants in Dublin. They bought pot still whiskey from the Bow Street distillery, aged it in their own casks and bottled it. They were using the name Redbreast by 1912. Irish Distillers bought the brand in 1986.

Taste it with nothing added. Notice how it feels in your mouth before you think about the flavour. Keep a little in the glass. We'll come back to it with pour three.

## [SLIDE 5] Pot still and Coffey still

In 1830 an Irishman called Aeneas Coffey patented a new kind of still. Coffey had been head of the excise service in Ireland. He left in 1824 and became a distiller in Dublin.

His still was two tall columns. The wash goes in at the top and steam comes up from the bottom. Spirit comes off all the time. With a pot still you have to stop, empty it and fill it again. Coffey's still was cheap to run, and the spirit it made was strong and light in flavour.

The Dublin distillers did not want it. They thought the spirit it made was hardly whiskey at all. Coffey moved his business to London, and his best customers were distillers in Scotland. Over the next few decades Scottish firms started mixing this cheap, light spirit with a smaller amount of their own malt whisky. The result was the same every time, easy to drink and much cheaper to make.

## [SLIDE 6] 1909: A Royal Commission declines to limit whisky to the pot still

The Dublin firms argued against this in public. In 1878 the four big distillers published a book called "Truths about Whisky". It said spirit from a Coffey still was not whiskey. It called it "pure alcohol and water, or nearly so, without distinctive flavour or quality".

In 1908 the British government set up a Royal Commission to decide what could be called whisky. It reported in August 1909. It did not rule against the Coffey still. It said whisky could be made in any kind of still. So spirit from a Coffey still was whisky, and so was a blend.

The Dublin distillers had lost the argument. By then the Scotch blends were already selling well in England and abroad.

The picture is the John's Lane distillery, from the same book as the last one.

## [SLIDE 7] From 1922, two jurisdictions

In 1922 Ireland was divided. Twenty-six counties became the Irish Free State. Northern Ireland stayed in the United Kingdom.

That put the distilleries in two different countries. Most of them were now in the Free State. That included Dublin, Cork, Tullamore and Kilbeggan. Bushmills, on the north coast, and the big Belfast distilleries were still in the UK. They went on selling in Britain as before.

The photograph is Bushmills. It was taken some time before the First World War.

You will see the year 1608 on Bushmills bottles. In 1608 King James I gave a local landowner a licence to distil in that part of County Antrim. The licence was not for this distillery. The company that ran Bushmills was formed in 1784.

## [SLIDE 8] Pour 2 of 4: Bushmills 10 Year Old

_Pour glass 2 now._

This is Bushmills 10 Year Old. It is a single malt. All the barley is malted and there is no raw barley in it. It is distilled three times.

Compare it with the Redbreast. Both are made in pot stills. Both are distilled three times. Both are 40 percent. The main difference is the raw barley in the Redbreast. Does this one feel lighter in your mouth?

Bushmills has had several owners. Irish Distillers owned it from 1972 to 2005. Then it was sold to Diageo. In 2014 Diageo swapped it with the owners of Jose Cuervo for the other half of Don Julio tequila.

## [SLIDE 9] The export markets close, 1920 to 1938

In 1920 Prohibition started in the United States. America had been one of the biggest markets for Irish whiskey. It was closed for thirteen years.

In 1922 the Free State was founded. In 1932 the Irish government stopped making certain payments to Britain. Britain put heavy tariffs on Irish goods, and Ireland put tariffs on British goods. That lasted until 1938. Britain bought about nine-tenths of everything Ireland exported, so for six years it was expensive to sell Irish goods there.

Prohibition ended in 1933. By then Scotch blends were cheap and easy to get in America. The Irish distillers had less whiskey to sell and fewer people selling it. Their whiskey was also heavier and cost more to make.

The photograph shows agents pouring liquor into a sewer during Prohibition.

## [SLIDE 10] 1966: The last three distilling firms in the Republic merge

By the 1960s there were three distilling companies left in the Republic. They were John Jameson and Son, John Power and Son, and the Cork Distilleries Company. The Cork company owned the old distillery at Midleton and made Paddy.

In 1966 the three merged. They had been competitors for generations, but sales were falling and there was not enough business for three. The company they formed is called Irish Distillers.

Bushmills joined in 1972. After that one company owned almost every distillery in Ireland, north and south.

## [SLIDE 11] Dublin closes and Midleton opens, 1971 to 1976

The old distilleries closed one at a time.

Tullamore stopped in 1954. So did Locke's at Kilbeggan, the distillery in the photograph on the first slide. The Kilbeggan buildings were left standing.

Irish Distillers decided to leave Dublin. Bow Street stopped distilling in 1971 and its production moved to John's Lane. The company built one new distillery at Midleton in County Cork, next to the old one. It started up in July 1975. John's Lane closed in 1976. The other two Dublin distilleries had closed long before. Nobody was making whiskey in Dublin any more.

## [SLIDE 12] 2 working distilleries on the island by 1980

Irish Distillers also owned a distillery at Coleraine in the north. By then it made only grain whiskey. The new Midleton could make grain whiskey too, so Coleraine closed in 1978. By 1980 there were two working whiskey distilleries in Ireland, Midleton and Bushmills. Irish Distillers owned both.

The new Midleton was built to make every kind of whiskey in one place. It has pot stills and column stills. Powers, Paddy and Redbreast had once come from different distilleries in different cities. Now they were all made at Midleton.

Through the 1980s all new Irish whiskey was made at one of those two distilleries.

## [SLIDE 13] Pour 3 of 4: Powers Gold Label

_Pour glass 3 now._

This is Powers Gold Label. Powers was made by John Power and Son at John's Lane in Dublin. When John's Lane closed in 1976 the brand moved to Midleton.

Today Gold Label is a blend. It is pot still whiskey mixed with grain whiskey from the column stills at Midleton. In 1878 John Power and Son was one of the four firms that said whiskey like this should not be called whiskey. It is 43.2 percent, a little stronger than most blends.

Taste it next to the Redbreast you kept. They come from the same distillery and both have pot still whiskey in them. The grain whiskey makes the Powers lighter and less spicy.

## [SLIDE 14] The revival starts at Cooley in 1987

In 1987 a businessman called John Teeling bought an old factory at Riverstown in County Louth. It had belonged to the state and had made alcohol from potatoes. It already had column stills. He added pot stills and started making whiskey. The distillery was called Cooley. Around then Cooley also took over the Locke's name and the old distillery at Kilbeggan.

In 1988 the French company Pernod Ricard bought Irish Distillers, and it still owns it. In 1993 Irish Distillers tried to buy Cooley. The competition regulator blocked the sale.

Whiskey has to spend three years in a cask before it can be sold as Irish whiskey. So a new distillery spends money for years before it earns any. Cooley stayed independent until the end of 2011, when the American company Beam agreed to buy it for about 95 million dollars. It now belongs to Suntory.

After that a lot of new distilleries opened. John Teeling's sons opened one in Dublin in 2015. It was the first new distillery in the city in more than 125 years. By the 2020s there were dozens across Ireland.

## [SLIDE 15] Pour 4 of 4: Connemara Original Peated Single Malt

_Pour glass 4 now._

This is Connemara. Cooley started selling it in 1996.

Cooley did two things here that were unusual for Irish whiskey. They dried some of the malt over peat smoke. And they distilled twice, the way most Scottish distilleries do, and not three times.

You should taste smoke. It is a single malt like the Bushmills, so compare the two if you have any Bushmills left.

## [SLIDE 16] Reading an Irish whiskey label

This slide shows what the words on an Irish whiskey label mean.

Irish whiskey has to be distilled in Ireland and aged there for at least three years in wooden casks. That is the law.

Single pot still means malted and unmalted barley, at least 30 percent of each, made in pot stills at one distillery. The Redbreast is one. Single malt means malted barley only, made in pot stills at one distillery. The Bushmills and the Connemara are single malts. Blended means two or more of these kinds mixed together. Usually one of them is grain whiskey. The Powers is a blend.

The last line is about who made the whiskey. A lot of the brands in the shops are newer than the whiskey in the bottle. If a brand started in 2015 and sells a ten-year-old whiskey, it bought that whiskey from someone else. For years the only places to buy it were Midleton, Bushmills and Cooley. Look on the back label for the name of a distillery. If it only names a company, the whiskey was probably bought in.

That's all from me. Thank you for coming. Sláinte.
