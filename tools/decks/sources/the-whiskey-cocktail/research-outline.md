# The Whiskey Cocktail

Research outline: The Old Fashioned, the Manhattan and the Sazerac

The Old Fashioned, the Manhattan and the Sazerac. A guide for anyone who wants to research the subject and build a talk of their own.

---

**Sources.** The facts in this deck were checked in November 2026 against the sources listed under each module: transcriptions of the 1806 newspaper and the early bar books, the Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails, the work of David Wondrich and Barry Popik, and the Library of Congress and Wikimedia Commons records for the images. What could not be confirmed is listed under "Not confirmed", with what would settle it; nothing in that list appears on a slide.

## Module 1. The 1806 definition

**The question.** What did "cock-tail" mean when it first appeared in print, and what was in it?

### Nailed down

- The Balance and Columbian Repository (Hudson, New York), edited by Harry Croswell, a Federalist, printed the definition on 13 May 1806 (Vol. V, No. 19, p. 146) in reply to a reader: "a stimulating liquor, composed of spirits of any kind, sugar, water, and bitters", "vulgarly called bittered sling", "an excellent electioneering potion, inasmuch as it renders the heart stout and bold, at the same time that it fuddles the head". Transcriptions differ slightly in punctuation and in whether "a" precedes "bittered sling".
- The question arose from a mock list of a losing candidate's election expenses printed on 6 May 1806.
- The earliest American use usually cited is The Farmer's Cabinet, Amherst, New Hampshire, 28 April 1803 ("excellent for the head"), with no recipe. One discussion notes the original issue had not been re-examined in recent years; the citation rests on reports by Barnhart and Wondrich.
- A London paper, The Morning Post and Gazetteer, used "cock-tail" for a drink on 20 March 1798.

### Primary documents

- The Balance and Columbian Repository, 6 and 13 May 1806 (digitised runs exist; Readex America's Historical Newspapers).
- The Farmer's Cabinet, 28 April 1803.

### Sources

- Word Histories, "1806: earliest definition of 'cocktail'": https://wordhistories.net/2019/09/19/cocktail-earliest-definition/
- Wikipedia, "Harry Croswell": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Croswell
- Wikipedia, "Cocktail" (with the scan citation): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocktails

### Traps

- The etymology of "cocktail" is unknown. Horse-docking, the egg-cup (coquetier) story and a dozen others are theories without documentary support.

## Module 2. American whiskey around 1806

**The question.** What did a drinker in New York State mean by whiskey at the time of the definition?

### Nailed down

- Rye was the prevalent whiskey of Pennsylvania, New York and Maryland; Pittsburgh was the centre of rye production in the late 1700s and early 1800s.
- Early Monongahela rye was sold young; ageing was largely a by-product of storage and transport. Barrel ageing was known by the 1790s, but long ageing in charred barrels as standard practice came later (the exact date is not settled).
- Corn whiskey developed in Kentucky from the late 1700s, because corn grew better there than rye or wheat; "bourbon" is first known in print around 1821.
- Angostura bitters: Johann Gottlieb Benjamin Siegert began selling them in Angostura (now Ciudad Bolívar), Venezuela, in 1824, as a stomach remedy.
- Abraham Overholt began commercial rye distilling at West Overton, Pennsylvania, in 1810 (West Overton Village dates distilling on the site to 1803).

## Module 3. Jerry Thomas and the "improvements"

**The question.** When was the whiskey cocktail first printed as a recipe, and how did it change?

### Nailed down

- Jerry Thomas, How to Mix Drinks, or The Bon-Vivant's Companion (New York, 1862). The Whiskey Cocktail as printed: "(Use small bar glass.) 3 or 4 dashes of gum syrup. 2 do. bitters (Bogart's). 1 wine-glass of whiskey, and a piece of lemon peel. Fill one-third full of fine ice; shake and strain in a fancy red wine-glass." "Bogart's" is generally read as Boker's; no Bogart's brand is known. The same wording is in the 1876 edition.
- The 1876 edition adds "Improved" brandy, whiskey and gin cocktails. The Improved Whiskey Cocktail: 2 dashes Boker's bitters, 3 dashes gum syrup, 2 dashes maraschino, 1 dash absinthe, a piece of lemon peel, a small wine-glass of whiskey, the glass rim moistened with lemon.

### Primary documents

- The 1862, 1876 and 1887 editions of Thomas's guide (digitised on the Internet Archive).
- David Wondrich, Imbibe! (2007; revised 2015), for a biography of Thomas and annotated recipes.

### Sources

- Huntington Library, "Artbites: Whiskey Cocktail": https://huntington.org/artbites-whiskey-cocktail
- Difford's Guide, "Boker's (Bogart's) Bitters": https://www.diffordsguide.com/beer-wine-spirits/category/1150/bokers-bogarts-bitters
- Thomas, Bar-Tender's Guide (1876), Internet Archive text: https://archive.org/stream/bartendersguideo00thom/bartendersguideo00thom_djvu.txt
- Difford's Guide, "Improved Whiskey Cocktail": https://www.diffordsguide.com/cocktails/recipe/5083/improved-whiskey-cocktail
- Imbibe Magazine, "History Lesson: Improved Cocktails": https://imbibemagazine.com/?p=30063

### Traps

- Thomas is often called the inventor of the cocktail. He printed recipes that were already in use.
- Thomas's biography includes stories he told about himself. Treat his travels as his account.

## Module 4. "Old-fashioned" and the Pendennis Club

**The question.** When did people start asking for the old-fashioned cocktail, and who claims to have invented it?

### Nailed down

- The Chicago Daily Tribune of 15 February 1880 ("The Democracy in Trouble", p. 4) mentions old-fashioned cocktails. In 1882 a Chicago barman told the Tribune that the old-fashioned cocktails in vogue were made with whiskey, rye more popular than bourbon. Wondrich points to an 1886 item in Comment and Dramatic Times as the earliest description of the drink by that name.
- George Kappeler, Modern American Drinks (New York, 1895), is credited with the earliest published Old-Fashioned recipes, for whiskey, brandy, Holland gin and Old Tom gin. The whiskey one dissolves a small lump of sugar in a little water with Angostura, then adds ice, lemon peel and a jigger of whiskey (as quoted by the Chanticleer Society; full text on Project Gutenberg).
- Brown-Forman says George Garvin Brown's Old Forester (Louisville, 1870) was the first bourbon sold only in sealed bottles. The 86 proof bottling dates from 1959 (company fact sheet).
- The Pendennis Club, Louisville, was founded in 1881, after the 1880 Chicago mention.
- The earliest known source for the Pendennis claim is Albert Stevens Crockett, Old Waldorf Bar Days (1931): the drink was "introduced by, or in honor of, Col. James E. Pepper" and "said to have been the invention of a bartender at the famous Pendennis Club". Wondrich and Boehm hold that the recipe predates the club.

### Primary documents

- Kappeler (1895), on the Internet Archive.
- Crockett (1931); The Old Waldorf-Astoria Bar Book (1935).
- Chronicling America (Library of Congress, free) for newspaper uses of "old fashioned cocktail" before 1890.

### Sources

- Wikipedia, "Old fashioned (cocktail)": https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Fashioned_(cocktail)
- History News Network on Wondrich and the Old Fashioned: https://www.historynewsnetwork.org/article/145228
- Difford's Guide, "Pendennis Club": https://www.diffordsguide.com/g/1198/old-fashioned-cocktail/pendennis-club
- Go To Louisville, "The Life and Legacy of Tom Bullock": https://www.gotolouisville.com/blog/the-life-and-legacy-of-tom-bullock/

### Traps

- The Pendennis claim is repeated by the city of Louisville and in brand marketing. Treat it as a legend.
- Old Forester's sealed bottles from 1870 are Brown-Forman's own account; the script attributes it.

## Module 5. The Manhattan and vermouth

**The question.** When was the Manhattan first recorded, and what was in it?

### Nailed down

- The earliest known mention is a syndicated New York gossip column of late summer 1882: Lyndon (Kansas) Leader, 31 August 1882; Olean Democrat (New York), 5 September 1882. It names the drink a Manhattan, Turf Club or Jockey Club cocktail.
- An 1883 Boston Sunday Herald item describes the vermouth cocktail made with bourbon.
- O. H. Byron, The Modern Bartenders' Guide (1884), gives two Manhattans. No. 1: 1 pony French vermouth, ½ pony whiskey, 3 or 4 dashes Angostura, 3 dashes gum syrup. No. 2: 2 dashes curaçao, 2 dashes Angostura, ½ wine-glass whiskey, ½ wine-glass Italian vermouth.
- The Jennie Jerome story is false on its own dates. Tilden's election was on general election day, Tuesday 3 November 1874; Jennie Jerome had married Lord Randolph Churchill in Paris on 15 April 1874 and gave birth to Winston Churchill at Blenheim Palace on 30 November 1874.
- William F. Mulhall, a Hoffman House bartender, wrote in Valentine's Manual of Old New York (1923) that the Manhattan "was invented by a man named Black, who kept a place ten doors below Houston Street on Broadway" in the 1860s. William Schmidt in 1891 credited the Manhattan Club. Neither is corroborated.

### Primary documents

- Byron (1884), facsimile at the EUVS vintage cocktail books library.
- New York newspapers of 1874 for the Manhattan Club's activities.

### Sources

- Barry Popik, "Manhattan Cocktail": https://barrypopik.com/blog/manhattan_cocktail
- Difford's Guide, Manhattan history: https://www.diffordsguide.com/g/1221/manhattan-cocktail/history
- Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails, "Manhattan": https://spiritsanddistilling.com/dictionary/acref-9780199311132-e-359
- Wikipedia, "Manhattan (cocktail)": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_(cocktail)
- Bar Vademecum, Byron's two Manhattans: https://bar-vademecum.eu/the-martini-cocktail-part-3-the-manhattan-cocktail/
- EUVS, Byron (1884) scan: https://euvs-vintage-cocktail-books.cld.bz/1884-The-Modern-Bartenders-Guide-by-O-H-Byron
- Wikimedia Commons record for the Penabert photograph (wedding date): https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lord_Randolph_Churchill_and_Lady_Jennie_Jerome_(1874)_(A).jpg

### Traps

- The Manhattan Club itself has repeated the Jerome story. A club's own history is not evidence.
- Some secondary sources say all the 1884 recipes are equal parts. Byron's No. 1 is two parts vermouth to one of whiskey.

## Module 6. The Sazerac

**The question.** Was the Sazerac ever a cognac drink, and how did it become a rye drink?

### Nailed down

- Sewell Taylor ran the Merchants Exchange Coffee House (Royal Street, back entrance on Exchange Alley) and later imported Sazerac-de-Forge et Fils cognac. Aaron Bird renamed it the Sazerac Coffee House, usually dated 1852.
- Thomas H. Handy, formerly Taylor's clerk, took over around 1869–1870 and acquired the Peychaud's bitters business, usually dated 1873. These come from secondary histories that disagree by a year or two.
- The Sazerac cocktail is first named in print in 1899. The first published recipe is in William Boothby, The World's Drinks and How to Mix Them (1908), with whiskey.
- The Peychaud-and-cognac origin story was popularised by Stanley Clisby Arthur, Famous New Orleans Drinks and How to Mix 'Em (1937). Wondrich argues the Sazerac House sold it in the 1880s–90s as a bottled whiskey cocktail. Whether earlier versions used cognac is disputed.
- Antoine Amédée Peychaud died in 1883 aged 80 (so born about 1803); most accounts say he came from Saint-Domingue, one says France. Dates for his shop (1832, 1834, 1841) and for the bitters (1830s to 1857) vary by source.
- Absinthe was banned in the US by USDA Food Inspection Decision 147, 25 July 1912. Herbsaint was launched in New Orleans by J. M. Legendre as Legendre Absinthe in December 1933 and renamed Herbsaint in 1934.
- Phylloxera devastated French vineyards in the late nineteenth century; Punch printed Sambourne's phylloxera cartoon on 6 September 1890.

### Primary documents

- New Orleans city directories (Peychaud, the Sazerac House, Handy).
- Boothby (1908); Arthur (1937), read critically.

### Traps

- The Sazerac Company, which owns the brand and Peychaud's, publishes a company history. It is marketing.
- The coquetier derivation of "cocktail" is false: the word is in print in 1803 and 1806.
- The phylloxera explanation for a switch to rye depends on the drink having been cognac first, which is not documented.

## Module 7. Prohibition and after

**The question.** What did Prohibition do to these drinks, and when were the origin stories written?

### Nailed down

- National Prohibition took effect on 17 January 1920 and ended with ratification of the Twenty-first Amendment on 5 December 1933.
- Fruit appears in Old Fashioned recipes in 1933 (Julien Proskauer, What'll You Have?: orange slice and cherry, not muddled); a 1935 bar guide decorates with orange, lemon peel and a cherry. Wikipedia argues muddling began after the Second World War.
- After Repeal, blends stretched limited straight whiskey stocks and lost share again after the Second World War; rye declined and bourbon led.
- The origin stories were written down after 1920: Mulhall 1923, Crockett 1931, Arthur 1937 (sources above). David Wondrich's Imbibe! was published in 2007.
- The Library of Congress dates the raid photograph on slide 15 to 1921 (New York World-Telegram and Sun collection).

### Sources

- Reason, on Imbibe! (2007): https://reason.com/2017/09/12/government-almost-killed-the-c/
- Library of Congress, LCCN 99405169: https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/99405169/
- Cornell LII, Constitution Annotated, Twenty-first Amendment: https://law.cornell.edu/constitution-conan/amendment-21/ratification-of-the-twenty-first-amendment
- Tasting Table, "The History Of The Old Fashioned Cocktail Is A Bit Blurry": https://tastingtable.com/968396/the-history-of-old-fashioned-cocktail-is-a-bit-blurry
- Bourbon Veach, "American blended whiskey": https://bourbonveach.com/2019/01/14/american-blended-whiskey-a-dying-category/

### Traps

- "Fruit was added to hide bootleg liquor" is the usual explanation and is not proven; the earliest fruit is a garnish.
- Dates for the revival depend on whose bar you count. Avoid naming a single "first" bar.

## The four pours

- **Pour 1. Plain whiskey cocktail, with Old Overholt.** Maker: Beam (Suntory Global Spirits), Kentucky. Straight rye, 4 years, 86 proof (current US label). Why it is in the flight: the 1806 recipe, with a Pennsylvania rye name.
- **Pour 2. Old Fashioned, with Old Forester 86 Proof.** Maker: Brown-Forman, Louisville. Kentucky straight bourbon, 86 proof. Why: a Louisville bourbon, from the town of the Pendennis claim.
- **Pour 3. Manhattan, with Wild Turkey 101 Rye.** Maker: Wild Turkey, Lawrenceburg, Kentucky. Kentucky straight rye, 101 proof. Why: rye was the usual New York whiskey; the proof holds up to vermouth.
- **Pour 4. Sazerac, with Sazerac Rye.** Maker: Sazerac Company, Buffalo Trace, Frankfort. Straight rye, 90 proof. Why: from the company that now owns the Sazerac name and Peychaud's.

Recipes are in the speaker notes; slide 17 matches them. Sazerac Rye now also comes at 100 proof and full proof; use the standard 90 proof bottle. Prices vary too much by region to quote.

## Not confirmed

None of these is on a slide.

- **1810 count of over 14,000 distilleries.** Cut from the deck. No source found gives the figure. Settle with Coxe's 1814 digest (census.gov, "A Statement of the Arts and Manufactures … for the year 1810").
- **Thomas in San Francisco during the Gold Rush.** Cut from the script. Settle with Wondrich's Imbibe!.
- **Whether the Blue Blazer engraving is from the 1862 book, and its date.** Commons dates it 1862 from the Museum of the American Cocktail; no other source found. The caption gives no date. Settle by checking the plates of the 1862 edition (Internet Archive, NYPL b11968240).
- **When long ageing in charred barrels became standard.** The slide and script say only "later". Settle with a history such as Crowgey, Kentucky Bourbon: The Early Years (1971).
- **Vodka overtaking whiskey in the 1970s (one source says 1976) and rye's near-disappearance.** Cut from the deck. Settle with Distilled Spirits Council statistics.
- **Peychaud's exact birthplace and the year his bitters were first sold.** Sources conflict; the script gives the range. Settle with New Orleans city directories and newspaper advertisements.
