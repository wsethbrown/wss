# Glenlivet and the Name Thieves

Research outline: the fight over the word Glenlivet, 1824 to now

_This is the other half of the speaker notes. It does not tell you what to say; it tells you where to look, what is settled, what is still argued over, and which repeated "facts" will trip you up. The facts were checked in November 2026. Bottle prices, labels and company names move: check them again before the night._

> **THE SPINE OF THE STORY**
> 1822: "Glenlivet" already means prized, smuggled Highland whisky → 1824: George Smith licenses his still at Upper Drumin in Glenlivet → 1843 onward: Andrew Usher sells Smith's whisky as "The Real Glenlivet" and later builds a blend called Old Vatted Glenlivet → 1868: the Smiths advertise that theirs is the only licensed distillery in Glenlivet → 1881: John Gordon Smith names "Glenfarclas Glenlivet" and "Cragganmore Glenlivet" as the problem → 1882: Smith goes to the High Court; his rivals fight back → 1884: settled out of court; the Smiths keep their trade mark, the others keep the word after their own names → 1897: Glen Moray opens at Elgin and takes the suffix too → 1980s: 28 distilleries registered or labelled "-Glenlivet" → 2026: a handful of company names and one independent bottler still carry it.

## How to use this

Each module below covers one chapter of the night: the question worth answering, what is established (with dates and sources), where to read more, and the traps. The bottle facts come after the modules, then the claims that don't hold up, the short shelf, what could not be confirmed, what is worth getting, the full source list and the image credits.

The standard of proof: a claim that rests on one secondary source is said aloud as "one account says". A claim that rests only on the maker's own story, or on a story nobody can trace to a document, is told as a story. The title of the deck is the Smiths' point of view, and the script says so: the settlement of 1884 let the other distilleries keep the word.

## Module 1. Glenlivet before the distillery

_What did "Glenlivet" mean before George Smith had a licence?_

### What's established

- **1822.** During George IV's visit to Edinburgh, his Lord Chamberlain "was looking everywhere for pure Glenlivet whisky; the King drank nothing else". Elizabeth Grant of Rothiemurchus, who kept her family's cellar, sent up whisky from her own bin, which she called "the true contraband goût" (her memoir, written later, published 1898). This was illicit whisky: Smith's licence was still two years away.
- **Through the 1820s.** "Glenlivet" was used in newspapers and merchants' lists as a name for smuggled Highland whisky from around the Spey, as well as for the glen itself (Dramface, 2022, citing the Dundee Advertiser of 1822 and Edinburgh merchants' advertisements of the later 1820s; the newspapers themselves were not read).
- **Before 1824.** Iain Russell writes that "even Usher" used the word for whisky from the Glenlivet region before Smith's distillery was founded; this was one of the arguments Smith's opponents used in the 1880s (ScotchWhisky.com, 2017).

### Go to the source

- › _Elizabeth Grant of Rothiemurchus, Memoirs of a Highland Lady (John Murray, 1898), the 1822 chapter. Internet Archive, memoirsofhighlan00graniala. Read the Glenlivet passages in full. Public domain._
- › _Gallie MacOmish, "Ye Olde Glenlivet Whisky", Dramface, 23 April 2022. Read in full: the best short essay on Glenlivet as a style word._

> **TRAPS**
> The King's Glenlivet is often told as if he drank The Glenlivet. He could not have: the Grants' whisky was contraband, and Smith's distillery did not exist until 1824.

## Module 2. George Smith's licence, 1824

_When did Smith go legal, and what can be said about the danger he was in?_

### What's established

- **1823.** The Excise Act made it practical to distil legally in the Highlands, with a licence fee and lower duty (ScotchWhisky.com, 2018; Dramface, 2022).
- **10 November 1824.** The Glenlivet estate office records that George Smith "had the intention of going to Elgin this week to obtain the requisite licence" (Gordon Castle Muniments, quoted by Russell, 2025). **25 January 1825:** the Scottish Excise Board is told the Drumin distillery is "about to commence" (Excise Board minute book, quoted by Russell, 2025).
- Russell, an archival historian, says the record confirms Smith was the first licensed distiller **in Glenlivet**, "but not in Scotland, as was widely reported in the 1990s".
- **1859.** After a fire at Drumin, Smith moved to a new distillery at Minmore, which was named The Glenlivet distillery (Russell, 2017; Wikipedia, citing Pacult, 2005).
- **1871.** George Smith dies; his son John Gordon Smith carries on (Dramface, 2022; Wikipedia, citing Pacult).
- **The pistols.** The company and many writers say Smith carried a pair of pistols, a gift from his landlord, because his unlicensed neighbours threatened him (Difford's Guide; The Whiskey Wash; Dramface, "is said to have"). The story comes from Smith's own account given in old age; the original was not read. Told on the night as Smith's own story.

### Go to the source

- › _Iain Russell, "Scotland's Oldest? Some Examples of the Use and Misuse of Archives in the Writing of Scotch Whisky History", Business and Industrial History 34 (2025), University of Glasgow, doi 10.36399/BIH.34.598. Read in full._
- › _Iain Russell, "Andrew and John Usher", ScotchWhisky.com, 7 June 2017. Read in full._

> **TRAPS**
> "The first licensed distillery in Scotland" is wrong; say "the first in Glenlivet". The story that the 4th Duke of Gordon made a famous speech in the House of Lords that led to the 1823 Act has no record in Hansard or the newspapers; Russell traces it to The Glenlivet's brand story. Leave it out.

## Module 3. A place, a style and a blend

_How did one glen's name come to be used by so many whisky makers?_

### What's established

- **By 1843.** Andrew Usher senior, an Edinburgh spirits dealer, was receiving 600 gallons a month of Smith's whisky and advertising "The Real Glenlivet whisky" in London; his firm became Smith's sole agent south of the Tay (Russell, 2017).
- **1862.** Jerry Thomas's Bar-Tender's Guide, published in New York, says the whiskey for a Scotch Whiskey Punch "should be Glenlivet or Islay, of the best quality". Glenlivet is used here as a kind of Scotch, beside an island. (Read in the 1862 edition.)
- **1879.** The Ushers register "Usher's Old Vatted Glenlivet" as a trade mark. By the 1880s it is a blend: just over 12% Smith's Glenlivet, a little Royal Brackla, some malt from the Ushers' Edinburgh distillery, and about two-thirds grain whisky (Russell, 2017).
- **1880s.** Other merchants used the word for their own blends. In 1882 a Bombay merchant advertised "Treacher's Glenlivet Whisky", described in the same advertisement as "a blend" (Bombay Gazette, 10 June 1882; read in OCR only, which is rough).

### Go to the source

- › _Jerry Thomas, The Bar-Tender's Guide (Dick & Fitzgerald, New York, 1862), recipe 8. Internet Archive, bartendersguide01thom. Read the recipe. Public domain._
- › _Iain Russell, "Andrew and John Usher", ScotchWhisky.com, 2017. Read in full._

> **TRAPS**
> Usher's OVG is often called the first blended Scotch. Russell says there is "little concrete evidence" that the Ushers pioneered blending. Don't claim it.

## Module 4. The Smiths object, 1868 to 1881

_What did the Smiths say, and whom did they accuse?_

### What's established

- **1868.** George and John Gordon Smith advertise in the Wine Trade Review that Glenlivet "is a district which belongs exclusively to his Grace the Duke of Richmond", that their distillery "was the First and is now the Only Licensed Distillery in Glenlivet", and caution the trade against others selling "Glenlivet Whisky" (quoted by ScotchWhisky.com, 2018; the 1868 paper itself was not read).
- **1885.** The same notice, almost word for word, was still running in the Bombay Gazette, now under the heading "Glenlivet Whisky. Registered Trade Mark, Glenlivet" and naming the Duke of Richmond and Gordon (Bombay Gazette, 24 July 1885, read in OCR). This confirms the wording of the 1868 text from a second, independent printing.
- **1881.** In a written statement of his intention to go to law, John Gordon Smith complained that dealers in the big towns were selling "lower priced Whiskies of a different character" bought as "Glenfarclas Glenlivet or Cragganmore Glenlivet", so that buyers paid a Glenlivet price for them (quoted by ScotchWhisky.com, 2018; one source).
- **The registered mark.** By the 1880s the Smiths owned "Glenlivet" as a registered trade mark (Russell, 2017; the 1885 notice). ScotchWhisky.com says they first registered it two years after the 1868 notice and that a solicitor's error on re-registration in 1875 weakened it; those details rest on that one article.

### Go to the source

- › _The Whisky Professor, "Why are other distilleries called Glenlivet?", ScotchWhisky.com, 21 February 2018. Read in full: quotes the 1868 notice and the 1881 statement._
- › _The Bombay Gazette, 24 July 1885. Internet Archive, india.history.resource.29426. Read the Smith notice in OCR._

> **TRAPS**
> The Smiths' grievance is the source of the "name thieves" idea. It is their side of the argument. Their opponents' side is in Module 5.

## Module 5. The case, 1882 to 1884

_Was there a court ruling, and what did the settlement say?_

### What's established

- **1882.** John Gordon Smith went to the High Court of Justice to have other trade names containing "Glenlivet" removed from the Register of Trade Marks. Usher's agents abroad demanded that competitors stop using the word (Russell, 2017). ScotchWhisky.com (2018) says affidavits were served on each of the other distillers in 1882.
- **The other side.** An alliance of distillers and blenders petitioned to strike out Smith's own mark and attacked the Ushers' right to use the word for a blend that was mostly grain whisky. Their main argument: the trade had long used "Glenlivet" for whisky from the Glenlivet district, as "Islay" was used for Islay whisky (Russell, 2017; ScotchWhisky.com, 2018).
- **1884.** After about two years the parties settled out of court. The challenge to Smith's "Glenlivet" mark was dropped. Smith and the Ushers undertook not to interfere with a long list of distillers using the word in addition to the names of their distilleries; the hyphen came later. Others could sell "blended Glenlivet". The Ushers had to add "a blend of Glenlivet and other whiskies" to their OVG mark. Smith and the Ushers paid £3,000 of their opponents' costs as well as their own bill of up to £15,000 (Russell, 2017). Russell calls it a failure for Smith and the Ushers.
- **May 1884.** ScotchWhisky.com (2018) dates the deed to May 1884, says only Smith's whisky could now call itself "The Glenlivet", and names ten distilleries the Smiths agreed not to stop: Glenlossie, Macallan, Aberlour, Benrinnes, Cragganmore, Linkwood, Glenrothes, Glen Grant, Mortlach and Glenfarclas. Whisky Advocate, Difford's Guide and The Whiskey Wash also give 1884.

### Go to the source

- › _Iain Russell, "Andrew and John Usher", ScotchWhisky.com, 2017. Read in full. The best account of the case by a historian who has worked in the archives._
- › _The Whisky Professor, ScotchWhisky.com, 2018. Read in full._
- › _Whisky Advocate, "Why Glenlivet Is the Original Speyside Scotch". Read in full (one paragraph on the case)._

### Threads worth pulling

- The 1884 deed itself, the High Court papers and the hundreds of affidavits. The Scotch Whisky Industry Record (H. Charles Craig, 1994) cites "Glenlivet case affidavits"; it is a lending-only scan and was not read.
- The two accounts of the costs disagree: £30,000 by 1883 (ScotchWhisky.com, 2018) against up to £15,000 plus £3,000 (Russell, 2017). The deck gives Russell's figures and says they are his.

> **BUT DON'T OVERCLAIM IT**
> There was no court ruling. Dates of 1880 (Dramface; Whisky Magazine, 2011) and 1886 (The Spirits Business, as seen in the scouting notes) are not supported by the fuller accounts. The Whiskey Wash's "court order" is also wrong. Say "settled out of court in 1884". And say plainly that the settlement let the others keep the word: on the record, the Smiths lost most of what they asked for.

## Module 6. The longest glen, 1884 to now

_How far did the suffix spread, and who still uses it?_

### What's established

- **1900.** R. Thorne & Sons of Greenock advertise as owners of the "Aberlour-Glenlivet Distillery, Greenock Distillery and Glen Moray-Glenlivet Distillery". On another page The Glenlivet advertises as "Only Distillery in Glenlivet. Trade Mark, 'GLENLIVET.'"; Balmenach advertises as "Balmenach-Glenlivet Distillery, Limited" (Slater's Royal National Commercial Directory of Scotland, 1900 edition, advertisement pages 45, 46 and 73; read in OCR).
- **1898.** A Court of Session case is reported as Milton v Glen-Moray Glenlivet Distillery Co Ltd (1898) 1 F. 135 (cited in a 2009 law book; the report itself was not read).
- **The joke.** Because so many distilleries far from the glen carried the name, Glenlivet came to be called "the longest glen in Scotland" (Whisky Magazine, 2011; The Whiskey Wash). The joke appears in a 1966 company booklet, "The Glenlivet", and in whisky guides of the 1980s and 1990s (Internet Archive search snippets; the books are lending-only and were not read). When it was first said is not known. Told as a joke from the trade.
- **1967.** Heublein, the American importer, listed "J. & G. Grant's Glenfarclas-Glenlivet" among its brands (Heublein annual report, 1967; read).
- **1980s.** "By the 1980s, 28 distilleries were either registered as being 'X-Glenlivet' or using the term as a trademark, or on their labels" (ScotchWhisky.com, 2018). Dramface (2022) says "30-odd".
- **Dropping it.** Macallan dropped the suffix in recent decades (ScotchWhisky.com, 2018). The Aberlour company was "Aberlour-Glenlivet Distillery Company Limited" from 1950 until 15 June 2007 (Companies House, SC027975).
- **Still registered, November 2026.** "Glen Moray-Glenlivet Distillery Co. Ltd." (SC217748) is active; it took that name on 27 August 2003, having been "Glen Moray Limited". "Tomintoul Glenlivet Distillery Limited (The)" (SC093694) is active, incorporated 1985 (Companies House, checked November 2026). ScotchWhisky.com (2018) also names Tamnavulin and Speyburn as still registered with the suffix; not checked here.
- **On labels.** The independent bottler Wm Cadenhead still put "-Glenlivet" on its Speyside bottlings in 2018, because "using the original name of the distillery is something that we have always historically done" (Mark Watt, quoted by ScotchWhisky.com, 2018); Dramface says the same in 2022.

### Go to the source

- › _Slater's Royal National Commercial Directory of Scotland, 1900. Internet Archive, slatersroyalglas1899dire. Read the advertisement pages._
- › _Companies House records SC217748, SC027975 and SC093694, find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk. Read November 2026._
- › _Heublein Inc. annual report, 1967, in "Kentucky Fried Chicken Corporation and Heublein Inc. Annual Reports, 1959–1982", Internet Archive, kfcheubleinannualreports. Read the brand list._
- › _Whisky Magazine, "The Longest Glen", issue 96, 3 June 2011. Read in full._

> **TRAPS**
> Don't say the 1884 settlement "required" a hyphen. Russell says the hyphenation came later. And don't call Glen Moray one of the original ten: it was built thirteen years after the settlement.

## Claims that don't hold up

- "George Smith had the first licence in Scotland." The first in Glenlivet (Russell, 2025).
- "The Duke of Gordon's speech in the House of Lords led to the 1823 Act." No record of the speech (Russell, 2025).
- "A court ruled in 1880 (or 1886) that only Smith could use 'The Glenlivet'." The case was settled out of court in 1884.
- "The settlement made the others hyphenate." The hyphen came later (Russell, 2017).
- "The University of Glasgow journal article is about the Glenlivet case." It is about claims to be Scotland's oldest distillery; it touches Smith's licence and the Duke of Gordon myth, not the 1880s case.
- "Glen Moray was one of the distilleries allowed the suffix in 1884." It did not exist until 1897.

## Not confirmed

- **The 1868 Wine Trade Review notice.** Seen only as quoted by ScotchWhisky.com; its wording is supported by the 1885 Bombay Gazette printing. The script gives the year 1868 as that article's.
- **The 1881 statement naming Glenfarclas and Cragganmore.** One source (ScotchWhisky.com). Said aloud as Smith's words "as quoted".
- **The list of ten distilleries in the 1884 deed, and the month of May.** One source (ScotchWhisky.com). Russell names Benrinnes and Macallan as examples of a "long list". Glenfarclas and Aberlour are on the ten; the script says "one account lists".
- **The costs.** Russell's £15,000 and £3,000 against ScotchWhisky.com's £30,000 by 1883. The script gives Russell's and names him.
- **The 1870 registration and the 1875 solicitor's error.** One source. Left out of the slides.
- **The pistols.** Smith's own later account, retold by the company. Told as his story.
- **When the "longest glen" joke started.** Not found. Told as a joke from the trade.
- **Smith's distillery as the only one in the Livet valley.** Dramface says a "Glenlivat" distillery at Auchorachan, run by Captain William Grant, worked until 1852. One source; left out.
- **Tamnavulin and Speyburn still registered with the suffix.** One source (2018); not checked at Companies House. Not on the slides.
- **Cadenhead's still using the suffix in 2026.** Reported in 2018 and 2022; no current label seen.
- **Bottle cask details.** From retailer listings, not the producers' pages.
