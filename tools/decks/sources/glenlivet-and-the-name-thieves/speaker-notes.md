## Part One: before the night

### The one-sentence version

In the 1880s the family behind The Glenlivet went to law to stop other distilleries calling their whisky Glenlivet, settled out of court in 1884, and left the rest of Speyside free to add the name after their own, which some still do.

### What the night is

Four Speyside single malts, poured in the order they come into the story. The first is The Glenlivet itself. The other three come from distilleries that used "Glenlivet" after their own names: two that were in the fight in the 1880s, and one that was built after it and took the name anyway. No blind tasting; the bottles are on the table from the start. Be warm to all four. Nobody in this story did anything the settlement did not allow.

### How many bottles

Four. They are all Speyside malts of about the same age and strength, so the differences come from the stills and the casks, and the room has time to talk about each one.

### Cast the roles, then find the bottles

- **The one with "The" (glass 1).** The distillery that started the fight and kept the trade mark. Tonight: The Glenlivet 12 Year Old.
- **The one named in the complaint (glass 2).** A distillery John Gordon Smith named in 1881. Tonight: Glenfarclas 12.
- **The one in the settlement (glass 3).** A distillery on the list of those allowed to keep the word. Tonight: Aberlour 12 Double Cask.
- **The latecomer (glass 4).** A distillery built after 1884 that took the suffix and still has it in its company name. Tonight: Glen Moray 12.

## Part Two: the script

_SET UP: glass 1, The Glenlivet 12, poured. The other three bottles on the table. Water out._

## [SLIDE 1] Glenlivet and the Name Thieves

Welcome. Tonight is about one word on a whisky label, Glenlivet, and a fight over who was allowed to use it.

Glenlivet is a glen in Speyside, the valley of a small river called the Livet. For about sixty years after 1824 a family called Smith ran the best-known distillery there, and in the 1880s they went to law to stop other distillers from calling their whisky Glenlivet. In their eyes the others were stealing the name. That is where tonight's title comes from.

It is their side of the story. By the end of the night you'll hear the other side too, and taste four whiskies from both sides of the argument.

## [SLIDE 2] Pour 1 of 4: The Glenlivet 12

_Glass 1 should already be poured. If not, pour it now and keep talking._

We start with the distillery at the centre of it. This is The Glenlivet 12 Year Old, made at Minmore in the glen of the Livet. It is matured in American and European oak and bottled at 40 percent.

Note the word "The" on the front. Since the 1880s this is the only distillery that calls itself The Glenlivet, and the rest of tonight is about how that came about.

_Ask: "What's the first thing you notice: the fruit, the sweetness or the oak?"_

## [SLIDE 3] In 1822 "Glenlivet" meant smuggled Highland whisky

Before there was a Glenlivet distillery, there was Glenlivet whisky.

In 1822 King George IV visited Edinburgh. Elizabeth Grant of Rothiemurchus kept her family's cellar, and in her memoir she wrote that the King's Lord Chamberlain "was looking everywhere for pure Glenlivet whisky; the King drank nothing else." Her father had her send up whisky from her own store. She described it as long in wood and mild as milk, with "the true contraband goût". It had been made without a licence.

So "Glenlivet" meant two things. It was a place, and it was a name for good illicit whisky from around the Spey. One writer, Gallie MacOmish, has found newspapers and Edinburgh merchants using the word that way in the 1820s.

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> The King did not drink The Glenlivet. George Smith's distillery did not exist until 1824. What the King drank was the Grants' contraband.

## [SLIDE 4] George Smith licensed his still at Drumin in 1824

In 1823 Parliament passed an Excise Act that made it practical to distil legally in the Highlands. George Smith was a farmer and illicit distiller at Upper Drumin, on the Livet, on the Duke of Gordon's estate.

The estate's letter book records that in November 1824 Smith said he was going to Elgin "to obtain the requisite licence". In January 1825 the Excise Board was told his Drumin distillery was about to start. The historian Iain Russell, who has worked through those records, says Smith was the first licensed distiller in Glenlivet. Many books say he was the first in Scotland, which is wrong.

Smith's own story, told years later and repeated by the company, is that his unlicensed neighbours threatened him and his landlord, the Duke of Gordon, gave him a pair of pistols, which he carried for years. Take it as his story.

In 1859, after a fire at Drumin, he moved down the hill to a new distillery at Minmore and called it The Glenlivet. George Smith died in 1871, and his son John Gordon Smith took over.

## [SLIDE 5] Glenlivet was a style as well as a place

While Smith was building his business, the word was travelling without him.

By 1843 his agent in Edinburgh, Andrew Usher, was taking 600 gallons of Smith's whisky a month and advertising it in London as "The Real Glenlivet". In 1862 the American bartender Jerry Thomas wrote that the whiskey for a Scotch punch "should be Glenlivet or Islay, of the best quality." He meant a kind of Scotch, the way you'd say Islay.

And the Ushers built a blend called Old Vatted Glenlivet. By the 1880s, according to Russell, it was just over 12 percent Smith's whisky and about two-thirds grain whisky. Smith's own agent was selling a Glenlivet that was mostly not from Glenlivet.

_Ask: "Does anyone know a word on a label today that started as a place and became a style?"_

## [SLIDE 6] 1868: the Smiths warn the trade

In 1868 the Smiths put a notice in the Wine Trade Review. It said Glenlivet was a district belonging to the Duke of Richmond, who owned the land by then, and that their distillery "was the First and is now the Only Licensed Distillery in Glenlivet". They cautioned the trade against anyone else selling Glenlivet whisky.

The same notice, almost word for word, was still running in the Bombay Gazette in 1885, now headed "Registered Trade Mark, Glenlivet". By then they owned the word as a trade mark.

## [SLIDE 7] In 1881 Smith named two distilleries

In 1881 John Gordon Smith wrote down why he meant to go to court. The way it's quoted, he said dealers in the big towns were selling "lower priced Whiskies of a different character" that they had bought as "Glenfarclas Glenlivet or Cragganmore Glenlivet", so that customers paid a Glenlivet price for something else.

His complaint was about price and confusion. The distilleries he named were real and licensed, a few miles from his own, and in the next glass you can judge for yourself whether their whisky was a different class.

## [SLIDE 8] Pour 2 of 4: Glenfarclas 12

_Pour glass 2 now._

This is Glenfarclas, the first name in Smith's sentence. The distillery is at Ballindalloch, about six miles from The Glenlivet in a straight line. It was first licensed in 1836, and a farmer called John Grant bought it in 1865. His family still owns and runs it.

Glenfarclas used the suffix for a long time. In 1967 the American importer Heublein listed "J. & G. Grant's Glenfarclas-Glenlivet" among its brands.

The 12 is matured in sherry casks and bottled at 43 percent, so it's a little stronger and richer than glass 1.

_Ask: "Taste it beside glass 1. Would you have paid a Glenlivet price for this in 1881?"_

## [SLIDE 9] 1882 to 1884: the Glenlivet case

In 1882 John Gordon Smith went to the High Court of Justice in London to have other trade names containing "Glenlivet" taken off the register. The Ushers' agents abroad told competitors to stop using the word.

The other distillers and blenders fought back together. They asked for Smith's own trade mark to be struck out. Their main argument was that "Glenlivet" had been the trade's word for whisky from that district long before Smith had a licence, in the same way "Islay" was. They also went after the Ushers for selling a blend that was two-thirds grain whisky under the name.

After about two years both sides gave up and settled out of court, in 1884. There was no ruling. You'll see the date given as 1880 or 1886, and you'll read that a court decided it; the fuller accounts support neither.

## [SLIDE 10] What the 1884 settlement gave each side

Here's what the settlement did, from Russell's account.

Smith kept his trade mark, and from then on his was the only whisky called The Glenlivet. The others kept the right to use the word Glenlivet after the names of their own distilleries. One account lists ten of them: Glenlossie, Macallan, Aberlour, Benrinnes, Cragganmore, Linkwood, Glenrothes, Glen Grant, Mortlach and Glenfarclas. Merchants could still sell "blended Glenlivet". The Ushers had to add "a blend of Glenlivet and other whiskies" to their label.

And Smith and the Ushers paid £3,000 towards their opponents' costs, on top of their own bill of up to £15,000. Russell calls it a failure for them.

The Smiths thought the others were name thieves. The settlement they signed gave those distillers the right to use the word.

> **SAY THE CAVEAT OUT LOUD**
> The hyphen came later. The settlement let them use the word with their names; writing it "Aberlour-Glenlivet" became the habit afterwards. And the costs differ between accounts; another source says £30,000.

## [SLIDE 11] Pour 3 of 4: Aberlour 12 Double Cask

_Pour glass 3 now._

Aberlour is on that list. James Fleming, a local banker and grain dealer, built it in the village of Aberlour and started production at the end of 1880, the year before Smith's statement. It's about nine and a half miles from The Glenlivet.

The company that owned it was registered as the Aberlour-Glenlivet Distillery Company from 1950 until 2007. Aberlour now belongs to Pernod Ricard, the same group that owns The Glenlivet.

This is the Double Cask 12, matured in two kinds of oak, at 40 percent.

_Ask: "Of the three so far, which would you put closest to glass 1?"_

## [SLIDE 12] In 1900 one Greenock firm owned two "-Glenlivet" distilleries

After 1884 the suffix spread to distilleries that were not in the settlement. In the 1900 edition of Slater's directory of Scotland, a Greenock firm, R. Thorne & Sons, advertised that it owned the Aberlour-Glenlivet Distillery and the Glen Moray-Glenlivet Distillery. A few pages away, The Glenlivet's own advertisement still read "Only Distillery in Glenlivet", with its trade mark.

So many distilleries carried the name that the trade started calling Glenlivet "the longest glen in Scotland". I couldn't find who said it first. It's been repeated in whisky books since at least the 1960s.

## [SLIDE 13] Pour 4 of 4: Glen Moray 12

_Pour glass 4 now._

Glen Moray was not on the 1884 list, because it didn't exist yet. It was a brewery at Elgin that was turned into a distillery, and the company says the first spirit ran on 13 September 1897. Elgin is about 21 miles from The Glenlivet, near the Moray coast.

It took the suffix anyway. Companies House lists an active company called Glen Moray-Glenlivet Distillery Co. Ltd., which took that name in 2003.

The 12 is matured in ex-bourbon casks and bottled at 40 percent.

_Ask: "Lightest of the four, or not?"_

## [SLIDE 14] Miles from The Glenlivet, in a straight line

Here are tonight's four, measured in a straight line from The Glenlivet distillery. Glenfarclas about six miles, Aberlour about nine and a half, Glen Moray about twenty-one.

None of the other three is in the glen of the Livet. In 1884 the settlement treated that as acceptable as long as the distillery's own name came first.

## [SLIDE 15] Where the suffix is in 2026

By the 1980s, 28 distilleries were registered as something-Glenlivet, used it as a trade mark, or printed it on labels. Most have dropped it. Macallan did, and Aberlour's company changed its name in 2007.

A few company names still carry it. Companies House lists Glen Moray-Glenlivet Distillery Co. Ltd. and Tomintoul Glenlivet Distillery Limited as active companies today. On bottles, the independent bottler Cadenhead's was still printing names like Linkwood-Glenlivet on its Speyside bottlings in 2022; in 2018 Mark Watt of Cadenhead's said the firm had always used the distilleries' original names.

_Ask: "Now that you've had all four, which would you buy for the house?"_

## [SLIDE 16] Sláinte

A toast to George Smith and his son, who fought for the name, and to their neighbours, who kept it. Sláinte.

_Finish with whichever glass people want to go back to._
