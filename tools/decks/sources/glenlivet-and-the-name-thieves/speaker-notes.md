Speaker's notes

## Part One: before the night

### The one-sentence version

In the 1880s the family that owned The Glenlivet took legal action to stop other distilleries calling their whisky Glenlivet. The case was settled in 1884, and the other distilleries were allowed to keep the word as long as it came after their own name.

### What the night is

Four Speyside single malts, all about 12 years old. The first is The Glenlivet. The other three come from distilleries that used "Glenlivet" after their own names. This is not a blind tasting. The bottles are on the table from the start.

### How many bottles

Four.

### Cast the roles, then find the bottles

- **Glass 1: The Glenlivet.** The distillery that took legal action. Suggested bottle: The Glenlivet 12 Year Old.
- **Glass 2: a distillery the Smiths complained about by name.** Suggested bottle: Glenfarclas 12.
- **Glass 3: a distillery listed in the 1884 settlement.** Suggested bottle: Aberlour 12 Double Cask.
- **Glass 4: a distillery built after the settlement that used the name too.** Suggested bottle: Glen Moray 12.

## Part Two: the script

_SET UP: glass 1, The Glenlivet 12, poured. The other three bottles on the table. Water out._

## [SLIDE 1] Glenlivet and the Name Thieves

Welcome. Tonight is about the word Glenlivet and a court case over who could put it on a bottle.

Glenlivet is a place. It is the valley of a small river called the Livet, in Speyside. From 1824 a family called Smith ran a distillery there. In the 1880s they took legal action to stop other distilleries calling their whisky Glenlivet. The Smiths thought those distilleries were stealing the name, and that is the title of tonight's deck.

The Smiths did not win. We'll get to what happened, and we'll taste The Glenlivet next to three of the whiskies they were trying to stop.

## [SLIDE 2] Pour 1 of 4: The Glenlivet 12

_Glass 1 should already be poured. If not, pour it now and keep talking._

_Tell the room to keep a little of glass 1 back to compare with the other three._

This is The Glenlivet 12 Year Old. It is made at Minmore, in Glenlivet. It is aged in American and European oak and bottled at 40 percent.

Look at the label. It says "The Glenlivet", with "The". Since 1884 this has been the only distillery allowed to use that name.

_Ask: "What's the first thing you notice: the fruit, the sweetness or the oak?"_

## [SLIDE 3] In 1822 "Glenlivet" meant smuggled Highland whisky

In 1822 King George IV came to Edinburgh and asked for Glenlivet whisky. A woman called Elizabeth Grant wrote about it in her memoir. Her family had some in the cellar, and her father made her send it to the King. It was smuggled whisky. Nobody in Glenlivet had a licence yet.

At that time people said "Glenlivet" for any good smuggled whisky from that part of the Highlands. George Smith's distillery opened two years later, so the King never drank The Glenlivet.

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> People often say the King drank The Glenlivet. He did not. The distillery did not exist until 1824.

## [SLIDE 4] George Smith licensed his still at Drumin in 1824

George Smith was a farmer at Upper Drumin, in Glenlivet. Like his neighbours, he made whisky without a licence. In 1823 a new law made a licence cheap enough to be worth having, and in 1824 Smith took one. He was the first in Glenlivet to do it. A lot of books say he was the first in Scotland, and that is not true.

Smith said later that his neighbours threatened him for going legal, and that his landlord, the Duke of Gordon, gave him two pistols to protect himself. We only have Smith's word for that.

In 1859 he built a bigger distillery down the hill at Minmore and named it The Glenlivet. He died in 1871 and his son, John Gordon Smith, took over.

## [SLIDE 5] Glenlivet was a style as well as a place

Smith sold most of his whisky through an agent in Edinburgh, Andrew Usher. By 1843 Usher was taking 600 gallons a month and advertising it in London as "The Real Glenlivet".

Other people were using the word too. In 1862 the American bartender Jerry Thomas wrote that the whisky for a Scotch punch "should be Glenlivet or Islay". He was using Glenlivet as a type of Scotch.

Usher also made a blend called Old Vatted Glenlivet. By the 1880s about 12 percent of it was Smith's whisky and about two-thirds was grain whisky.

_Ask: "Can you think of another name on a label that started as a place and turned into a style?"_

## [SLIDE 6] 1868: the Smiths warn the trade

In 1868 the Smiths put a notice in a trade paper, the Wine Trade Review. It said theirs "was the First and is now the Only Licensed Distillery in Glenlivet", and it warned merchants not to buy Glenlivet whisky from anyone else.

They kept running that notice for years. It was still being printed in a Bombay newspaper in 1885.

## [SLIDE 7] In 1881 Smith named two distilleries

In 1881 John Gordon Smith wrote down what he was angry about. He said merchants were buying cheaper whisky from other distilleries and selling it to customers as Glenlivet. He named two of those distilleries: Glenfarclas and Cragganmore.

Both were legal, licensed distilleries a few miles from his. Glenfarclas is the next glass.

## [SLIDE 8] Pour 2 of 4: Glenfarclas 12

_Pour glass 2 now._

This is Glenfarclas 12. The distillery is at Ballindalloch, about six miles from The Glenlivet. A farmer called John Grant bought it in 1865, and his family still owns it.

Glenfarclas sold its whisky as Glenfarclas-Glenlivet for a long time. It was still being sold under that name in America in the 1960s.

The 12 is aged in sherry casks and bottled at 43 percent. It is a little stronger than glass 1.

_Ask: "Taste it next to glass 1. Smith said this was a cheaper whisky of a different kind. Do you agree?"_

## [SLIDE 9] 1882 to 1884: the Glenlivet case

In 1882 John Gordon Smith went to the High Court in London. He had registered Glenlivet as a trade mark, and he asked the court to remove every other trade mark that used the word.

The other distillers and merchants joined together against him. They said Glenlivet was the name of a district and a kind of whisky, the same as Islay, and that people had been using it since before Smith had a licence. They also pointed at Usher's blend, which was sold as Glenlivet and was two-thirds grain whisky.

The case went on for two years. In 1884 the two sides settled out of court. No judge ever ruled on it. Some books give the date as 1880 or 1886 and say the court decided it. That is wrong.

## [SLIDE 10] What the 1884 settlement gave each side

This is what the two sides agreed.

Smith kept his trade mark. From then on, only his whisky could be called The Glenlivet.

The other distilleries could use the word Glenlivet as long as it came after their own name. One account lists ten of them: Glenlossie, Macallan, Aberlour, Benrinnes, Cragganmore, Linkwood, Glenrothes, Glen Grant, Mortlach and Glenfarclas. Merchants could still sell "blended Glenlivet". Usher had to change his label to say his whisky was a blend of Glenlivet and other whiskies.

Smith and Usher also paid £3,000 of the other side's legal costs. Their own costs were up to £15,000.

So the Smiths called the others name thieves, and then signed an agreement that let them keep using the name.

> **IF SOMEONE ASKS**
> The hyphen, as in "Aberlour-Glenlivet", came later. The settlement only said the word could follow the distillery's name. The cost figures also differ between sources. One gives £30,000.

## [SLIDE 11] Pour 3 of 4: Aberlour 12 Double Cask

_Pour glass 3 now._

Aberlour was one of the ten on that list. A local banker, James Fleming, built it in the village of Aberlour, and it started making whisky at the end of 1880. It is about nine and a half miles from The Glenlivet.

The company was called Aberlour-Glenlivet until 2007. Today Aberlour and The Glenlivet have the same owner, Pernod Ricard.

This is the 12 Year Old Double Cask. It is aged in American oak and sherry casks and bottled at 40 percent.

_Ask: "Of the three so far, which is closest to glass 1?"_

## [SLIDE 12] In 1900 one Greenock firm owned two "-Glenlivet" distilleries

After 1884, distilleries that had not been part of the settlement started using the name as well. This slide comes from a Scottish trade directory printed in 1900. One company, R. Thorne & Sons, advertised two distilleries: Aberlour-Glenlivet and Glen Moray-Glenlivet. In the same directory, The Glenlivet's own advertisement said "Only Distillery in Glenlivet".

So many distilleries used the name that people in the whisky trade joked that Glenlivet was the longest glen in Scotland.

## [SLIDE 13] Pour 4 of 4: Glen Moray 12

_Pour glass 4 now._

Glen Moray was not on the 1884 list because it had not been built. It was a brewery in Elgin that was converted to a distillery in 1897. Elgin is about 21 miles from The Glenlivet, near the coast.

By 1900 it was calling itself Glen Moray-Glenlivet. There is still a company registered under that name.

The 12 is aged in bourbon casks and bottled at 40 percent.

_Ask: "Is this the lightest of the four?"_

## [SLIDE 14] Miles from The Glenlivet, in a straight line

This slide shows how far each of tonight's distilleries is from The Glenlivet in a straight line. Glenfarclas is about six miles away. Aberlour is about nine and a half. Glen Moray is about twenty-one.

None of those three is in Glenlivet. Under the settlement that did not matter, as long as their own name came first.

## [SLIDE 15] Where the suffix is in 2026

By the 1980s, 28 distilleries were using Glenlivet in their name. Most have stopped. Macallan dropped it, and Aberlour's company changed its name in 2007.

You can still find it in a few places. Glen Moray and Tomintoul both have companies registered with Glenlivet in the name. The independent bottler Cadenhead's was still printing names like Linkwood-Glenlivet on its labels in 2022.

_Ask: "Now that you've had all four, which one would you buy?"_
