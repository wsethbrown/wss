# Through the Charcoal

Research outline: Tennessee whiskey and Nearest Green

Tennessee whiskey and Nearest Green. A guide for anyone who wants to research the subject and build a talk of their own.

---

**A note on confidence.** The facts in this deck were checked against the sources listed under each module, in November 2026. What could not be confirmed is listed under "Not confirmed" near the end, and the deck either leaves it out or says it is unconfirmed.

## Module 1. The Lincoln County Process

**The question.** What is the charcoal step, what does it do to the spirit, and where did it come from?

### Nailed down

- New-make spirit runs through vats of sugar-maple charcoal before it is barrelled. Jack Daniel's describes its vats as ten feet tall with about ten feet of packed charcoal; the spirit is pumped in at the top and runs down by gravity. The maple is burned on site in ricks.
- The time the spirit takes is given variously as two to three days (Distiller, Difford's) and about ten days (one magazine). The script says only "days".
- The name refers to Lincoln County. Moore County, with Lynchburg as its seat, was formed in 1871 (Acts of 1871, chapter 96) from parts of Lincoln and neighbouring counties.
- George Dickel chills the spirit before the charcoal step. The company ties this to a belief that whisky made in winter was mellower; VinePair calls it legend. The chilling is a process fact; the attribution is company lore.

- Distiller, "Lincoln County Process": https://blog.distiller.com/lincoln-county-process/

- Tennessee Secretary of State, Moore County: https://sos.tn.gov/node/1396
- CTAS, Acts of 1871 Chapter 96: https://www.ctas.tennessee.edu/private-acts/acts-1871-chapter-96

### Primary documents

- Distillery technical descriptions (Jack Daniel's, Cascade Hollow).
- Published sensory or chemical studies comparing charcoal-treated and untreated spirit.

### Traps

- The claim that the method was invented by Alfred Eaton around 1825 rests on weak evidence. A search found nothing to support it. Name it as a story.
- The claim that the method came from West African practice brought by enslaved people has no documentary support found. Present it as a theory or leave it out.
- "Charcoal filtered" on non-Tennessee labels usually describes a filtration after ageing. It is a different step.

## Module 2. Dan Call, Nearest Green and the young Jack Daniel

**The question.** What do we know about Green, his position on Call's farm, and his teaching of Jack Daniel?

### Nailed down

- Nathan "Nearest" Green (sometimes spelled "Nearis") was enslaved and taught the young Jack Daniel to distil. This is the account the New York Times reported in 2016 and Jack Daniel's now tells.
- Dan Call, a preacher and grocer, employed the young Jack Daniel; for over a century the company credited Call with teaching him.
- Ownership: the 2016 Times article called Green one of Call's slaves. Later research cites documents showing he was owned by a firm, Landis & Green, which likely hired him out to Call. The deck gives both and says they differ.
- Risen himself wrote that the story rests on oral tradition and "the thinnest of archival trails".
- No photograph of Nearest Green is known to survive.
- Green's sons George and Eli worked for Daniel, and grandsons after them; the family and company count seven generations of descendants at the distillery (three still worked there in 2017).
- Fawn Weaver's book _Love & Whiskey_ (June 2024) sets out her research. Weaver notes that Ben Green's 1967 biography mentions Green and his sons more often than Daniel's own family.
- Dissent: the Whiskey Lore podcast (season 7, episode 9) argues no record places Green as an enslaved distiller under Call or Daniel in the way the modern account has it.

### Primary documents

- Ben A. Green, _Jack Daniel's Legacy_ (1967).
- U.S. censuses of 1860 (slave schedules), 1870 and 1880 for Lincoln and Moore counties.
- Lincoln and Moore County court, deed and tax records.
- The archive assembled by Fawn Weaver and the Nearest Green Foundation.

### Traps

- Do not say flatly that Green was Call's slave or that he was Landis & Green's; say the sources differ.
- Green's dates of birth and death are not firmly established. Do not give them.
- The seven-generations count comes from the family and the company. Present it as theirs.
- Dan Call is often called a Lutheran preacher; the sources checked say only "preacher". Leave the denomination out.
- Do not attribute direct quotations to Call, Green or Daniel unless they appear in a dated document.

## Module 3. Jack Daniel and the 1866 date

**The question.** When did Jack Daniel's own distillery begin?

### Nailed down

- The company uses 1866 and calls it the first registered distillery in the United States.
- Peter Krass, _Blood and Whiskey: The Life and Times of Jack Daniel_ (2004), puts the start at 1875, from registration records of a partnership with Dan Call effective November 27, 1875. An 1896 Nashville American article gave 1874 and a 1904 Bonfort's Wine and Spirit Circular gave 1872.
- Daniel's birth year is disputed: his headstone says 1850; Krass argues for January 1849.
- Jack Daniel handed the business to his nephew Lem Motlow in 1907 and died in 1911.

### Sources

- The Whiskey Wash, "Jack Daniel: The Mystery Surrounding America's Most Famous Distiller": https://thewhiskeywash.com/whiskey-articles/jack-daniel-the-mystery-surrounding-americas-most-famous-distiller/
- Wikipedia, "Jack Daniel" (citing Krass): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Daniel
- Jack Daniel's, "Our story": https://www.jackdaniels.com/our-story
- Tennessee Encyclopedia, "Jack Daniel Distillery": https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/?p=1037

### Primary documents

- Internal revenue assessment lists for the district covering Lynchburg (National Archives).
- Moore County deed records for Cave Spring Hollow.

### Traps

- The story that Daniel died from an infected toe after kicking his safe is told at the distillery and cannot be checked. Name it as a story.
- The origin of "Old No. 7" has several competing explanations and no settled answer.
- The photograph of Daniel with his workers is captioned online as showing Nearest Green. No photograph of Nearest is known. The 2016 Times article called the man beside Daniel "possibly one of Green's sons"; Jack Daniel's says it "has reason to believe" he is George Green, Nearest's son. The photograph is undated; secondary sources give dates from the late nineteenth century to about 1904. The slide calls it undated and gives the company's identification as the company's.

## Module 4. Tennessee prohibition, 1909–1938

**The question.** Why did Tennessee go dry so early, and how did Lynchburg get its distillery back?

### Nailed down

- Edward Ward Carmack, prohibitionist and editor, was shot dead in Nashville on November 9, 1908, by Robin Cooper; Robin and his father Duncan Cooper, an ally of Governor Malcolm R. Patterson, were convicted of second-degree murder, and in April 1910 Patterson pardoned Duncan Cooper. Accounts differ on who fired first.
- In January–February 1909 the legislature passed prohibition laws over Patterson's veto, including a ban on manufacture (chapter 10 of the Acts of 1909, passed over the veto on February 4, 1909, effective January 1, 1910).
- After the ban, Motlow moved distilling to St. Louis and to Birmingham, Alabama (Tennessee State Library and Archives; Tennessee Encyclopedia). The Birmingham dates are tangled with Jefferson County's own local prohibition.
- Motlow was elected to the Tennessee House in 1933 and the Senate in 1939.
- Tennessee repealed its prohibition in 1937; Moore County voted narrowly in 1938 to allow distilling, and the Lynchburg distillery reopened in 1938.
- Moore County remains dry; a special act of 1994 lets the distillery sell commemorative bottles.

- Tennessee State Library and Archives, "Saloon, Anarchy, Prohibition in Tennessee": passage of prohibition: https://digitaltennessee.tnsos.gov/exhibit/saloon-anarchy-prohibtion-in-tennessee/passage-of-prohibition/
- Tennessee State Library and Archives, "Saloon, Anarchy, Prohibition in Tennessee": brewing and distilling: https://digitaltennessee.tnsos.gov/exhibit/saloon-anarchy-prohibtion-in-tennessee/brewing-distilling-in-tennessee/
- Motlow v. State (Tennessee Supreme Court): https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/motlow-v-state-894880421
- Tennessee Encyclopedia, "Edward Ward Carmack": https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/?p=511
- Tennessee Encyclopedia, "Cooper v. State": https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/?p=626
- Tennessee Encyclopedia, "Malcolm R. Patterson": https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/?p=1414
- Tennessee Encyclopedia, "Jack Daniel Distillery": https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/?p=1037
- Wikipedia, "Lem Motlow": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lem_Motlow
- Wikipedia, "Alcohol laws of Tennessee": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_laws_of_Tennessee

### Primary documents

- Tennessee Public Acts, 1909, chapters 1 and 10.
- Tennessee Public Acts of 1937–1939 on manufacture and local option.
- Nashville newspapers on the Carmack killing and the Cooper trial (Chronicling America).

### Traps

- One popular account dates the Carmack killing to 1909; it was 1908.
- Sources differ on whether the 1938 reopening followed a 1937 repeal or a 1938 special act; the deck says the law changed in 1937 and the county voted in 1938.

## Module 5. George Dickel and Cascade

**The question.** Who was George Dickel, and what is the history of the Cascade distillery?

### Nailed down

- George A. Dickel (born 1818 in Germany) was a Nashville merchant who ran a wholesale liquor business. He was not a distiller.
- In 1888 his firm (through its partner, the Shwab family) bought into the Cascade distillery near Tullahoma and became its sole distributor. Dickel died in 1894; Victor E. Shwab later gained full control.
- Under Tennessee prohibition the Cascade brand was made in Louisville, Kentucky, until prohibition there.
- Schenley built a new distillery near the old site; Difford's gives 1958 for the rebuild, Wikipedia gives the first mash as July 4, 1959. The deck uses 1959.
- The distillery is Cascade Hollow Distilling Co., owned by Diageo. The company says its "whisky" spelling follows the Scottish tradition.

### Sources

- Tennessee Encyclopedia, "George Dickel Distillery": https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/?p=867
- Wikipedia, "George A. Dickel": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_A._Dickel
- Wikipedia, "George Dickel": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Dickel
- Difford's Guide, Cascade Hollow Distillery history: https://www.diffordsguide.com/producers/646/cascade-hollow-distillery-geo-a-dickel-and-co/history
- George Dickel, "Our story": https://www.georgedickel.com/en-us/our-story

### Primary documents

- Nashville city directories and newspaper advertising, 1870s–1900s.
- Records of Geo. A. Dickel & Co. and the Shwab family's acquisition of Cascade.

### Traps

- The story that Dickel founded the distillery on a trip in 1870 has no supporting evidence.
- Sources name the 1888 buyer differently (John F. Shwab, Victor Shwab, the firm). The deck says "his firm".
- Pre-Prohibition Cascade whisky was made with a different process and owners from today's. Avoid suggesting continuity of recipe.

## Module 6. The 2013 law and the bourbon question

**The question.** What does the law say Tennessee whiskey is, and is it bourbon?

### Nailed down

- House Bill 1084, signed by Governor Bill Haslam on May 13, 2013 (2013 Tenn. Acts ch. 341, effective July 1, 2013), now Tenn. Code Ann. § 57-2-106: made in Tennessee; at least 51% corn; distilled to no more than 160 proof; aged in new charred oak barrels in Tennessee; filtered through maple charcoal before ageing; barrelled at no more than 125 proof; bottled at not less than 80 proof. Brown-Forman lobbied for it.
- Benjamin Prichard's, Kelso, was exempted from the charcoal step. A later state attorney general's opinion said the exemption might be unconstitutional.
- In 2014 a bill introduced at Diageo's request would have allowed used barrels and ageing outside Tennessee; the Senate sent it to a summer study and it went no further.
- The federal bourbon standard (27 CFR 5.143) has the same corn, proof and new charred oak rules; the state rules mostly echo it.
- NAFTA (in force January 1, 1994), Annex 313, recognised Bourbon Whiskey and Tennessee Whiskey as distinctive US products; the USMCA (Annex 3-C) keeps "Tennessee Whiskey, which is a straight Bourbon Whiskey authorized to be produced only in the State of Tennessee".

### Sources

- Justia, Tenn. Code § 57-2-106 (2014 and 2019 editions): https://law.justia.com/codes/tennessee/2019/title-57/chapter-2/section-57-2-106/
- NPR, "It's Not Tennessee Whiskey If It's Aged In Kentucky, State Says" (2014): https://www.npr.org/sections/thesalt/2014/06/19/321080522/its-not-tennessee-whiskey-if-its-aged-in-kentucky-state-says
- AP via KSL, "Exemption in 'Tennessee Whiskey' law raises legal flags": https://www.ksl.com/article/33886270
- Time, "Tennessee whisky bill dies in Senate" (2014): https://time.com/37275/tennessee-whisky-bill-dies-in-senate
- Manufacturing.net, "Effort To Rewrite Tenn. Whiskey Law Fails": https://www.manufacturing.net/home/news/13204329/effort-to-rewrite-tenn-whiskey-law-fails
- eCFR, 27 CFR Part 5 Subpart I: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-27/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-5/subpart-I
- GrayRobinson, "The USMCA and America's Alcohol Industry": https://www.gray-robinson.com/article/post/2639/the-usmca-and-americas-alcohol-industry
- Distilled Spirits Council NAFTA comments (2017): https://www.distilledspirits.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Distilled_Spirits_Council_-_NAFTA_Modernization_Written_Comments_HWM_2017.pdf

### Traps

- The claim that charcoal treatment "disqualifies" Tennessee whiskey from being bourbon is marketing, not law.
- "Sour mash" is a process term with no special legal meaning and is used by most bourbon distilleries.
- NAFTA was signed in 1992 and took effect in 1994; do not say it was signed in 1994.

## Module 7. Recovering Green's story, 2016 onward

**The question.** How did Green come back into the company's story, and how reliable is the modern account?

### Nailed down

- Clay Risen's article "Jack Daniel's Embraces a Hidden Ingredient: Help From a Slave" ran on the front page of the New York Times on June 25, 2016. It began with a pitch from a Jack Daniel's publicist tied to the 150th anniversary.
- Fawn Weaver read it in Singapore, researched Green's life, set up a foundation in his name and founded the Uncle Nearest brand. Risen wrote a follow-up on her work in 2017.
- Jack Daniel's calls Green "Jack's first Master Distiller" on its own site.
- Uncle Nearest Inc. was placed in federal receivership in August 2025 (Farm Credit Mid-America, over $100 million in loans; receiver Phillip G. Young Jr.). A Chapter 11 filing by Weaver was dismissed in March 2026; in May 2026 the court found the company insolvent and refused to end the receivership; the receiver removed Fawn and Keith Weaver effective June 1, 2026, and as of October 2026 was negotiating a sale. Products stayed on shelves under the receiver, but supply after a sale cannot be relied on, so pour four was changed.

### Sources

- The Times article and Gastropod, as in Module 2.
- Jack Daniel's, "How Jack Daniel Came to Make Whiskey", as in Module 2.
- The Spirits Business, "Court says Uncle Nearest was 'haemorrhaging money'" (May 2026): https://www.thespiritsbusiness.com/2026/05/court-says-uncle-nearest-was-haemorrhaging-money/
- The Spirits Business, "Receivers close in on Uncle Nearest sale" (October 2026): https://www.thespiritsbusiness.com/2026/10/receivers-close-in-on-uncle-nearest-sale/
- Bloomberg, "Uncle Nearest Nears Sale as Receivership, Federal Probe Weigh on Whiskey Maker" (October 12, 2026): https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-12/embattled-whiskey-brand-uncle-nearest-eyes-potential-sale

### Primary documents

- The New York Times article (2016).
- Brown-Forman and Jack Daniel's statements from 2016–2017.
- Weaver's published research and the materials of the Nearest Green Foundation.

### Traps

- Weaver's purchase of the Dan Call farm and the 2020 Nearest & Jack Advancement Initiative are unconfirmed (see Not confirmed); do not cite them without checking.
- Uncle Nearest's ownership after the receiver's sale. Check before reinstating its bottle.
- Weaver's research is the main modern source and she has a commercial stake in it. Note this without dismissing the work.

## The four pours

- **Pour 1. Jack Daniel's Old No. 7.** The reference Tennessee whiskey; judge body and weight
- **Pour 2. George Dickel Signature Recipe (formerly No. 12).** The same process at another distillery, chilled before the charcoal
- **Pour 3. Buffalo Trace.** A Kentucky bourbon at the same proof as the Dickel, with no charcoal step
- **Pour 4. Jack Daniel's Bonded.** The distillery that names Green its first master distiller, bottled in bond at 100 proof

Uncle Nearest 1856 was the original pour four; it was replaced because its maker is in receivership and being sold (Module 7). Jack Daniel's Bonded is listed by US retailers (Total Wine, Whole Foods, QFC) as a 700 ml bottled-in-bond Tennessee whiskey at 100 proof (50% ABV); a one-litre version is sold in travel retail. Sources: https://www.totalwine.com/spirits/american-whiskey/tennessee-whiskey/jack-daniels-bonded-tennessee-whiskey/p/245570700 and https://www.wholefoodsmarket.com/product/jack-daniels-bonded-tennessee-whiskey-700-ml-b0b4x4bybb .

## Not confirmed

- Alfred Eaton as originator of the charcoal step, and a West African origin for it. Searched for both; found nothing for either. The script names them as unsupported stories. What would settle it: 1820s records of Eaton, or scholarship on African charcoal filtration.
- Exactly how long the spirit takes to pass through the charcoal (sources give two to ten days). The script says "days". Settled by the distillery's own production figures.
- The precise date and identification of the group photograph on slide 6. Searched company, press and archive descriptions; it is undated, and the identification is the company's belief. Settled by the original print's provenance.
- Whether Lynchburg itself lay in Lincoln County before 1871 (Moore was formed partly from Lincoln, Bedford and Franklin). The deck says only that Moore County was formed partly from Lincoln County in 1871. The 1871 act's boundary description would settle it.
- Weaver's purchase of the former Dan Call farm, and the 2020 Nearest & Jack Advancement Initiative. Not searched; not used in the deck.
- The charcoal postcard's publisher and date. Settled by the Commons file page or the postcard's reverse.
