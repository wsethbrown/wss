## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] Through the Charcoal

Thanks for coming. Tonight is Tennessee whiskey, which in practice means Jack Daniel's, George Dickel and a few newer names. Two things.

The first is the one step that makes Tennessee whiskey different from bourbon, which is running the new spirit through charcoal before it goes into the barrel. The second is a man called Nathan Green, usually known as Nearest Green, who was enslaved, who ran a still near Lynchburg, and who taught Jack Daniel how to make whiskey. He was left out of the company's own account for a long time, and I'll try to be careful about what we know and what we don't.

## [SLIDE 2] Maple charcoal, between the still and the barrel

First the charcoal. At Jack Daniel's they buy sugar maple, cut it into strips, stack it in square piles called ricks, and burn it outdoors until it's charcoal. The postcard on the title slide shows a man hosing down a burning pile so it chars instead of turning to ash.

The charcoal is broken up into small pieces and packed into tall vats, about ten feet deep. The new spirit, straight off the still and completely clear, is dripped in at the top and left to work its way down. It takes days to get through.

Only after that does it go into new charred oak barrels, the same as bourbon. So the charcoal comes before the ageing. Other whiskeys that say "charcoal filtered" usually get a quick pass through charcoal after the barrel, just before bottling, which is a different thing.

## [SLIDE 3] Called the Lincoln County Process

The industry name for this is the Lincoln County Process. In 1871 the state carved Moore County, with Lynchburg as its seat, partly out of Lincoln County, hence the name. The distillery calls it charcoal mellowing.

What the charcoal does is take out some of the oils and heavier flavours that come off a still. New spirit run through charcoal tastes cleaner and a bit sweeter than the same spirit before it went in.

What people argue about is how much of the difference survives four or more years in a barrel. The distilleries say a lot; some Kentucky distillers say not much. Two of tonight's pours are there so you can try the comparison yourselves.

As for who first did it, nobody knows. You'll see it credited to a distiller called Alfred Eaton in the 1820s, and that story rests on very little. You'll also hear that it came from techniques enslaved Africans brought with them. I've found no evidence for either.

## [SLIDE 4] Dan Call's farm, near Lynchburg, around 1860

Now the people. Around 1860, just outside Lynchburg, a man named Dan Call kept a store, preached on Sundays, and ran a still on his farm.

The man who ran the still was Nathan Green, called Nearest, or "Uncle Nearest" by the people who wrote about him later. He was enslaved. The sources differ on who owned him. The New York Times, in 2016, called him one of Call's slaves. Later research found documents saying he was owned by a firm called Landis & Green, which probably hired him out to Call for a fee. Hiring out enslaved people was a common arrangement.

The third person is Jack Daniel, who was a boy at the time and had gone to work for Call. The account that's been handed down is that Call put him with Green, and Green taught him how to make whiskey.

Most of what we know about this comes from a 1967 biography of Jack Daniel, written by Ben Green (no relation, as far as anyone knows), and from what people in Lynchburg remembered. Clay Risen, who broke the story in the New York Times, put it plainly: it rests on oral tradition and "the thinnest of archival trails". Very little written down on the farm itself survives.

## [SLIDE 5] 1866: The date on every bottle of Jack Daniel's

This is Jack Daniel himself. Every bottle says 1866, and the company has long described that as the year his distillery was registered.

Peter Krass's biography, published in 2004, went through the registration records and put the start of Daniel's own business at 1875, in partnership with Dan Call. Papers of 1896 and 1904 gave 1874 and 1872. The company still uses 1866. I'd say the later dates are better supported, but it isn't settled.

His age is uncertain too. His headstone says 1850; Krass argues for January 1849.

## [SLIDE 6] After 1865, Green worked for Daniel as a free man

Slavery ended in 1865. Green stayed on and worked for Daniel as a free man, and when Daniel set up on his own, Green went with him. Today the company calls Green its first master distiller.

Two of Green's sons, George and Eli, worked for Daniel, and grandsons after them. The family and the company count seven generations of Green descendants who have worked at the distillery.

This photograph shows Jack Daniel in the white hat with some of his distillery workers. It isn't dated. You'll see it online captioned as Nearest Green, but no photograph of him is known to survive. The Times in 2016 described the man on the left as possibly one of Green's sons. Jack Daniel's now says it has reason to believe he is George Green, Nearest's son. Nobody has confirmed that from a document.

## [SLIDE 7] Pour 1 of 4: Jack Daniel's Old No. 7

Let's have the first one. This is plain Jack Daniel's, the black label, at 80 proof. Nobody knows where the "Old No. 7" name came from; there are several stories.

Before you think about flavour, notice how thick or thin it feels in your mouth. The point of the charcoal is to make the spirit lighter, so this is the one to judge that on.

## [SLIDE 8] 1910: Tennessee bans making whiskey, ten years before the rest of the country

Tennessee went dry early. In 1909 the legislature passed laws banning the sale of liquor across nearly the whole state and banning its manufacture from 1910. The governor, Malcolm Patterson, vetoed them, and the legislature passed them again over his veto.

Part of the reason it went through so easily was a killing. In 1908 a prohibitionist politician and newspaper editor, Edward Carmack, was shot dead in the street in Nashville by men who were political allies of Patterson. Patterson later pardoned one of them, and the prohibition campaign gained a great deal from it.

## [SLIDE 9] Lynchburg from 1907 to 1938

Jack Daniel never married and had no children. In 1907 he handed the business to his nephew, Lem Motlow, who'd been working for him for years.

Three years later the state ban came in. The Tennessee State Library says Motlow moved distilling out of the state, to St. Louis and to Birmingham, Alabama, until Prohibition closed those too. Jack Daniel died in 1911.

When national Prohibition ended in 1933, Tennessee didn't follow straight away. Motlow, elected to the state House in 1933, worked to change the law, the state changed it in 1937, Moore County voted narrowly in 1938 to allow distilling, and that year the Lynchburg distillery opened again. So the town went 28 years without legal whiskey. Moore County is still dry; the distillery can sell bottles only under a special act of 1994.

## [SLIDE 10] George Dickel, a Nashville merchant

The other old name in Tennessee whiskey is George Dickel, and he's a different sort of figure. He was a German-born merchant who ran a wholesale liquor business in Nashville. He didn't distil. In 1888 his firm bought into the Cascade distillery in Cascade Hollow, near Tullahoma, and became its sole distributor.

Dickel died in 1894. When the state ban came, the Cascade brand was made in Louisville, Kentucky, until Prohibition there. It only came back to Tennessee when Schenley built a new distillery near the old site; it first distilled in 1959.

The company says Dickel himself thought his whisky tasted better in winter, and that's why they chill the spirit before it goes through the charcoal. I know of no evidence for it, so treat it as company lore. The chilling is real. The company spells whisky without an e, which it says follows the Scottish spelling.

## [SLIDE 11] Pour 2 of 4: George Dickel Signature Recipe

Second pour. This is George Dickel Signature Recipe, which was called No. 12 until recently; the whisky is the same. It's made at Cascade Hollow, which Diageo owns.

Same kind of charcoal step, but a different distillery and recipe, chilled before the charcoal, and 90 proof rather than 80. Taste it against the Jack Daniel's and see if you notice a difference in sweetness and body.

## [SLIDE 12] Tennessee whiskey in state law, 2013

For most of its history "Tennessee whiskey" was just a description. In 2013 the state made it a legal term. Governor Bill Haslam signed the bill in May, and Brown-Forman, which owns Jack Daniel's, lobbied for it.

These are the requirements. At least 51 percent corn in the mash. Distilled to no more than 160 proof. Into the barrel at no more than 125 proof. Bottled at 80 proof or more. Aged in new charred oak. Made and aged in Tennessee. And filtered through maple charcoal before ageing.

If those numbers look familiar, it's because all of them except the last two are copied from the federal definition of bourbon.

Diageo, which owns Dickel, objected that the law was written around Jack Daniel's, particularly the new-oak rule, and in 2014 backed a bill to allow used barrels and ageing outside the state. It was sent to a study committee and went no further. One exception was written in from the start: Prichard's, a small distillery in Kelso, which had been making Tennessee whiskey without charcoal for years and was allowed to carry on.

## [SLIDE 13] Bourbon and Tennessee whiskey

Put the two side by side. Bourbon has to be at least 51 percent corn and aged in new charred oak, and it can be made in any state. Tennessee whiskey meets every one of those rules and then adds the charcoal step and the state requirement.

That means Tennessee whiskey is, legally, bourbon. It meets the federal definition, and NAFTA, in force from 1994, listed Tennessee whiskey as a straight bourbon that may only be made in Tennessee. Its successor, the USMCA, keeps that wording.

Jack Daniel's doesn't put "bourbon" on the label, and has said the charcoal makes it something else. That's a choice about branding.

## [SLIDE 14] Pour 3 of 4: Buffalo Trace

Third pour, and this one's from Kentucky: Buffalo Trace, from the distillery of the same name in Frankfort.

It's a bourbon with no charcoal step before the barrel. It's also 90 proof, the same as the Dickel, which is why it's here. Taste them one after the other. Same proof, and the main process difference is the charcoal. Whether you can pick that out after years in the barrel is the argument from slide three.

## [SLIDE 15] 2016: The New York Times reports on Nearest Green, June 2016

Back to Nearest Green. His name was never completely lost. It's in the 1967 biography, and people in Lynchburg knew about him. But for most of the twentieth century the company's story was about Jack Daniel and Lem Motlow, and Green wasn't in the tours or the advertising.

In 2016, the year Jack Daniel's marked 150 years from its 1866 date, the company's publicists offered Green's story to the press as part of the anniversary. Clay Risen's article, "Jack Daniel's Embraces a Hidden Ingredient: Help From a Slave", ran on the front page of the New York Times on June 25, 2016, and got a lot of attention.

One of the people who read it was Fawn Weaver, a writer and businesswoman, who was in Singapore at the time. She began researching Green's life and family, set up a foundation in his name, and Risen wrote about her work the following year. A lot of what's now said about Green comes from that research. Jack Daniel's now calls Green its first master distiller.

## [SLIDE 16] What the record supports, and where it is thin

I want to be careful here, because Green's story gets told with more certainty than the records give it.

What's well supported: Green was enslaved, he worked a still near Lynchburg, and he taught the young Jack Daniel. That's in the 1967 biography and the local memory, the company accepts it, and it calls him its first master distiller. Not everyone is convinced even of that much. The Whiskey Lore podcast has argued that no record places Green as a distiller working under Call or Daniel in the way the modern story has it.

What's thin: we don't have firm dates for his birth or death. We don't know exactly what he taught Daniel, and in particular whether the charcoal step came from him. The idea that he brought the method from West African practice is a theory, and I haven't seen evidence for it. We can't be sure who's in the photographs either.

It's also fair to say that the main modern researcher is someone with a stake in the story. Weaver went on to found a whiskey company named after Green, Uncle Nearest. She has been open about that, and her research, set out in her 2024 book _Love & Whiskey_, has been widely accepted, but it's worth knowing when you weigh what you read.

## [SLIDE 17] Pour 4 of 4: Jack Daniel's Bonded

Last pour. You might have expected Uncle Nearest 1856 here, from Weaver's company. I've left it out because the company has been in federal receivership since August 2025, after its lender sued over more than $100 million in loans, and the receiver is trying to sell it. Nobody can say what will be on the shelf.

So we go back to Lynchburg, to the distillery that now calls Green its first master distiller. This is Jack Daniel's Bonded. Bottled in bond means one distillery, one distilling season, at least four years in a government-bonded warehouse, and bottled at exactly 100 proof. It's the strongest of the four, so taste it neat, then with a few drops of water, and compare it with the first pour.

When you're done, go back over all four. I'll ask for a show of hands on which one you'd buy.

## [SLIDE 18] Reading a Tennessee whiskey label

To finish, a guide to what the words on a Tennessee whiskey label mean.

"Tennessee whiskey" has had a legal definition since 2013: bourbon's rules, made in Tennessee, through maple charcoal before the barrel. "Bourbon" is the federal category, and Tennessee whiskey qualifies even though the label rarely says so.

"Sour mash" means some of the spent mash from one batch is added to the next, to keep conditions steady. It's printed on Jack Daniel's, but most Kentucky bourbon is made the same way and doesn't bother to say so.

"Charcoal filtered" on other whiskeys usually means a quick pass after ageing, a different step.

Whisky with or without an e makes no legal difference in the United States.
