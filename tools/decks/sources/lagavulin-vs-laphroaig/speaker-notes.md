# Lagavulin vs Laphroaig

Speaker's notes

The Whiskey Share Society

_READ THIS FIRST. Two parts of this story come only from Laphroaig: that Peter Mackie cut off its water, and that he copied its stills. The script says "Laphroaig says" for both. Keep that in when you tell it._

## Part One: before the night

### The one-sentence version

For about sixty years the firm that ran Lagavulin also sold all of Laphroaig's whisky. Laphroaig ended that in 1907. The next year Lagavulin's owner, Peter Mackie, built a small distillery inside Lagavulin to make whisky like Laphroaig. It never tasted like Laphroaig.

### What the night is

Four whiskies and fifteen slides, about 45 minutes. All four whiskies are connected to Peter Mackie. The first is from a Speyside distillery he invested in. The second is his blend. The third is from his own distillery, Lagavulin. The fourth is Laphroaig. This is not a blind tasting.

Most of what we know about the quarrel comes from Laphroaig. Nobody has found Mackie's version.

### How many bottles

Four. Two of them are heavily peated, and after that much smoke it is hard to taste anything else.

### Cast the roles, then find the bottles

- **Glass 1: an unpeated malt from the Speyside distillery Mackie invested in.** Tonight: Craigellachie 13.
- **Glass 2: Mackie's blend, which was built on Lagavulin.** Tonight: White Horse.
- **Glass 3: Lagavulin, Mackie's own distillery.** Tonight: Lagavulin 16.
- **Glass 4: Laphroaig, the whisky Mackie sold and then tried to copy.** Tonight: Laphroaig 10.

## Part Two: the script

_SET UP: four glasses each, numbered 1 to 4. Water on the table. Pour glass 1 during slide 2._

## [SLIDE 1] Lagavulin vs Laphroaig

Welcome, and thanks for coming.

Tonight is about two distilleries on the south coast of Islay. They are Lagavulin and Laphroaig, and they are about two miles apart on the same road.

For about sixty years the firm that ran Lagavulin also sold all the whisky Laphroaig made. In 1907 Laphroaig's owners ended that. The next year the owner of Lagavulin, Peter Mackie, built a small distillery to make whisky like Laphroaig.

We have four whiskies tonight. All four are connected to Mackie.

## [SLIDE 2] Peter Mackie, 1855 to 1924

_Pour glass 1 now, the Craigellachie. Keep talking while you pour._

This is Peter Mackie. The drawing is from the magazine Vanity Fair in April 1908. The caption under it says "Restless Peter".

Mackie was born in 1855 near Stirling. His father was a farmer and grain merchant. At 23 he went to work for his uncle, who had a whisky business in Glasgow. The uncle's partners were the Graham family, who ran Lagavulin. Peter was sent to Lagavulin to learn how whisky was made. His uncle died in 1889 and Peter took over the firm.

One writer described him as "one third genius, one third megalomaniac and one third eccentric".

## [SLIDE 3] Glass 1: Craigellachie 13

Glass 1 is Craigellachie 13. It is bottled at 46 percent.

Craigellachie is on Speyside. The distillery was built in 1890 and 1891 at a place where four railway lines met. Mackie was one of several investors. His firm bought the whole distillery in 1916, and most of its whisky went into his blends.

There is no peat in this one, so we are having it first. The distillery cools its spirit the old way, in coils of copper pipe that sit in tanks of cold water. They are called worm tubs.

_Ask: "What do you taste in it?"_

## [SLIDE 4] Two distilleries two miles apart

Now Islay. In the mid-1880s a writer called Alfred Barnard went round the distilleries of Britain and Ireland for a book. He visited Lagavulin and Laphroaig, and this slide shows what he found.

Lagavulin was the bigger one. It made about 75,000 gallons a year. Its water came from two lochs on the hill. Some of its whisky was sold by itself as a single whisky, and Barnard thought that was unusual.

Laphroaig made about 23,000 gallons a year. Its water came from a small stream that ran through the property. Most of its whisky went to blenders.

Both dried their malt over peat and nothing else.

Barnard wrote one more thing about Laphroaig. All of its whisky was sold by Mackie & Co of Glasgow, and he was told that had been so for sixty years.

## [SLIDE 5] For sixty years one firm sold both

Laphroaig was started by two brothers called Johnston. The company gives the year as 1815. Lagavulin got its licence in 1816. By the late 1830s a Glasgow family called Graham had Lagavulin.

In 1847 the owner of Laphroaig, Donald Johnston, fell into a vat of hot liquid at the distillery. He died two days later. His son was eleven. Laphroaig was leased to Graham of Lagavulin, who ran it until the son was old enough.

The son took over in 1857. The Lagavulin firm stayed on as Laphroaig's agent. That means it sold Laphroaig's whisky for it. This went on until 1907. By the 1880s the Lagavulin firm was Mackie & Co.

## [SLIDE 6] White Horse, registered in 1890

Mackie registered the name White Horse for his blend in 1890. The name came from an old coaching inn in Edinburgh. The Mackie family had owned property next to it.

White Horse was sold abroad first. It went on sale in Britain in 1901. Between 1902 and 1914 Mackie spent £90,000 advertising it in Britain. Sales there went from 700 cases a year to 70,000.

The blend was built on Lagavulin, with Craigellachie in it as well.

## [SLIDE 7] Glass 2: White Horse

_Pour glass 2 now, the White Horse._

This is White Horse. It is 40 percent. Diageo makes it now. Shops that sell it still say Lagavulin is a big part of it. Diageo does not publish the recipe.

The Craigellachie was 46 percent and this is 40. We are going in order of smoke tonight, not strength.

_Ask: "Can you taste any smoke in it?"_

## [SLIDE 8] 1907: Laphroaig ends Mackie's agency

In 1907 the man who ran Laphroaig, Alexander Johnston, died. The distillery went to his two sisters and his nephew. They thought Mackie was not giving them a fair deal. They took him to court and ended his agency.

The next part comes from Laphroaig. Laphroaig says Mackie then cut off the water to its distillery. The older version of the story says he had his men pull stones out of the channel that carried the water. Laphroaig's website says he blocked the stream with rocks. In both versions a court made him put it back.

> **IF SOMEONE ASKS**
> Most accounts give 1907 for the end of the agency. One gives 1908. The water story comes only from Laphroaig, and nobody has found Mackie's side of it.

## [SLIDE 9] Glass 3: Lagavulin 16

_Pour glass 3 now, the Lagavulin._

This is Lagavulin 16, from Mackie's own distillery. It is 43 percent.

Mackie always said Lagavulin got its flavour from its water, which runs down from the lochs over peat and moss. Barnard was told the same thing in the 1880s.

Mackie died in 1924. In 1927 his firm joined the Distillers Company, and Diageo owns Lagavulin today. In 1988 the 16 was chosen as one of six whiskies sold together as the Classic Malts.

Keep some of this in the glass. The Laphroaig is next and you will want to compare them.

_Ask: "What do you taste in this one?"_

## [SLIDE 10] 1908: Malt Mill, inside Lagavulin

In 1908 Mackie built a second, small distillery inside Lagavulin. He called it Malt Mill. He wanted it to make whisky like Laphroaig.

Malt Mill had its own malting floor, its own peat kiln and two stills. The story is that the stills were copies of Laphroaig's and that Mackie hired Laphroaig's brewer to run the place.

It did not work. Malt Mill's whisky was nothing like Laphroaig. It went into Mackie's blends, and White Horse was one of them.

Malt Mill kept going after Mackie died. It stopped around 1960, and some accounts say 1962. Its stills were moved into Lagavulin. Nobody has a bottle of Malt Mill that has been proved genuine. In the 2012 film _The Angel's Share_ the lost cask is a cask of Malt Mill.

## [SLIDE 11] 1887: Barnard on two distilleries a short distance apart

Barnard's book came out in 1887. That was 21 years before Mackie built Malt Mill.

At the end of his pages on Laphroaig, Barnard says a distiller cannot change the character of a whisky. He says it depends on the place, the water and the position of the distillery. The example he gives is Lagavulin and Laphroaig. They are a short distance apart, and each makes a different kind of whisky.

He also says Laphroaig's owners would not change anything at the distillery, in case the whisky changed.

## [SLIDE 12] Laphroaig sells its own whisky

In 1908 the family sent the nephew, Ian Hunter, to Islay to look after the distillery. He was an engineer. Money was short because of the court cases.

Laphroaig says Mackie tried twice more to get the distillery. When Laphroaig's lease came up, he offered to pay more rent for it. In 1921 the estate sold its land, and he tried to outbid Laphroaig for the ground it stood on. Laphroaig kept its distillery both times.

In 1923 and 1924 Hunter added two more stills, which made four. From about 1927 Laphroaig sold its whisky itself, with no agent.

Hunter died in 1954. His secretary, Bessie Williamson, was also a director of the company. She took charge and ran it until it was sold in 1967.

## [SLIDE 13] Glass 4: Laphroaig 10

_Pour glass 4 now, the Laphroaig._

This is Laphroaig 10. It is the whisky Mackie sold for years and then tried to make himself.

Laphroaig still malts about a fifth of its own barley at the distillery. The 10 is aged in barrels that were used for bourbon. It is 43 percent, the same as the Lagavulin.

Taste the two side by side. Both are heavily peated, and they are made two miles apart.

_Ask: "What is the biggest difference between glass 3 and glass 4?"_

## [SLIDE 14] The two distilleries today

Lagavulin has four stills. It stopped malting its own barley in 1974. Diageo owns it.

Laphroaig has seven stills and still malts some of its own barley. Suntory has owned it since 2014.

They still use different water. Lagavulin's comes from the lochs on the hill. Laphroaig says its water comes from the Kilbride stream.

## [SLIDE 15] Sláinte

Thanks for coming, and thanks to everyone who helped pour.

Raise a glass to Lagavulin and to Laphroaig. Sláinte.

_Leave the bottles out for anyone who wants to compare glasses 3 and 4 again._
