## Part One: before the night

### The one-sentence version

For about sixty years the firm that ran Lagavulin also sold all of Laphroaig's whisky; when Laphroaig ended the arrangement in 1907, Peter Mackie built a small distillery inside Lagavulin to make Laphroaig-style whisky, and it never did.

### What the night is

Four pours and fifteen slides, about 45 minutes. It is history with the glasses alongside: one bottle from Mackie's Speyside distillery, his blend, his Islay distillery, and the neighbour he lost. Be fair to both sides. Mackie built a great business and loved Lagavulin; Laphroaig's owners wanted to sell their own whisky. The feud is a good story and the record of it is thin, so say where each part comes from.

### How many bottles

Four. Two are heavily peated Islay malts, and past four the smoke flattens everything after it.

### Cast the roles, then find the bottles

Every bottle on the table should be doing a job. Decide the jobs, then go shopping.

- **Mackie's distillery (glass 3).** Tonight: Lagavulin 16.
- **The neighbour (glass 4).** The whisky Mackie sold for years and then tried to copy. Tonight: Laphroaig 10.

## Part Two: the script

_SET UP: four glasses each, marked 1 to 4, and water on the table. Pour glass 1 during slide 2._

## [SLIDE 1] Lagavulin vs Laphroaig

Good evening, and thanks for coming.

Tonight is about two distilleries on the south coast of Islay, Lagavulin and Laphroaig, about two miles apart on the same road. For around sixty years the firm that ran Lagavulin also sold every drop Laphroaig made. Then, in 1907, Laphroaig's owners ended the arrangement, and the owner of Lagavulin built a small distillery of his own to make Laphroaig-style whisky.

We'll drink four whiskies tied to the man at the centre of it.

## [SLIDE 2] Peter Mackie, 1855 to 1924

_Pour glass 1 now, the Craigellachie. Keep talking while you pour._

This is Peter Mackie, as the magazine Vanity Fair drew him in April 1908. The caption under the picture reads "Restless Peter", which was what people called him.

He was born in 1855 near Stirling, the son of a farmer and grain merchant. At 23 he went to work for his uncle, James Logan Mackie, who had a whisky business in Glasgow in partnership with the Graham family, the people who ran Lagavulin. Peter was sent straight out to Islay to learn distilling there. When his uncle died in 1889, Peter took over.

He became one of the big blenders of his day. The writer and diplomat Sir Robert Bruce Lockhart described him as one third genius, one third megalomaniac and one third eccentric.

## [SLIDE 3] Glass 1: Craigellachie 13

In 1890 and 1891 a company was formed to build a new distillery at Craigellachie on Speyside, at a railway junction where four lines met. Mackie was one of the investors, with a local distiller, Alexander Edward, and some wine merchants. Mackie's firm bought it outright in 1916, and most of what it made went into his blends.

It has no peat in it, which makes it a good place to start before the Islay whiskies. The distillery still condenses its spirit in worm tubs, coils of copper pipe in tanks of cold water, and it makes a heavy, meaty spirit.

_Ask: "What do you get from it before any smoke comes along?"_

## [SLIDE 4] Two distilleries two miles apart

Now to Islay. The Victorian writer Alfred Barnard toured every distillery in the United Kingdom in the mid-1880s, and his book came out in 1887. He visited Lagavulin and Laphroaig on consecutive days.

Lagavulin was the bigger of the two: about 75,000 gallons a year, two stills, water from two lochs up on the hill. Laphroaig made about 23,000 gallons from two old stills, with water from a burn that runs through the property. Both dried their malt over nothing but peat. Lagavulin was being sold as a single whisky as well as for blending, which Barnard thought unusual. Laphroaig was mostly sought after by blenders.

Barnard noted one more thing about Laphroaig: all of its whisky, for the last sixty years, had been sold by Mackie & Co of Glasgow.

## [SLIDE 5] For sixty years one firm sold both

How did that come about?

Laphroaig was started by the Johnston brothers; the company dates it to 1815. Legal distilling at Lagavulin starts in 1816, and by the late 1830s Lagavulin was in the hands of the Graham family from Glasgow.

In 1847 Donald Johnston, who owned Laphroaig, fell into a vat of hot liquid at the distillery and died two days later. His son was eleven. So Laphroaig was leased to Graham of Lagavulin, who ran it until the son came of age in 1857. After that the Lagavulin firm stayed on as Laphroaig's agents, selling its whisky, right up to 1907.

So for fifty years the two distilleries were tied together, and the firm that became Mackie & Co sold both whiskies, mainly to blenders.

## [SLIDE 6] White Horse, registered in 1890

Mackie needed those smoky Islay malts for his own blend. He registered the White Horse trademark in 1890. The name came from an old coaching inn on the Canongate in Edinburgh, next to property the Mackie family had owned.

White Horse was sold abroad first and launched in Britain in 1901. Mackie spent heavily on advertising it, and it became one of the big brands. At its heart was Lagavulin, along with Craigellachie.

## [SLIDE 7] Glass 2: White Horse

_Pour glass 2, the White Horse._

This is the blend. It is still made, now by Diageo, and the people who sell it still say Lagavulin is a key part of it. Diageo doesn't publish the recipe, so let's see what we can find in the glass.

Tonight's order goes by smoke. The Craigellachie was 46 percent and this is 40.

_Ask: "Can you find the Islay in it? Where does it show up?"_

## [SLIDE 8] 1907: Laphroaig ends Mackie's agency

In 1907 Alexander Johnston, who had been running Laphroaig, died. After a court case of its own the distillery passed to his two sisters and his nephew, and they felt they were not getting a fair deal from Mackie. They went to court and ended his agency. That is the date in Laphroaig's own history and in most accounts; one source puts the change in 1908.

What happened next is Laphroaig's account, so I'll tell it as theirs. Mackie is said to have stopped the water reaching Laphroaig. The older tellings say he had his men pull stones out of the lade, the channel that carried the stream to the distillery. Laphroaig's website today says he blocked the stream with rocks. Either way, the water was Laphroaig's cooling water as well as its brewing water, and a court made Mackie put it right.

> **SAY THE CAVEAT OUT LOUD**
> Every version of the water story comes from Laphroaig's side: its website and a history its owner published in 1995. The court papers would settle it, and nobody working on this deck has read them.

## [SLIDE 9] Glass 3: Lagavulin 16

_Pour glass 3, the Lagavulin._

This is Mackie's distillery. He was devoted to it and liked to say its water, coming down from the lochs over peat and moss, was what gave it its flavour. Barnard was told the same in the 1880s.

Lagavulin has belonged to the Distillers Company and its successors since 1927, and today it is Diageo's. The 16-year-old has been the main bottling since the late 1980s, when Diageo's predecessor chose it as one of its six Classic Malts.

_Ask: "Remember this one. In a few minutes you'll have Laphroaig next to it. What would you say is the difference?"_

## [SLIDE 10] 1908: Malt Mill, inside Lagavulin

In 1908 Mackie built a second, small distillery inside the Lagavulin site and called it Malt Mill. It had its own floor maltings and peat kiln and two pear-shaped stills. The stills, the story goes, were made the same as Laphroaig's, and Mackie hired Laphroaig's brewer to run it. The aim was to make Laphroaig at Lagavulin.

Malt Mill never made whisky like Laphroaig. Every account agrees on that, and one adds that it wasn't like Lagavulin either. It went into Mackie's blends, White Horse among them. Mackie himself died in 1924, and Malt Mill outlived him by more than thirty years.

It stopped around 1960; some sources say 1962. Its stills were taken into Lagavulin's own still house. No bottle of Malt Mill has ever been authenticated, which is why it turns up as the lost cask in the 2012 film _The Angel's Share_.

## [SLIDE 11] 1887: Barnard on two distilleries a short distance apart

Barnard wrote about these two distilleries twenty years before Malt Mill was built.

At the end of his Laphroaig entry he says that even the most experienced distillers can't change a whisky's character, because it depends on things like locality, water and position. And his example was these two distilleries: although they stand a short distance from each other, each makes a whisky of a distinct type. He adds that Laphroaig's owners would not change anything there in case the character was lost.

## [SLIDE 12] Laphroaig sells its own whisky

Back to Laphroaig. In 1908 the family sent Ian Hunter, the nephew, out to Islay to look after their interest. He was an engineer, and money was tight because of the court cases.

According to Laphroaig's own history, Mackie tried twice more to get hold of the place. When Laphroaig's lease came up he offered more to rent it, and in 1921, when the estate sold its land, he tried to outbid Laphroaig for it again. Both times Laphroaig kept its distillery.

Hunter doubled the distillery in 1923 and 1924 with a second pair of stills built as copies of the first, and from about 1927 he sold Laphroaig's whisky himself, with no agent. When he died in 1954, his secretary and fellow director, Bessie Williamson, ran the company until it was sold in 1967 and stayed on as chairman until 1972.

## [SLIDE 13] Glass 4: Laphroaig 10

_Pour glass 4, the Laphroaig._

This is the whisky Mackie sold for years and then tried to make himself. Laphroaig still malts part of its own barley on a floor at the distillery, about a fifth of what it uses, and kilns it over Islay peat. The 10-year-old is matured in ex-bourbon barrels.

Taste it next to the Lagavulin. Both are at 43 percent, both are heavily peated, and they are made two miles apart.

_Ask: "If you'd been Mackie's brewer in 1908, what would you have tried to copy?"_

## [SLIDE 14] The two distilleries today

Lagavulin today has four stills, gave up its floor maltings in 1974, and is owned by Diageo. Laphroaig has seven stills, still malts part of its own barley, and has been owned by Suntory since Suntory bought Beam in 2014.

Each still uses its own water. Lagavulin's comes down from the lochs on the hill, and Laphroaig's, as Laphroaig tells it, from the Kilbride stream.

## [SLIDE 15] Sláinte

Thanks for coming, and thanks to everyone who helped pour.

A toast to Barnard, who saw the difference between these two in 1887, to Bessie Williamson and Ian Hunter, who kept Laphroaig going, and to Restless Peter, who loved Lagavulin enough to try to build a second one.

Sláinte.
