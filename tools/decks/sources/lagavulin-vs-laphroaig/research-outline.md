# Lagavulin vs Laphroaig

Research outline: Peter Mackie, Laphroaig and Malt Mill

_This is the other half of the speaker notes. It does not tell you what to say; it tells you where to look, what is settled, what is still argued over, and which repeated stories rest on one telling. Facts were checked in November 2026. Prices, stock, owners and bottle strengths move quickly and should be checked again before the night._

> **THE SPINE OF THE STORY**
> 1815 to 1816: Laphroaig and Lagavulin are licensed two miles apart on Islay's south coast. → 1847: Laphroaig's owner dies and the Lagavulin firm runs Laphroaig until his son comes of age. → 1857 to 1907: the Lagavulin firm, later Mackie & Co, sells Laphroaig's whisky as its agent. → 1878: Peter Mackie arrives at Lagavulin; by 1890 he is registering White Horse and investing in Craigellachie. → 1907: Laphroaig's new owners go to court to end the agency; the water to Laphroaig is cut and a court has it restored. → 1908: Mackie builds Malt Mill inside Lagavulin to make a Laphroaig of his own, with copied stills and Laphroaig's brewer. → about 1960 to 1962: Malt Mill stops without ever having made Laphroaig.

## How to use this

Each module is one chapter of the night. "What's established" lists facts found in the sources named under it, with the year first. "Go to the source" says where to read more and how far each source was read for this outline. "Traps" are the claims that trip people up.

The standard of proof: a claim with two independent sources is stated as fact. A claim with only one source, or only the distillery's own account, is said aloud as "Laphroaig's own history says" or "the story goes". Most of the feud rests on two company histories (Laphroaig's website and a 1995 history issued by Laphroaig's then owner, Allied Distillers) and on writers who repeat them. The court papers have not been read by anyone working on this deck.

## Module 1. Two distilleries two miles apart

_How close were they, and how did one firm end up running both?_

### What's established

- **1815.** Laphroaig's own history dates the distillery to 1815, founded by the brothers Donald and Alexander Johnston on land leased from the laird. The 1995 Allied Distillers history says only that they started farming at Laphroaig "some time between 1810 and 1816", and that Donald Johnston is entered with the Excise as distiller in 1826. Barnard, in 1887, was told 1820.
- **1816.** Legal distilling starts at Lagavulin under John Johnston (scotchwhisky.com; Wikipedia, citing Diageo). Barnard was told the business went back to 1742, when "ten small and separate smuggling bothys" worked on the site.
- **1836.** Donald Johnston buys out his brother and becomes sole owner of Laphroaig (Allied history; Laphroaig's own history gives £350 for the share).
- **1835 to 1837.** Lagavulin passes to the Graham family of Glasgow. Barnard says the present firm took possession in 1835; scotchwhisky.com gives 1836 for the purchase by Alexander Graham; Tom Bruce-Gardyne says the Grahams leased it from the Kildalton estate from 1837.
- **1847.** Donald Johnston dies at Laphroaig, two days after falling into a vat of "burnt ale" (Allied history; scotchwhisky.com says boiling pot ale). His son Dugald is eleven. Laphroaig is leased to Graham of Lagavulin for nine years (Allied history); scotchwhisky.com names Walter Graham, manager at Lagavulin, as the man who stepped in.
- **1857.** Dugald Johnston takes over Laphroaig, and "the Lagavulin people continued as agents until 1907" (Allied history).
- **Distance.** scotchwhisky.com puts the two distilleries two miles apart. Barnard gives Laphroaig as one mile from Port Ellen and Lagavulin as three, along the same road.

### Go to the source

- › _Alfred Barnard, The Whisky Distilleries of the United Kingdom (1887), entries for Lagavulin and Laphroaig. Public domain. Read in full in the Whiskipedia transcription: whiskipedia.com/barnard/lagavulin/ and whiskipedia.com/barnard/laphroaig/._
- › _"Laphroaig: History", Allied Distillers, 1995, scanned on the University of Edinburgh whisky pages: dcs.ed.ac.uk/home/jhb/whisky/smws/laphroa2.html. Read in full. This is the distillery owner's own history._
- › _Laphroaig, "Our Heritage": laphroaig.com/heritage. Read in full. The distillery's own telling._
- › _scotchwhisky.com Whiskypedia, Lagavulin and Laphroaig entries. Read in full._

### Threads worth pulling

- Lagavulin's founder and Laphroaig's founders share the Johnston name. None of the sources read says whether they were related.
- A blog account (Noel on Whisky, 2024) describes an earlier water dispute in the 1830s and 1840s between Laphroaig and the neighbouring Ardenistiel distillery. No primary source was found for it in this research; Neil Wilson's _Scotch and Water_ (1985) may have it.

> **TRAPS**
> Laphroaig's founding year is 1815 on the label and 1820 in Barnard. The company date is the one on the bottle; say that the record is thin.
> "Lagavulin vs Laphroaig" is usually told as a rivalry from the start. For sixty years before 1907 the Lagavulin firm ran, leased or sold Laphroaig. The quarrel came at the end of a long partnership.

## Module 2. Barnard's visit, 1886 to 1887

_What did the two distilleries look like before the feud, in the words of someone who walked through both?_

### What's established

- **Lagavulin, about 1886.** Resident partner Mr J. C. Graham. Water from two lochs on the hill of Solan, said to give the whisky its flavour. Peat only in the kiln. Two pot stills. Annual output 75,000 gallons. Sold as a single whisky as well as for blending, which Barnard calls unusual.
- **Laphroaig, about 1886.** Mr Johnston in charge. Water from a burn running through the property. Peat only in the kiln. Two old pot stills. Annual output 23,000 gallons, "all of which has for the last sixty years been supplied and sold by Messrs. Mackie & Co., of Glasgow". The whisky is "largely sought after for blending purposes".
- **Barnard's comparison.** He closes the Laphroaig entry with the two distilleries as his example of how locality, water and position fix a whisky's character: although they stand a short distance apart, "each produce Whisky of a distinct and varied type". He adds that the proprietors would not change Laphroaig's arrangements in case the character were lost.

### Go to the source

- › _Barnard (1887), as above. The Laphroaig entry is short; read it in one sitting._

> **TRAPS**
> "Sixty years" in Barnard means the firm and its predecessors at Lagavulin, not Mackie & Co by that name, which dates from the 1880s and 1890s.
> Barnard was told the dates he prints; he did not check them against records.

## Module 3. Peter Mackie, White Horse and Craigellachie

_Who was the man who held both whiskies, and what did he want them for?_

### What's established

- **1855.** Peter Mackie born at Corsepatrick, St Ninians, near Stirling (Bruce-Gardyne); SMWS names his father as Alexander Mackie, a farmer and grain merchant.
- **1878.** Aged 23, he joins his uncle James Logan Mackie's Glasgow whisky firm, which was in partnership with John Graham of Lagavulin, and is sent to Lagavulin to learn distilling (Bruce-Gardyne; SMWS; scotchwhisky.com).
- **1889.** James Logan Mackie dies and Peter takes control of Lagavulin (scotchwhisky.com timeline). Wikipedia gives 1889 for Peter becoming sole partner.
- **1890.** The White Horse trademark is registered (Bruce-Gardyne). The name came from the White Horse inn in Edinburgh's Canongate, next to property the Mackie family had owned (Bruce-Gardyne; SMWS). White Horse sold abroad first (24,000 cases a year overseas) and was launched in Britain in 1901; between 1902 and 1914 Mackie spent £90,000 advertising it in the UK, and home sales rose from 700 to 70,000 cases a year (Bruce-Gardyne).
- **1890 to 1891.** Craigellachie distillery on Speyside is built, designed by Charles Doig, at a railway junction where four lines met, by the Craigellachie Distillery Co Ltd, whose investors included Mackie, the local distiller Alexander Edward and several wine merchants (SMWS). Mackie & Co (Distillers) Ltd bought it outright for £9,000 in 1916 (SMWS).
- **White Horse and Lagavulin.** Bruce-Gardyne calls Lagavulin "the heart and soul" of White Horse along with Craigellachie. SMWS says White Horse "had Lagavulin at its heart". Current retailer listings (Liquor Barn, Spec's, Wine and Cheese Place) still describe Lagavulin as a key component; Diageo does not publish the recipe.
- **"Restless Peter".** Vanity Fair ran a Spy caricature of him on 8 April 1908 captioned "Restless Peter" (Commons file, image seen). Bruce-Gardyne and SMWS quote the writer and diplomat Sir Robert Bruce Lockhart's description of Mackie as "one third genius, one third megalomaniac and one third eccentric".
- **Lagavulin's water.** Mackie "always maintained" that Lagavulin's flavour came from the water of the Solan (Solum) lochs running over peat and moss (Bruce-Gardyne); Barnard was told the same.
- **1924.** Mackie dies in September; the firm becomes White Horse Distillers, and in 1927 it joins the Distillers Company (Bruce-Gardyne; SMWS; scotchwhisky.com).

### Go to the source

- › _Tom Bruce-Gardyne, "Peter the great", Whisky Magazine issue 9, 16 April 2000: whiskymag.com/articles/peter-the-great/. Read in full. The best single account of Mackie._
- › _SMWS Unfiltered, "Unsung hero: Craigellachie distillery": smws.com/unfiltered/unsung-hero-craigellachie-distillery. Read in full._
- › _Difford's Guide, Craigellachie history. Read in part (the bottlings section)._

### Threads worth pulling

- SMWS says Mackie commissioned Barnard to write a pamphlet, _How to Blend Whisky_, in the early 1900s, and quotes it on Craigellachie's "pineapple flavour". The pamphlet itself was not found.

> **TRAPS**
> "Mackie built Craigellachie" is close but not exact: it was a company with several investors, and Mackie's firm owned it outright only from 1916.
> Some retailers date White Horse to 1861, from the inn or from James Logan Mackie. The trademark date is 1890.

## Module 4. 1907: the agency ends and the water stops

_What happened when Laphroaig's owners dropped Mackie as their agent?_

### What's established

- **1907.** Alexander Johnston, who ran Laphroaig, dies. After a court case the distillery passes to his two sisters and his nephew (Allied history).
- **1907.** The new owners, feeling they were not getting a fair deal, take Mackie to court and end his agency (Allied history; Bruce-Gardyne). scotchwhisky.com instead puts the change of agent in 1908, credited to Ian Hunter.
- **The water.** Every account says Mackie stopped the water reaching Laphroaig and was made by a court to restore it. The details differ. The Allied history says he "gave instructions to his men to pull out the stones at the lade". Bruce-Gardyne says he "ordered stones to be ripped up from the lade". Laphroaig's website now says that in 1907 he tried "blocking it up with rocks", that this cut off its only source of cooling water, and that "the courts quickly waded in".
- **1908.** Ian Hunter, son of one of the sisters and trained as an engineer, is sent to Islay to look after the family's interest (Allied history).

### Go to the source

- › _Allied Distillers history (1995), as above. Read in full._
- › _Laphroaig, "Our Heritage", section "Water wars (1887 to 1908)". Read in full._
- › _Bruce-Gardyne (2000), as above._

### Threads worth pulling

- The court papers: the agency case and the water case should be in the Court of Session records (National Records of Scotland) or in the Session Cases or Scottish Law Reporter for 1907 to 1909. A search of the Internet Archive's full texts did not find them. They would settle the year, the order of the cases and what Mackie did to the lade.

> **SAY WHOSE ACCOUNT IT IS**
> Every telling of the water story comes from Laphroaig's side: the company's own website, the 1995 history issued by its then owner, and writers who repeat them. No account from Mackie's side was found. Tell it as Laphroaig's story.
> The two tellings disagree on the method: stones pulled out of the lade in the older accounts, rocks blocking the stream on the website.

## Module 5. Malt Mill, 1908 to about 1960

_What did Mackie build, and why did it fail?_

### What's established

- **1908.** Mackie builds a small distillery inside Lagavulin, called Malt Mill, to make whisky like Laphroaig (scotchwhisky.com; Bruce-Gardyne; Allied history; Laphroaig's website).
- **The copy.** He used stills the same as Laphroaig's and hired Laphroaig's brewer (Allied history; Bruce-Gardyne; Laphroaig's website, which says "head brewer" and "an exact copy of our still house").
- **The plant.** A small, traditional distillery with its own floor maltings and peat kiln and two pear-shaped pot stills (Bruce-Gardyne).
- **The result.** It did not make anything like Laphroaig (Allied history; Bruce-Gardyne; scotchwhisky.com, which adds that it did not make Lagavulin either). Its spirit went into Mackie's blends, White Horse among them (scotchwhisky.com; Whiskyfun).
- **The end.** Whiskyfun (2018) says it was dismantled in 1960, and that a 1962 "Malt Mill last filling" sample at Lagavulin was in fact distilled at Lagavulin, into which Malt Mill's equipment and stills had been absorbed. scotchwhisky.com gives 1962. Bruce-Gardyne says it was demolished in the 1960s and that its stills briefly joined Lagavulin's.
- **No bottle.** No authenticated bottle or cask of Malt Mill is known (Whiskyfun). A fictional cask of it is the centre of Ken Loach's film _The Angel's Share_ (2012) (scotchwhisky.com; Whiskyfun).

### Go to the source

- › _Whiskyfun, "Malt Mill for Christmas Eve", 24 December 2018: whiskyfun.com/2018/Malt-Mill-for-Christmas-Eve.html. Read in part (the history section)._
- › _scotchwhisky.com Whiskypedia, Lagavulin and Mackie's entries. Read in full._
- › _Andrew Jefford, Peat Smoke and Spirit (2005). Not read (lending-only). Whiskyfun credits it with the fullest account of Malt Mill._

> **TRAPS**
> Do not give one closing year as settled. Say "around 1960; some sources say 1962".
> "Mackie's copy failed because of the water" is a common explanation and a reasonable one, but no source read here shows anyone tested it. Barnard's 1887 line about locality is the best evidence that the trade expected it.

## Module 6. Laphroaig after Mackie

_How did Laphroaig manage once it sold its own whisky?_

### What's established

- **1908 onward.** Money was short because of the court cases, and Mackie & Co put in a higher offer to rent Laphroaig when its lease came up (Allied history).
- **1921.** The Ramsay estate sells its land, offering the distilleries first refusal; Laphroaig buys its land, and Mackie again tries and fails to outbid it (Allied history).
- **1923 to 1924.** Laphroaig doubles capacity with a new wash still and spirit still duplicating the old pair, and finishes its maltings (Allied history gives 1923; scotchwhisky.com gives 1924 for two stills to four).
- **About 1927.** Hunter ends the agency with Robertson & Baxter and sells direct from the distillery (Allied history).
- **1954.** Hunter dies and Bessie Williamson, his secretary and a director, becomes managing director (Allied history; Laphroaig's website; scotchwhisky.com). Laphroaig passes to Long John / Seager Evans in 1967 (Allied history; scotchwhisky.com; Laphroaig's website).
- **Today.** Laphroaig still malts about 20% of its barley on its own floor; it has seven stills; it is owned by Suntory, which bought Beam in 2014 (scotchwhisky.com). Lagavulin's floor maltings closed in 1974; it has four stills, takes its water from Lochan Sholum, and is owned by Diageo (scotchwhisky.com; Wikipedia). Lagavulin 16 was chosen in 1988 as one of the six Classic Malts (scotchwhisky.com) and is the standard bottling (Wikipedia). Laphroaig's website names its water as the Kilbride stream; whether this is the burn Barnard describes is not stated anywhere read.

### Go to the source

- › _Allied Distillers history (1995), as above._
- › _scotchwhisky.com Whiskypedia, Laphroaig entry, history, timeline and facts. Read in full._

> **TRAPS**
> The 1921 bid and the 1908 rental bid come from one source, the 1995 company history. Say so if you use them.

## Claims that don't hold up

- "Mackie owned Laphroaig." He was its agent and, by Barnard's account, sold all of its whisky. The Graham family of Lagavulin leased it from 1847 to 1857. Mackie never owned it.
- "Mackie blocked the stream with rocks." That is Laphroaig's current telling. The older accounts say he had stones pulled out of the lade, the channel that carried the water.
- "Malt Mill closed in 1962." Possibly; Whiskyfun argues the last true Malt Mill was made in 1960.
- "White Horse dates from 1861." The trademark was registered in 1890.

## Not confirmed

- The year the agency ended: 1907 (Allied history; Bruce-Gardyne) or 1908 (scotchwhisky.com). The script says 1907 and mentions that some give 1908.
- What Mackie did to the water, and when. All accounts trace to Laphroaig's side. Said aloud as Laphroaig's account; the slide says "Laphroaig's account".
- The court cases: none of the papers was read. The script says "a court" and does not name the court or give a date for the water case.
- That Malt Mill's stills were exact copies and that the man hired was Laphroaig's "head brewer". Both are Laphroaig-side accounts repeated by Bruce-Gardyne. Said as "the story goes" for the brewer.
- Malt Mill's closing year: 1960 or 1962. Both given.
- Mackie's 1908 bid to rent Laphroaig and his 1921 bid to buy its land: one source (Allied history). Kept in the script with "Laphroaig's history says".
- Laphroaig's founding year: 1815 (company), 1820 (Barnard), 1826 (first Excise entry, Allied history). Script gives 1815 as the company date.
- White Horse's current recipe and how much Lagavulin is in it. Not published; script says "built on Lagavulin" as history and lets the room taste.
- The earlier Ardenistiel water dispute: blog only. Not used.
- Kroger's $49.99 for Craigellachie 13 and Target's $59.99 for Laphroaig 10 were seen only in search results and are not used.
