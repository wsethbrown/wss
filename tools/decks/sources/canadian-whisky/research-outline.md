# Canadian Whisky

Research outline: Canadian whisky and the American market

Canadian whisky and the American market. A guide for anyone who wants to research the subject and build a talk of their own.

---

**Sources.** The facts in this deck were checked in November 2026 against the sources listed under each module. What could not be confirmed is listed under "Not confirmed", with what would settle it.

## Module 1. The Ontario millers

**The question.** Who started commercial distilling in Canada, and why did millers do it?

### Nailed down

- Gooderham & Worts: William Gooderham and James Worts built a windmill on Toronto Bay in 1832; Worts died in 1834; Gooderham added a distillery in 1837 to use surplus and second-grade grain.
- J.P. Wiser (1825–1911) came to run a distillery at Prescott in 1857 and was sole owner by 1862. Accounts differ on whether he arrived as a manager or a partner.
- Hiram Walker bought land opposite Detroit in 1856 and was selling flour and whisky by 1858; the DCB dates the finished mill and distillery to about 1859. 1858 is the company's date.
- Henry Corby bought a mill at Hayden's Corners in 1855 and was distilling by 1859; the village was renamed Corbyville in 1882.
- Joseph E. Seagram was hired at the Granite Mills and Waterloo Distillery (founded 1857 by William Hespeler and George Randall) in 1864, bought out his partners in stages (sources disagree on the years), and was sole owner from 1883.

- Dictionary of Canadian Biography: James Gooderham Worts: https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/worts_james_gooderham_11E.html
- Dictionary of Canadian Biography: Henry Corby: https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/corby_henry_11E.html
- Dictionary of Canadian Biography: Hiram Walker: https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/walker_hiram_12E.html
- Dictionary of Canadian Biography: Joseph Emm Seagram: https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/seagram_joseph_emm_14E.html

### Primary documents

- Dictionary of Canadian Biography entries for Gooderham, Worts, Walker, Wiser, Corby and Seagram (free online).
- Toronto and county directories of the period; Library and Archives Canada.

### Traps

- "The largest distillery in the world" (Gooderham & Worts, 1870s) is widely repeated and unmeasured. The talk says "one of the largest".
- Founding dates on modern labels are company dates and sometimes mark a purchase or a mill rather than the first distillation.

## Module 2. Hiram Walker and the Civil War

**The question.** How did Canadian whisky first reach American buyers in volume?

### Nailed down

- Hiram Walker (1816–1899), born East Douglas, Massachusetts; Detroit from 1838. Walkerville became a village in 1869, was incorporated in 1890 and joined Windsor in 1935. Canadian Club was introduced in 1884. (DCB; The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Hiram Walker": https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/hiram-walker; Library and Archives Canada heirloom series: https://epe.lac-bac.gc.ca/100/205/301/ic/cdc/heirloom_series/volume4/359.html)
- US federal excise on distilled spirits, per proof gallon: 20¢ from 1 August 1862; 60¢ from 7 March 1864; $1.50 from 1 July 1864; $2.00 from 1 January 1865 to 20 July 1868, then 50¢.

### Sources

- TTB, Historical Tax Rates: https://www.ttb.gov/tax-audit/historical-tax-rates
- Congressional Research Service, Federal Excise Taxes on Alcoholic Beverages: A Summary of Present Law and a Brief History (RL30238): https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL30238.html

### Primary documents

- U.S. Statutes at Large, Internal Revenue Acts of 1862 and 1864 (free, Library of Congress).
- Annual Reports of the Commissioner of Internal Revenue, for whiskey production and imports.
- US customs tariffs of the 1860s, for the duty on imported spirits.

### Traps

- "The $2 tax was several times the cost of the whiskey" is often said; no production-cost figure was found. The talk says so and the slide no longer claims it.
- The story that American distillers forced Walker to add "Canadian" to "Club" by law is the company's account.
- The wartime advantage to Canadian imports depended on the import duty as well as the excise. Do not claim a price advantage without both figures.

## Module 3. The ageing law of 1890

**The question.** When did Canada require whisky to be aged, and who did it suit?

### Nailed down

- An ageing requirement was introduced in 1887 and phased in, with two years in wood required from 1890 (Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails; de Kergommeaux). Whether 1887 first set a one-year minimum is disputed (Mental Floss says so; the others describe a phase-in).
- The current rule is at least three years in small wood (casks of 700 litres or less), Food and Drug Regulations B.02.023, with exceptions for bourbon and Tennessee whisky.
- At least one writer (Distiller magazine) says the aim was to put small, tax-evading distillers out of business.

### Sources

- Davin de Kergommeaux, "Canadian Whisky in Three Periods", Distiller magazine: https://distilling.com/distillermagazine/canadian-whisky-in-three-periods/
- _The Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails_, "whisky, Canadian": https://www.spiritsanddistilling.com/dictionary/acref-9780199311132-e-1213
- Canadian Whisky (de Kergommeaux), "A Journalist's Primer on Canadian Whisky": https://canadianwhisky.org/blog/news-views/a-journalists-primer-on-canadian-whisky.html
- Food and Drug Regulations, s. B.02.023: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.,_c._870/section-B.02.023.html
- Canadian whisky (Wikipedia, for the 1890 date and the bonded programme; secondary): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_whisky

### Primary documents

- Statutes of Canada, 1887 and 1890 (Inland Revenue amendments). Canadiana.org has scans.
- Reports of the Department of Inland Revenue.

### Traps

- Sources differ on whether the 1887 law set a one-year minimum first. The talk says accounts differ.
- "The first country to require ageing" is common and unproven. The talk says Canada was well ahead of the US.
- The link between the law and consolidation of the industry is an interpretation, not a documented effect.

## Module 4. How Canadian whisky is made

**The question.** What does Canadian blending mean in practice?

### Nailed down

- No legal recipe exists for base and flavouring whiskies; the Canadian standard sets grain, place, ageing and strength, not the split.

### Primary documents

- Davin de Kergommeaux, _Canadian Whisky: The Portable Expert_ (2012; second edition 2017). The standard modern reference.
- Distillery technical descriptions from Hiram Walker, Crown Royal (Gimli) and Forty Creek.

### Traps

- Practice varies by distillery. Distillation strengths quoted in books are typical, not required.
- "Blended" on a Canadian label does not mean whisky cut with neutral spirit, as it can on an American blended whiskey.

## Module 5. Prohibition and the export trade

**The question.** How did Canadian whisky reach the United States between 1920 and 1933, legally on one side and illegally on the other?

### Nailed down

- National Prohibition in the US ran from January 1920 to December 1933.
- An amendment to the Export Act received royal assent on 30 May 1930. It added a section requiring officials to refuse release from bond and to refuse clearance to vessels where that would help bring liquor into a country that banned its import. It was general in its terms; its target was the United States. The same prohibition remains in the current Export Act.

### Sources

- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, vol. I, document 811.114 Canada/4310: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d539
- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, vol. I, document 811.114 Canada/4337: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d545
- Export Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. E-18: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-18/FullText.html

### Primary documents

- Report of the Royal Commission on Customs and Excise (final report tabled 1928).
- Canadian export clearance records; Windsor and Detroit newspapers (Chronicling America for Detroit, free).
- US Coast Guard and Prohibition Bureau annual reports.

### Traps

- "75 percent of smuggled liquor crossed the Detroit River" has no source. The talk says so.
- Stories of cars driven across the frozen river are common; find a dated newspaper report before telling one as fact.
- Canada's 1930 law did not name the US; say "countries that banned import".

## Module 6. The Bronfmans, Hatch and Repeal

**The question.** Who came out of Prohibition with the largest share of the North American whisky trade, and how?

### Nailed down

- Sam Bronfman's Distillers Corporation Limited opened a distillery at LaSalle, Quebec, in 1924, and bought Joseph E. Seagram & Sons of Waterloo from the Seagram heirs in 1928 (one source says 1927; 1928 is the usual date). The merged firm was Distillers Corporation-Seagrams Limited.
- In 1934 Sam Bronfman and his brothers were charged with evading customs duties; the case came to court in 1935 and was thrown out, with shipping records and account books unavailable.
- The Royal Commission on Customs and Excise reported in 1928.
- Harry Hatch bought control of Gooderham & Worts in 1923 and Hiram Walker & Sons in 1926, merging them in 1927 as Hiram Walker-Gooderham & Worts.
- Crown Royal was launched by Seagram in 1939 for the royal tour of George VI and Queen Elizabeth (17 May to 15 June 1939), the first visit to Canada by a reigning monarch. It was sold only in Canada until 1964.
- Diageo acquired Crown Royal when the Seagram drinks business was broken up (sale to Diageo and Pernod Ricard, 2000–2001).

### Sources

- The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Seagram Company Limited": https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/seagram-company-limited
- Hagley Library, "Seagram Ltd. and the Bronfman Family" (research guide; the Seagram papers are at Hagley): https://www.hagley.org/research/library-guide-seagram
- Britannica Money, "Seagram Company Ltd.": https://britannica.com/money/Seagram-Company-Ltd
- Maclean's, "The ties that bind the Liberals and the Bronfmans": https://www.macleans.ca/politics/ottawa/the-ties-that-bind-the-liberals-and-the-bronfmans/
- Encyclopedia.com (International Directory of Company Histories), "The Seagram Company, Ltd.": https://www.encyclopedia.com/books/politics-and-business-magazines/seagram-company-ltd
- Royal Commission on Customs and Excise, final report (Government of Canada Publications): https://publications.gc.ca/site/eng/9.820954/publication.html
- The Canadian Encyclopedia, "1939 Royal Tour": https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/1939-royal-tour
- Royal Collection Trust, King George VI and Queen Elizabeth in Canada: https://www.rct.uk/collection/stories/canada/king-george-vi-and-queen-elizabeth-at-st-john-new-brunswick-during-their-tour-of-canada
- The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Distillery Historic District / Gooderham & Worts National Historic Site" (1927 merger): https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/distillery-historic-district-gooderham-worts-national-historic-site

### Primary documents

- Michael R. Marrus, _Mr. Sam: The Life and Times of Samuel Bronfman_ (1991).
- Seagram Company records, Hagley Museum and Library, Wilmington, Delaware.

### Traps

- The Bronfmans' own account and the prosecutors' account differ. Present both.
- The 1934–35 case is often called an acquittal; the sources describe it being thrown out of court. Do not name the judge without a court record.
- The size of Canadian aged stocks at Repeal compared with American stocks is often asserted without figures. Find Internal Revenue warehouse figures for 1933.
- The "600 blends tried" Crown Royal story comes from brand sources.

## Module 7. "Rye", the 9.09% rule and the regulations

**The question.** What does Canadian law say Canadian whisky is?

### Nailed down

- From 2006 until 17 December 2024 the standard was Food and Drug Regulations s. B.02.020, titled "Canadian Whisky, Canadian Rye Whisky or Rye Whisky": a grain distillate, mashed, distilled and aged in Canada, aged in small wood for not less than three years, at least 40% alcohol by volume, and allowed to contain caramel and flavouring. No minimum rye content.
- SOR/2024-244 (in force 18 December 2024) repealed B.02.020 and moved the standard, unchanged in substance, into the Canadian Food Compositional Standards, Volume 2, section 2.1.3, incorporated by reference.
- Age claims count only time in small wood (a separate provision of the regulations), and small wood means casks of 700 litres or less.
- The 9.09% figure is not in the Canadian definition. Writers including Whisky Advocate and Whisky Magazine describe it as the most other spirit (aged at least two years) or wine a Canadian whisky may contain; several sources say that since 1 July 2009 it applies only to whisky exported to the US.
- Its origin is disputed: de Kergommeaux has suggested a tariff arrangement negotiated by Sam Bronfman; others cite US tax rules or the 1989 Canada–US Free Trade Agreement.

### Sources

- Food and Drug Regulations, s. B.02.020, version 2006-03-22 to 2024-12-17: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/Regulations/c.r.c.,_c._870/section-B.02.020-20060322.html
- Canada Gazette Part II, SOR/2024-244: https://gazette.gc.ca/rp-pr/p2/2024/2024-12-18/html/sor-dors244-eng.html
- CFIA, Canadian Food Compositional Standards: https://inspection.canada.ca/en/about-cfia/acts-and-regulations/list-acts-and-regulations/documents-incorporated-reference/canadian-food-compositional-standards-0
- CFIA, regulatory references (concordance table): https://inspection.canada.ca/en/food-labels/labelling/summary-changes-compositional-standards/regulatory-references
- Whisky Magazine, "'We should celebrate it': Canadian whisky's controversial 9.09 per cent rule": https://whiskymag.com/articles/we-should-celebrate-it-canadian-whiskys-controversial-909-per-cent-rule/
- Whisky Advocate, "Instant Expert: Canadian Whisky": https://whiskyadvocate.com/instant-expert-canadian-whisky
- WineAlign, "Whisky Rules" (2016, on the 2009 change): https://www.winealign.com/articles/2016/11/02/whisky-rules/
- Forbes (Joe Micallef), "How To Understand The World Of Canadian Whisky": https://www.forbes.com/sites/joemicallef/2024/04/25/how-to-understand-the-world-of-canadian-whisky/
- Whisky Magazine, "Following the Rules": https://www.whiskymag.com/articles/following-the-rules/

### Primary documents

- Canadian Food Compositional Standards, Volume 2, 2.1.3 (CFIA).
- US 27 CFR Part 5 for the American rye standard (51% rye) and the US standard for Canadian whisky.

### Traps

- The regulation section numbers changed in December 2024. Cite the Compositional Standards for current law.
- The origin of "rye" as a name for all Canadian whisky, and its link to German and Dutch settlers, is told often with little evidence. Present it as the usual account.
- Do not describe the 9.09% rule as part of the Canadian definition of whisky.

## Module 8. The reputation for lightness

**The question.** Where did "Canadian whisky is light and bland" come from, and is it fair?

### Nailed down

- Nothing in this module was independently confirmed in this pass; it is interpretation, kept in the talk as such.

### Primary documents

- Distilled Spirits Council and Spirits Canada volume statistics by category.
- Mid-century US magazine advertising for Canadian Club, Seagram's V.O. and Crown Royal.

### Traps

- Avoid claiming specific sales rankings by year without the statistics. (The talk no longer says Canadian Club and Crown Royal are among the best-selling whiskies in the US.)
- Light is a style choice that suited a market. Do not present it as a fault in the method.

## The four pours

- **Pour 1. Canadian Club 1858.** Maker: Hiram Walker & Sons, Windsor (brand owned by Suntory Global Spirits). Style: Blended Canadian whisky. ABV: 40%. Why it is in the flight: Walker's brand, the light style most Americans know.
- **Pour 2. Lot No. 40.** Maker: Corby, at the Hiram Walker distillery, Windsor (Pernod Ricard). Style: Canadian rye whisky, 100% rye, single copper pot still. ABV: 43%. Why it is in the flight: close to a flavouring whisky on its own.
- **Pour 3. Crown Royal Fine Deluxe.** Maker: Diageo, at Gimli, Manitoba. Style: Blended Canadian whisky. ABV: 40%. Why it is in the flight: Seagram's 1939 blend, the Bronfman story.
- **Pour 4. Forty Creek Copper Pot Reserve.** Maker: Forty Creek Distillery, Grimsby (Campari since 2014). Style: Canadian whisky, barley, corn and rye distilled and aged separately. ABV: 43%. Why it is in the flight: a newer distiller using the separate-grain method.

All four are sold in the US (US retailer and grocery listings, November 2026). Forty Creek renamed Copper Pot Reserve "Copper Bold" in 2022; US retailers mostly still list it under the old name. John Hall took over the Grimsby distillery in 1992. Canadian Club says it blends its distillates before ageing and has done so from the start. Prices vary too much by region to quote.

## Not confirmed

- "Canadian" added to "Club" because of a US origin-labelling rule: the company's account; Wikipedia repeats it. Kept in the talk as the company's version. A US statute or Treasury regulation of the 1880s–1890s would settle it.
- When the minimum age rose from two to three years (often given as 1974): not found. The slide says only "now three". Settle with the regulation's amendment history (Canada Gazette).
- Whether the 1887 law set a one-year minimum first: sources disagree; the talk says so. Statutes of Canada 1887 would settle it.
- Gooderham & Worts as "the largest distillery in the world" in the 1870s: unmeasured; the talk says "one of the largest". Inland Revenue production returns would settle it.
- The 9.09% origin and the exact rule for US-bound whisky: secondary sources only, and they disagree; the talk says so. TTB or US Customs rulings on imported Canadian whisky would settle it.
- The German and Dutch settler origin of "rye": told often with little evidence; the talk says so.
- "Since the 2000s more all-rye bottlings in the US": kept as interpretation. Industry statistics or de Kergommeaux would settle it.
- Crown Royal component whisky from Valleyfield, Quebec, as well as Gimli: one reviewer says so; the slide says Gimli, where Diageo distils and blends it.
