## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] Whisky in Hot Countries

Thanks for coming. Tonight is about three distilleries that make single malt in hot places: Amrut in Bangalore, Paul John in Goa, and Kavalan in the north-east of Taiwan. We'll go through them in the order they started selling malt, and taste each one when we get to it.

All three ran into the same problem, which is that whisky ages very differently in heat, and you'll notice as we go that none of their standard bottles carries an age statement. By the end I'll explain why, and the last pour is a twelve-year-old Scotch so we have something to measure them against.

## [SLIDE 2] Amrut was founded in Bangalore in 1948

The first story starts in Bangalore. Amrut was founded there in 1948, the year after independence, by J. N. Radhakrishna Rao Jagdale. (Some accounts give 1947, for an earlier family firm, Amrut Laboratories.) For its first fifty-odd years it did what nearly every Indian distiller did, which was to make the spirits Indians actually drink in large quantities.

India is the biggest whisky market in the world by volume, by the drinks-research firm IWSR's figures, and several of the best-selling whisky brands anywhere are Indian brands you will probably never see in a shop here. That volume is a very particular kind of whisky.

## [SLIDE 3] What India calls whisky

Most of what is sold in India as whisky starts with molasses, the thick syrup left over when sugar is made from cane. India grows a great deal of cane, so molasses is cheap.

The usual Indian whisky is that molasses spirit, with some malt whisky blended in for flavour, plus colouring. In the Indian trade this whole category goes by an odd name left over from the colonial period: Indian-made foreign liquor.

Under India's food-safety rules this is whisky: a blended whisky may be mostly neutral spirit of agricultural origin, which includes molasses, with as little as 2% malt or grain whisky. That is a legitimate local rule. It does run into trouble abroad. The European Union's spirits regulation, 2019/787, says whisky must be distilled from a mash of malted cereals, which molasses is not, must spend at least three years in wooden casks of no more than 700 litres, and must be bottled at 40% or more. So the bulk of Indian whisky cannot be sold in Europe under that name.

## [SLIDE 4] 2004: Amrut launches its single malt in Glasgow

Amrut had been distilling some malt whisky from Indian barley to blend into its ordinary brands. By then the company was run by Radhakrishna's son, Neelakanta Rao Jagdale, who had joined in 1972 and became chairman after his father's death in 1976. In the early 2000s the family decided to bottle some of the malt on its own as a single malt.

They launched it on 24 August 2004, and they launched it in Glasgow, at an Indian restaurant. (Accounts disagree on its name.)

The company's explanation is that it had nowhere to sell it in India. An Indian single malt would cost several times as much as the whisky Indians were used to, and Indians who had that much to spend bought Scotch. An Indian malt would be taken seriously at home only once people who knew whisky had taken it seriously first.

## [SLIDE 5] Selling an Indian malt in Britain

The launch is credited to the founder's grandson, Rakshit Jagdale. As an MBA student in Newcastle in the 1990s he had taken a sample of Amrut's malt spirit to the owner of the Pot Still, a Glasgow whisky bar. The story told about the company's early years is that its people took bottles round restaurants and whisky shops one at a time, a lot of them Indian restaurants to begin with, and asked people to taste it. I'd treat the details of that as company lore, but the slow start is real; for several years Amrut was a curiosity.

What changed things was a rating. In the 2010 edition of his *Whisky Bible*, Jim Murray gave Amrut Fusion 97 points and placed it third in the world, behind an American whiskey and a Scotch. Murray is one critic with strong opinions, but the score was reported widely and shops started asking for the bottle. It is the one we are about to taste.

## [SLIDE 6] Pour 1 of 4: Amrut Fusion

This is Amrut Fusion. The name comes from the barley: about three-quarters is unpeated Indian barley and a quarter peated barley from Scotland, by the usual accounts. It's bottled at 50%, so it's the strongest thing on the table.

Try it neat first, then with a few drops of water. You may find the smoke faint and the oak early; the next slide explains why.

There's no age on the label. Amrut doesn't publish one for Fusion. A figure of about four years is often repeated, and I haven't found a source for it.

## [SLIDE 7] What a year in the warehouse costs

When Amrut started holding malt for longer, they found out how much heat matters.

In a Scottish warehouse a cask loses on average about 2% a year to evaporation, the part distillers call the angels' share; that is the Scotch Whisky Association's own figure. Amrut's figures for Bangalore are higher and not quite consistent. An older interview with the family quotes about 12% a year, and Rakshit Jagdale has more recently said 9 to 10%. Bangalore is on a plateau and has a milder climate than most of India, and Rakshit Jagdale has said that on the coast and in the north the loss can reach 13 to 15%.

The heat also speeds up the exchange between spirit and wood. Amrut's master blender, Surinder Kumar, has been reported as saying a year in Bangalore does what three years would do in Scotland. That is the company's own estimate, and there's no agreed way of measuring it.

## [SLIDE 8] What is left in the cask

This is the arithmetic, using 2% a year for Scotland and 10% for a hot warehouse. Ten per cent sits inside the distilleries' own figures: Amrut's 9 to 12%, and Paul John's 8 to 10%. Kavalan's American importer gives a higher figure for its hottest warehouse floors, at least 15%. The chart assumes the same loss every year; real losses vary with the cask and where it sits.

After four years the Scottish cask still holds about 92% of what went in. The hot cask holds about two-thirds.

After twelve years the Scottish cask holds about 78%. The hot cask is down to just over a quarter, and what's left has spent twelve years in wood that works much harder.

So an Indian or Taiwanese distiller who wanted a twelve-year-old would have to fill nearly three casks to get what one cask gives in Scotland, store them for twelve years, and hope the oak hadn't taken over. The cost is the main reason almost nobody does it.

## [SLIDE 9] Paul John: malt from Goa, first sold in 2012

The second distillery belongs to John Distilleries, the company of Paul P. John, which is based in Bangalore and started, like Amrut, by making whisky for the Indian mass market. Its best-known brand is Original Choice. Sources disagree on when the company was founded: 1992 in some accounts, 1996 on its own website. It began making malt in Goa in 2008, and its first single malt, a single-cask bottling, was launched in London on 4 October 2012, before it was sold in India.

The malt distillery is at Cuncolim in south Goa, a few miles inland from the coast, with copper pot stills. Goa is at sea level, hot all year and very humid, which is a different set of conditions from Bangalore's dry heat. The company puts its loss to evaporation at 8 to 10% a cask a year.

Paul John followed Amrut's route abroad.

## [SLIDE 10] Pour 2 of 4: Paul John Brilliance

This is Paul John Brilliance, their unpeated standard bottling, at 46%. It's made from Indian six-row barley, the kind most Indian farmers grow, and matured in casks that held bourbon.

I don't want to make claims about what six-row barley tastes of, because it's hard to separate the barley from the climate and the casks. But taste it next to the Amrut and see whether the cask shows more clearly when there's no smoke in the way.

Again, no age statement. Have a look at the label for what it does tell you.

## [SLIDE 11] Kavalan: private distilling in Taiwan from 2002

The third distillery is in Taiwan, and it couldn't have existed much earlier than it did.

Until 2002 alcohol in Taiwan was a state monopoly. Taiwan joined the World Trade Organization at the start of 2002, and as part of that the monopoly was opened up and private companies were allowed to distil.

One of the companies that took the chance was King Car, a food and drinks group founded by Lee Tien-tsai in 1979 and best known in Taiwan for canned coffee. It built a malt distillery in Yilan County, on the north-east coast, in 2005, and the first spirit came off the stills on 11 March 2006. It named the whisky Kavalan, the old name of the Yilan area and of the indigenous people who lived there, and it hired the Scottish consultant Jim Swan to advise on wood and maturation. Yilan is subtropical and humid, and the warehouses run hot for most of the year.

## [SLIDE 12] January 2010: Kavalan wins a blind tasting in Leith

Kavalan's first bottles, the single malt now called Classic, came out in December 2008.

In January 2010, around Burns Night, the Scottish editor of *The Times* asked the whisky writer Charles MacLean to set up a blind tasting. The paper wanted to see how St George's, a new English malt, compared with Scotch. The rule was that nothing could be older than the St George's, so everything in the line-up was very young. It was held at the Vintners' Rooms in Leith, Edinburgh's port, by four judges, with MacLean in the chair.

The surprise entry was a Kavalan, reported as two years old, which was not then sold in Britain. It scored 27.5 out of 40 and came first. Two three-year-old Scotch blends came next, then St George's, and young Scotch malts last. Accounts differ on whether there were five whiskies or six; I've followed MacLean's own. None of them names the Kavalan bottling.

It was one small tasting set up for a newspaper story. The result was reported around the world, and Kavalan went on to win larger, judged competitions. At the World Whiskies Awards in 2015, Kavalan Solist Vinho Barrique, a single-cask, cask-strength bottling matured in casks that had held wine, was named World's Best Single Malt.

## [SLIDE 13] Pour 3 of 4: Kavalan Classic

This is Kavalan Classic, the distillery's first single malt. The American bottling is 43%; in Britain it's 40%, so check your label. After the Amrut it will seem light.

It doesn't carry an age. Notice how much colour and how much sweetness from the wood there is for a whisky from a distillery that only started in 2006.

## [SLIDE 14] Why these labels carry no age

None of our first three bottles tells you how old it is, for three reasons.

The first is cost, from the arithmetic on slide eight. Old stock is very expensive to make in a hot warehouse.

The second is that the number would mislead. A drinker reads "four years" against Scotch, where four years is very young, and expects something raw.

The third is that the companies make the case that the number measures the wrong thing. Kavalan's master blender, Ian Chang, has said a year in a cask in Taiwan does what four or five do in a cooler climate. That is a reasonable argument and also a convenient one for a distillery with no old stock, and it's fair to keep both in mind.

Where an age is given, here or in Scotland, the law in Scotland and the European Union says it must be the age of the youngest whisky in the bottle.

## [SLIDE 15] Pour 4 of 4: Glenfiddich 12 Year Old

Last pour, and this one is the control. Glenfiddich 12, from Dufftown in Speyside, made by William Grant and Sons. It's bottled at 40%.

Every drop in it spent at least twelve years in a Scottish warehouse, losing something like 2% a year. Taste it against the Kavalan, which is close in strength, and then against the other two. See whether you'd have guessed which one was three times older. Then go back to whichever you liked best; I'll ask for a show of hands.

## [SLIDE 16] Reading a world whisky label

To finish, a short guide for when you see one of these on a shelf.

"Single malt" means malted barley, from one distillery. In Scotland and the EU that's a legal definition; Amrut, Paul John and Kavalan all follow it.

"Whisky" in the European Union means distilled from a mash of malted cereals and aged at least three years in wood. An Indian bottle without "malt" or "grain" on it may be molasses spirit, which is legal in India and not whisky in Europe.

An age statement, where there is one, is the youngest whisky in the bottle. A missing age on a hot-country malt is normal and tells you very little.

The alcohol strength on these labels is the one number you can always compare.
