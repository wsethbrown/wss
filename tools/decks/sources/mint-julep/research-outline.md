# Mint Julep

Research outline: a map for doing your own digging

_This is the other half of the speaker notes. It doesn't tell you what to say; it tells you where to look, what's already settled, what's still contested, and which widely repeated "facts" will embarrass you. Work through whichever module interests you. Each one is a night's reading, not a semester. Facts were checked again in November 2026; the Derby cup programme and bottle prices change every year, so check those before the night._

> **THE SPINE OF THE STORY**
> Persian rosewater medicine (gulāb) → Arabic julāb → medieval European apothecary syrup → the rose is swapped for mint → Virginia turns it into a morning dram of rum, then brandy → ice arrives, 1807 → Black bartenders make it the finest drink in America → tax revolt, civil war, and phylloxera hand it to bourbon → it dies of fashion → the Kentucky Derby resurrects it as a souvenir.
> Every load-bearing element there is Virginian rather than Kentuckian. That's the reading the evidence below points to, but the sources are listed so you can weigh them yourself and land wherever they take you.

## How to use this

Each module gives you the question worth answering, the facts that are nailed down (with dates, so you can check them), the primary documents, most of them free online, and the traps. Chase the primary sources. Cocktail history is unusually infested with blog posts citing blog posts, and the underlying documents are almost all digitized and sitting there for free.

Where a fact below was checked against a primary source for this edition, the source is named with it and listed under **Sources read for this edition** at the end. Where only a secondary account could be reached, the claim is in **Not confirmed**.

## Module 1. The word before the drink

_Why is an American whiskey drink named after a Persian rose?_

### What's established

- Persian gulāb = gul (rose) + āb (water). Enters Arabic as julāb, medieval Latin as julapium, Old French as julap, Middle English in the 14th century (Merriam-Webster: "Middle English, from Middle French, from Arabic julāb, from Persian gulāb, from gul rose + āb water"; first known use 14th century).
- In English it means a sweetened liquid used as a vehicle for medicine, not a drink you order for fun. 18th-century British medical texts are full of camphor juleps and worse. The American Museum of July 1787, for one, prints a julep "for the most part effectually" restraining the stomach, made with laudanum and oil of peppermint.
- The rose → mint substitution happens somewhere in the drink's westward drift. Nobody knows who did it or when. This is a genuine hole in the record, not a fact being withheld. The map on slide 3 puts it in the Levant to show the direction of travel; no document places it there.

### Go to the source

- › _OED and Merriam-Webster, entry "julep": the etymology chain in one place._
- › _David Wondrich, The Oxford Companion to Spirits & Cocktails (2021), the "julep" entry. The single best 800 words on this. Your library likely has database access._
- › _Search 18th-century British pharmacopoeias on archive.org for julep: the recipes are grim and make the point better than any summary._

### Threads worth pulling

- Track how mint julep shows up in English medical books before it shows up in America. Some of those are mint-flavored spirit cut with salt and balsam. What was it curing?
- Compare the julep's path west to sherbet and sorbet, which came the same way from the same word-family. Two Persian drinks, two very different American afterlives.

> **TRAPS**
> Do not say the julep "came from" a Persian cocktail. The word and the form (sweet syrup + aromatic + water) travelled. The alcohol is an American addition.

## Module 2. The paper trail

_What is the actual earliest evidence, and why does everyone cite the wrong document?_

### What's established

- **1770.** Robert Munford's play The Candidates (plural; written about 1770, first printed in 1798 in a collection his son published) is reported to feature a drunkard named Mr. Julip. The same year, the Virginia Gazette (Rind, Williamsburg, 11 January 1770) runs "A Short Poem on Hunting", which begins "At early dawn I leave a downy bed" and mentions the julep. Satire this early means the drink was already common.
- **1784.** Medical Communications, vol. 1 (London, 1784): Maxwell Garthshore, M.D., a London physician, reports a woman first seen on 20 February 1783 with sickness at the stomach and "a difficulty of swallowing": "I then prescribed her an emetic, some … powders, and a mint julep." A London case, not a Virginian one.
- **1787.** The American Museum (Philadelphia, March 1787): "A man in this line rises in the morning about six o'clock. He then drinks a julap, made of rum, water, and sugar, but very strong." The passage is a reprint from J. F. D. Smyth, A Tour in the United States of America (London, 1784), describing "the lower, and many of the middling classes" of Virginia. Note: no mint.
- **1793.** Rev. Harry Toulmin, in Norfolk, gives effectively the first recipe: rum and water, well sweetened, with a slip of mint.
- **1803.** John Davis, Travels of Four Years and a Half in the United States of America (London, 1803). The line is a footnote: "A dram of spirituous liquor that has mint steeped in it, taken by Virginians of a morning." It glosses the story of "Dick the Negro", an enslaved groom, who says his young master's first act on getting out of bed "was to call for a Julep; and I honestly date my own love of whiskey, from mixing and tasting my young master's Juleps."
- Every drinking reference here is Virginian. Kentucky was part of Virginia until it was admitted as a state on 1 June 1792.

### Go to the source

- › _John Davis, Travels… (1803): full text on archive.org (travelsfouryear00davigoog) and Google Books. Read the surrounding pages, not just the quote; the "Old Dick" passage is the one the definition belongs to._
- › _J. F. D. Smyth, A Tour in the United States of America (London, 1784), vol. 1, archive.org (tourinunitedstat01stua_0); and its reprint in The American Museum, March 1787, archive.org (sim_american-museum-or-universal-magazine_1787-03_1_3)._
- › _Medical Communications, vol. 1 (London, 1784), archive.org (bim_eighteenth-century_medical-communications-_society-for-promoting-me_1784_1): Garthshore's case of difficult swallowing._
- › _Virginia Gazette archive: Colonial Williamsburg has it digitized and searchable. Find the 11 Jan 1770 poem yourself; it takes ten minutes._
- › _Robert Munford, The Candidates: in print in scholarly editions of early American drama, and online in the University of North Carolina's Documenting the American South._

### Threads worth pulling

- Davis is cited as "the first mention" in roughly every article ever written. It is at least the fifth. Why did Davis win? (Guess: he's quotable, he's a book rather than a newspaper, and he's in English archives.) This is a nice little case study in how a canonical citation forms.
- The 1787 julep has no mint. When exactly does mint become obligatory? The answer is somewhere in the 1780s–90s and it is not firmly established. There is a real finding here for someone patient.
- Davis's Dick mixed juleps, tasted them, and loved whiskey for it. Whose julep is the first one in print, then: the master's or the man who made it?

> **TRAPS**
> Retellings frequently misdate John Davis to the 1770s, and often call his line the first written record of the drink. His book is 1803, and it is roughly the fifth mention, not the first: the 1770s belong to Munford's play and the hunting poem. If a source puts Davis first, it hasn't looked past the most quotable line.
> The 1787 "traveler in American Museum magazine" is Smyth, three years earlier. Either date is defensible; say which one you mean.
> "White Sulphur Springs, West Virginia" is an anachronism for an 1808 resort menu: West Virginia doesn't exist until 1863. It was Virginia. (The resort is now The Greenbrier.)

## Module 3. Ice, and why it is the whole story

_The julep's defining ingredient is the one nobody lists. When does it arrive, and what does it change?_

### What's established

- **4 May 1807.** First known printed reference to an "Iced Julep": an advertisement for the Wig-Wam Gardens, in the Norfolk Gazette and Publick Ledger.
- Richmond taverns were building ice-houses as early as the 1780s.
- **1817.** Orsamus Willard, at New York's City Hotel, is taught the iced julep by a Virginian patron. From there it goes national, then global.
- **1830s.** In New York, ice smashed with a mallet produces the "hailstorm julep". Tyrone Power, writing of 1833–35, calls Cato Alexander "foremost amongst cullers of mint, whether for julep or hail-storm" (Impressions of America, 1836, vol. 1, p. 58).
- Wondrich's larger claim: the iced julep is the drink that taught America to drink cold. The entire on-the-rocks tradition descends from it.

### Go to the source

- › _Norfolk Gazette and Publick Ledger, 4 May 1807, p. 3: the ad is filed under "Fire Works." Chase it through Virginia newspaper archives (Virginia Chronicle) and Chronicling America._
- › _Gavin Weightman, The Frozen Water Trade: Frederic Tudor and the Boston ice industry. This is the book that makes the julep make sense._
- › _Wondrich, Imbibe!: on Willard and the early American bar._
- › _Tyrone Power, Impressions of America (London, 1836), vol. 1, archive.org (impressamer01powerich): the drive to Cato's and the "hail-storm"._

### Threads worth pulling

- Map the ice trade against the julep's spread. Tudor starts shipping ice in 1806; the first iced julep ad is 1807. Is that a coincidence? Probably not.
- Ice is a class marker before it's a commodity. Who could afford a julep in 1810, and what did serving one signal?

## Module 4. The men who actually made it

_Why are the best bartenders of the julep's golden age missing from the julep's story?_

### What's established

- Roughly 1820 to the Civil War: the most celebrated bartenders in America were Black, enslaved and free, and the julep was their signature drink.
- **Cato Alexander (c. 1780–1858).** Born enslaved in New York; free by 1799. As a boy, working in an inn, he waited on George Washington, during Washington's presidency, when he lived in New York. Opened Cato's Tavern on the Boston Post Road c. 1811; ran it about 30 years. Ruined by lending money to customers. Tyrone Power (1836): "a gentleman of colour who presides at a little tavern … some four miles from New York", "second to no man as a compounder of cock-tail, and such a hand at a gin-sling!"
- **Jasper Crouch,** Richmond: caterer and cook to the Quoit Club, also called the Barbecue Club, and to the Richmond Light Infantry Blues. Samuel Mordecai (Richmond in By-Gone Days, 1856) gives the club's drinks as "toddy, punch and mint julip", records Judge Marshall among its quoit players, and names "Jasper Crouch, their mulatto cook, and who officiated at all public dinners". Wondrich calls him a freedman. Often named the first acknowledged master of the julep.
- **"Carter"** kept the "Hole in the Wall" bar inside the U.S. Capitol until it was lost to renovation around mid-century.
- **Tom Bullock:** The Ideal Bartender (1917), the first cocktail book published by a Black author in the United States.

### John Dabney (c. 1824–1900): the big one

- Born enslaved near Hanover Junction (now Doswell), Hanover County, Virginia; held by the DeJarnette family, latterly Cora Williamson DeJarnette. Trained first as a jockey; when he outgrew it, hired out through her brother to the railroad restaurant at Gordonsville (head waiter at 18, his son wrote) and then to Richmond hotels: the Columbian, then the bar and kitchen of the Ballard House and Exchange Hotel in the late 1850s.
- Could not read or write. Wendell Dabney records that when a member of the owner's family tried to teach him, "she was detected and sent away, while he received a severe whipping."
- Married Elizabeth Foster, also enslaved. When her owners decided to sell her after the birth of their son Clarence, Dabney, who already had an agreement to buy his own freedom from his tips, paused those payments with DeJarnette's consent and bought Elizabeth.
- Opened his own establishments while still enslaved: the Senate House (1862) and the Dabney House restaurant (1865), with Black partners, blocks from Richmond's slave-trading district.
- He was still paying instalments on his own freedom when Emancipation cancelled the debt. He paid the balance anyway. The Richmond Enquirer's account, reprinted on the front page of the Alexandria Gazette on 26 September 1866, praised his "sense of honesty and personal obligation" and said "John will stand higher than ever in the estimation of every Richmond gentleman." Wendell: "My father's note was good at any bank, and his word equivalent to an oath."
- The City of Richmond gave him an engraved silver goblet thanking him for his "Champion Juleps". A Lexington journalist in 1894 described a quart-sized sterling goblet 14 inches high inscribed "John Dabney, presented by the gentlemen of Richmond for champion juleps" (quoted by Wondrich).
- **7 June 1900.** Died at home, 1414 East Broad Street. All four of Richmond's white dailies covered it. The Richmond Dispatch (8 June 1900): "He had the reputation of being the best man living to mix mint juleps." The Times (8 June 1900): "the well-known caterer and the celebrated julep maker", "about seventy-five years of age". The Black Richmond Planet, three weeks later, gave him twelve words, appended to a sentence about another man's funeral.
- His son Wendell Phillips Dabney (born November 1865, named for the abolitionist) became a Cincinnati newspaper founder and first president of its NAACP chapter, and is the main source for his father's life.

### The Dabney recipe, and why it matters

- Dabney never wrote it down; he spoke it to his son. Crushed ice packed as tight as possible; sugar; mint bruised in with the ice; then good brandy; crowned with more mint, a strawberry, a cherry, a slice of pineapple, and "any other fixings you like."
- The greatest julep maker in American history was making a brandy drink, not bourbon. If you take one fact from this module, take that one, but see the trap below.
- He shaved his ice with a carpenter's plane turned blade-up, with wooden sides to catch the shavings. He froze ice onto the outside of the cup in patterns; Wendell admitted, "I could never make the ice stick on the outside of a big mint julep."

### Jim Cook and the Prince of Wales, October 1860

- The Prince stayed at the Ballard Hotel (N. A. Woods, The Prince of Wales in Canada and the United States, 1861). The Toronto account of the tour (Visit of His Royal Highness the Prince of Wales … in the Year 1860, Rowsell, 1861, pp. 375–76) says "The Exchange Hotel in Richmond boasts the possession of the best compounder of cooling drinks in the world. The name of this celebrated man is Jim Cook." The Ballard and the Exchange were joined and run as one hotel.
- That account, about 330 words, describes a tumbler "capable of holding a pint and a half", shaved ice pressed into an obelisk mould, a bouquet of flowers on top, the glass surrounded with ornamental ice bored through for straws. The Prince "sucked away until it was all gone; and then ordered another", which the Duke of Newcastle, the Earl of St Germains and Major General Bruce helped dispatch. A third, for the next morning, "was a very large one; was furnished with thirteen tubes, out of which an equal number of the Royal party pulled at one and the same time."
- The account does not name Dabney, and does not say whether Cook was enslaved. Wondrich calls Cook "also a slave" and Dabney's only rival in elaborate juleps.

### Tom Bullock (1872–1964): the one who left a book

- Born Louisville, 1872. His father was a formerly enslaved man who fought for the Union Army. Began as a bell boy at Louisville's Pendennis Club, rose to bartender, worked a railroad bar, then the St. Louis Country Club. (G. H. Walker's introduction to his book names the Pendennis Club and the St. Louis Country Club, "for the past quarter of a century"; Wondrich names Louisville's Kenton Club.)
- The Ideal Bartender (1917), the first cocktail book published by a Black author in America. Introduced by George Herbert Walker, grandfather of one president and great-grandfather of another.
- **Roosevelt v. Newett** (Marquette, Michigan, May 1913). The Iron Ore had printed that Roosevelt "gets drunk, and that not infrequently, and all his intimate friends know it." On the stand (26 May 1913) Roosevelt said: "At the White House we had a mint bed … Since I left the White House … I have drunk mint juleps twice, on one occasion at the Country club at St. Louis" where, in another report, "I drank part of a glass".
- The St. Louis Post-Dispatch editorial of 28 May 1913, as reprinted at the front of The Ideal Bartender: "Who was ever known to drink just a part of one of Tom's? Tom, than whom there is no greater mixologist of any race, color or condition of servitude, was taught the art of the julep by no less than Marse Lilburn G. McNair, the father of the julep."
- **31 May 1913.** Newett retracted, saying he could find no witness who had seen Roosevelt drink to excess. Roosevelt waived damages and the jury, as directed, awarded the nominal six cents allowed by Michigan law.

### Go to the source

- › _Encyclopedia Virginia, "John Dabney (ca. 1824–1900)" by Philip J. Schwarz: free, scholarly, footnoted. Start here, not with the blogs._
- › _hailstormdabney.com: the research site behind The Hail-Storm: John Dabney in Virginia, a documentary by Hannah Ayers and Lance Warren (Field Studio). The long-form "story" page is the richest thing online, and reproduces the 1866 and 1900 newspaper pieces._
- › _Richmond Dispatch, 8 June 1900, p. 2, and The Times (Richmond), 8 June 1900, p. 7: the obituaries, free on Chronicling America._
- › _Wendell P. Dabney's own writings: he is the primary source for almost everything known. Cincinnati's Colored Citizens (1926) is on archive.org for borrowing._
- › _David Wondrich, "A History of Black Bartenders," The Bitter Southerner (2016): free online; the best entry point for the wider trade._
- › _Samuel Mordecai, Richmond in By-Gone Days (1856), archive.org (richmondinbygone01mord): the Quoit Club and Jasper Crouch._
- › _Tyrone Power, Impressions of America (1836), vol. 1, archive.org: Cato's._
- › _Visit of His Royal Highness the Prince of Wales to the British North American Provinces and United States in the Year 1860 (Toronto: Rowsell, 1861), archive.org (visitofhisroyalh00celluoft), pp. 375–76: Jim Cook's julep._
- › _Tom Bullock, The Ideal Bartender (1917): public domain, full text on Project Gutenberg (no. 13487) and as a scan on Wikimedia Commons (DPLA, Missouri Historical Society copy). Read the front matter, not just the recipes: the Post-Dispatch reprint and the Walker introduction are the story._
- › _James McCommons, "Roosevelt v. Newett: The Libel Trial of 1913," in Upper Country: A Journal of the Lake Superior Region, free via NMU Commons. The full trial account._
- › _Chronicling America, May–June 1913: the testimony and verdict were wired nationally (East Oregonian, 27 May; Las Vegas Optic, 27 May; Daily Missoulian, 29 May and 1 June)._
- › _Chantal Martineau, "Tom Bullock and the Forgotten Legacy of African American Bartenders," PUNCH._

### Threads worth pulling

- Who actually made the Prince's julep? Encyclopedia Virginia and most Richmond sources say Dabney. Wondrich argues it was Jim Cook, and the 1861 Toronto account names only Cook. Go read the 1860 tour account and decide. This is a live disagreement and you are allowed an opinion.
- Why did he pay the debt? Three explanations, all defensible: the white papers said honour; Wendell said gratitude, because DeJarnette had let him pause payments while he fought to keep his wife; historians say he was buying a reputation, which converted directly into bank credit. Present all three.
- The Planet's twelve-word obituary against four full white obituaries is a whole slide on its own: who gets remembered, and by whom.
- Bullock's book was prefaced by George Herbert Walker, grandfather of a U.S. president. Sit with that.
- The Post-Dispatch's "of any race, color or condition of servitude" is close to the Fifteenth Amendment's "race, color, or previous condition of servitude". Was that the joke, and who was it on?

> **TRAPS**
> Cato "regularly served George Washington at his tavern" is wrong: Washington died in 1799, the tavern opened c. 1811. He waited on Washington as a child, at an inn.
> The pierceandluna.com post is decent but has three errors: it calls the owner "Cara" (it's Cora) Williamson DeJarnette; it places the Sweet Springs resort in "West Virginia" (in the 1850s that was still Virginia); and it hands the Prince's julep to Dabney without noting the Cook dispute. Its 3 oz peach brandy measure is a modern reconstruction: Dabney's spoken recipe gives no quantities at all.
> The recipe Dabney spoke to his son is brandy. The Hail-Storm site describes his juleps as "brandy or whiskey", and an account by General Dabney H. Maury (1906) gives whiskey. Say "his recipe called for brandy", not "he never used whiskey".
> Do not sand the edges off him. Dabney kept a gun behind the bar and was, by his son's account, hot-tempered. The charming-servant version is the version his white customers wanted.

## Module 5. How bourbon stole the drink

_The julep was a brandy drink. What actually changed, and it wasn't taste?_

### What's established

- **3 March 1791.** Congress passes an excise tax on distilled spirits (Hamilton's): in the TTB's words, "the first nationwide internal revenue tax". This is not a tariff. A tariff is a duty on imports; an excise is a tax on domestic production. The distinction is the whole grievance.
- **Whiskey Rebellion, 1791–94:** armed resistance centred on western Pennsylvania. In 1794 the Secretary of War asked four states for 12,950 militiamen; almost 13,000 gathered at Carlisle, and Washington led them in person (TTB).
- Kentucky's resistance was different and arguably more effective: not violence but total non-compliance. Grand juries wouldn't indict for four years. Thomas Marshall (revenue chief, and John Marshall's father) offered informants half the penalties and got no takers. Washington couldn't find a prosecutor willing to take the job.
- **1802.** "By 1802, Congress repealed the distilled spirits excise tax and all other internal Federal taxes" (TTB), under Jefferson.
- Post-Civil War: imported brandy and rum become unaffordable in the ruined South.
- 1870s–80s: phylloxera devastates French vineyards; cognac becomes scarce worldwide. This is the decisive blow. By 1900 a julep means bourbon. Bullock's own book (1917) has a "Mint Julep, Kentucky Style" with "two jiggers of Old Bourbon Whiskey", next to a separate Brandy Julep.

### Go to the source

- › _TTB (Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau), Michael Hoover, "The Whiskey Rebellion": the government's own account, free at ttb.gov._
- › _Excise Act of 3 March 1791, 1 Stat. 199: National Archives legislative records._
- › _Michael Veach (Kentucky Bourbon Hall of Fame) writes clearly on the Excise Act and its Kentucky afterlife._
- › _Jerry Thomas, Bar-Tenders Guide (1862): public domain. His julep is brandy-based, and he lists five variants. Proof, in print, that bourbon had not yet won._

### Threads worth pulling

- The Jerry Thomas 1862 juleps are the best single artifact you can show a room. Print the page.
- Quantify the phylloxera argument: find cognac import figures 1865–1895 and see whether the price curve matches the bourbon substitution. Someone should do this and nobody has.

> **TRAPS**
> The 1791 measure is routinely called a tariff. It was an excise. A tariff taxes goods crossing a border; an excise taxes what you produce at home, which is precisely why distillers took it as a betrayal. Sources that call it a tariff have not understood the grievance.
> The popular story that distillers fled to Kentucky to escape the tax is a myth. The tax was federal and applied everywhere. Kentuckians just refused to pay it.
> Some sources give the repeal as 1801 (when Jefferson took office and proposed it). The TTB gives 1802.

## Module 6. Death, and the Derby

_The julep fell out of fashion. What brought it back, and is the Derby story even true?_

### What's established

- Late 19th century: the julep is too slow to make and too strong for a bar working three deep. Fizzes, Collinses and coolers take its place.
- **1875.** Meriwether Lewis Clark Jr. founds Churchill Downs; reportedly plants mint behind the clubhouse.
- **1877.** The founding anecdote: the Polish actress Helena Modjeska is handed an oversized julep meant for sharing, drinks it herself, and asks for more. No primary source has been found. Modjeska made her first American appearance in English at the California Theatre, San Francisco, on 20 August 1877.
- Modjeska learned English at thirty-six and became, in Beth Holmgren's words, "America's most distinguished Shakespearean actress" of the late 19th century. In 1883 she staged Ibsen's A Doll's House (as "Thora") at Macauley's Theatre, Louisville, opening 7 December: the first Ibsen production in the United States (IbsenStage). The Modjeska, a caramel-covered marshmallow, was created in 1889 by the Louisville candy-maker Anton Busath and is still made.
- **Official Derby drink:** sources split between 1938 and 1939. The track used decorative water glasses in 1938, patrons took them home, and the first souvenir julep glass followed (Kentucky Derby Museum). Churchill Downs' own marketing often says 1938. Pick one and say why.
- About 120,000 juleps over Derby weekend is the standard cited figure (Churchill Downs, 2009, for Oaks and Derby days together).
- **The charity cup, 2026 (Kentucky Derby 152):** Woodford Reserve sold 76 polished pewter cups at $2,000 and 76 gold-plated sterling cups at $5,000, handmade by Graham Newton of From The Vault; net proceeds to Closely Crafted, a nonprofit supporting artisan skills (Spectrum News 1, 2 April 2026). Earlier years were $1,000 and $5,000 with horse charities as beneficiaries.

### Go to the source

- › _Kentucky Derby Museum: curator Chris Goodlett is quoted in most reputable pieces; the museum is the primary institutional source._
- › _Woodford Reserve press releases, year by year: these give the exact cup specs and prices, which drift annually._
- › _Contemporary Louisville newspaper coverage of the 1877 Derby (the Courier-Journal is on Chronicling America): has anyone actually found the Modjeska anecdote in a primary source?_
- › _Beth Holmgren, Starring Madame Modjeska (2011): the scholarly life._

### Threads worth pulling

- The Modjeska story is always hedged as "legend has it." Go find the source. If it traces only to a much later retelling, that is itself a great slide. Start with her movements in spring 1877.
- The julep's revival is a marketing event, not a culinary one. Compare it to how the Old Fashioned and the Manhattan came back. Different mechanisms entirely.

> **TRAPS**
> The Derby's premium julep is widely described as a silver cup at $1,000. Check the current year before repeating it: in 2026 the pewter cup was $2,000 and the gold-plated sterling one $5,000. The details of that promotion have changed repeatedly, and most write-ups are years out of date.
> Also worth knowing: no cup metal is "historically required." Silver and pewter are traditional because metal frosts, which is the better reason anyway.

## Module 7. The tasting: Virginia vs Kentucky

_Can the room actually taste the history? (This is the point of the whole evening.)_

### What's established

- The historically accurate Virginia julep (c. 1820) is cognac, often lifted with peach brandy, sometimes wine, with berries in season and a great deal of mint.
- The Kentucky julep is bourbon, sugar, mint, crushed ice: a post-1870s drink.
- Both are smashes: the family where fresh aromatics are muddled into the drink. The mojito and the brandy smash are cousins.
- Crushed ice is not garnish. The julep is designed to dilute as you drink it; it changes in the cup, which is why the last third tastes different from the first.

### Go to the source

- › _Jerry Thomas (1862): build his brandy julep exactly as written and serve it. That is the closest thing to a time machine available to you._
- › _General Dabney H. Maury's account of John Dabney's recipe (published 1906, in The New Lucile Cook Book): crushed ice, sugar, bruised mint, whiskey, fruit on top._
- › _The Hail-Storm site's recipe page: Robert Moss's reconstruction of Dabney's julep from Wendell's description._

### Threads worth pulling

- Run it blind and ask the room which one is "the real mint julep" before revealing. It's the same shape as a label-blind tasting, with two centuries standing in for two price tags.
- Try a third glass: rum, per the 1787 and 1793 descriptions. That's the oldest recipe on record.

> **TRAPS**
> Watch for "Virginia julep" recipes that are simply the bourbon julep under a different name: the error is common online, and it quietly erases the distinction the whole story depends on. A Virginia julep is built on brandy: cognac, or domestic peach brandy, or both.
> Peach liqueur and peach schnapps are not peach brandy. Real peach brandy is distilled from peaches and is hard to find.

## Module 8. The bottles

_What goes in the two glasses, checked November 2026?_

### What's established

- **Cognac (Glass A).** Any VS cognac works. Pierre Ferrand 1840 Original Formula: about $45 for 750 ml at US retailers (Total Wine, The Liquor Barn, Mission Liquor listings, November 2026).
- **Peach brandy (Glass A).** Scarce. Catoctin Creek (Purcellville, Virginia) lists a Short Hill Mountain Peach Brandy; George Washington's Mount Vernon sells a peach brandy at about $125. Availability varies by state; check before the night.
- **Bourbon (Glass B).** Any bourbon you would drink neat. Old Forester 100 Proof: about $20–28 for 750 ml (Kroger, Smith's, ABC Fine Wine & Spirits listings, November 2026).
- No Jameson in this package, ever.

## Claims that don't hold up

These are the errors you will meet most often. They circulate widely, several of them appear together on the julep page at revelry.tours (which ranks near the top of most searches), and a few have made it into print. None of them survive contact with a primary source. Knowing them saves you from repeating them and, better, gives you something to say when someone else does.

**About the bartenders**

- "George Washington was a regular at Cato Alexander's." Cato's opened around 1811; Washington died in 1799. What's true is better: as a boy, Cato waited on Washington at an inn during Washington's New York presidency.
- "Cato invented the mint julep." / "Tom Bullock invented the mint julep." Neither. The julep is in print by 1770; Bullock was born in 1872. (Bullock's first name is also frequently mangled to "Tim.")
- "Dabney's julep was a tin bucket of bourbon." His own recipe, spoken to his son, calls for brandy, and the vessel was silver.
- "Dabney worked at a resort in West Virginia." In the 1850s that was Virginia. West Virginia does not exist until 1863.

**About the geography**

- "The julep travelled from the northern states to the southern." Backwards. It went south to north: Virginia first, then New York, where Orsamus Willard was taught it by a Virginian in 1817.
- "Distillers fled to Kentucky to escape the whiskey tax." The excise was federal. It applied everywhere. Kentucky simply declined to enforce it. And Kentucky whiskey is corn; rye is Pennsylvania and Maryland.

**About the objects, and the law**

- "A silver cup is historically required." Silver and pewter are traditional because metal frosts. That is the real reason, and a better one.
- "Prohibition converted drinkers to bourbon because corn looked less suspicious than rye." No mechanism is offered and none is obvious: Prohibition banned production of both.
- The $1,000 julep's Irish mint, Bavarian ice, Australian sugar and gold straw describe an older promotion. The programme changes; check the current year.

**About Buckner**

- "Lieutenant General S. B. Buckner Jr. of the Confederate Army." Wrong man, and the irony is worth having. Buckner Jr. (1886–1945) was a United States Army general, killed at Okinawa on 18 June 1945, the highest-ranking American killed by enemy fire in the Second World War. His father was the Confederate general. The letter's recipient was Maj. Gen. William D. Connor, not "Connar." In 1937 Buckner was not yet a general; he was a lieutenant general when he died.

> **TWO STORIES WORTH THE TROUBLE**
> The Roosevelt libel suit and the Buckner letter are both real and both verifiable, and both are better than the retellings. Go to the primary sources; they're listed in the modules above.
> Don't reproduce the Ray Charles lyrics. "One Mint Julep" (Rudy Toombs, 1952, first recorded by The Clovers) is under copyright, and Ray Charles's famous 1961 version is an instrumental, so lyrics quoted "from his cover" are doubly confused. Name the song; don't print the words.

## The short shelf

If you only chase five things, chase these.

- › _David Wondrich, The Oxford Companion to Spirits & Cocktails (2021): the "julep" entry, plus "ice, history of its use." The scholarly baseline for everything above._
- › _David Wondrich, Imbibe!: the popular version. Most reliable accounts of this drink trace back to him one way or another._
- › _Wondrich, "A History of Black Bartenders," The Bitter Southerner: free, online, essential._
- › _Jerry Thomas, Bar-Tenders Guide (1862): public domain. Five juleps, none of them bourbon-first._
- › _Tom Bullock, The Ideal Bartender (1917): public domain. The first cocktail book by a Black American author._

> **THE STANDARD OF PROOF, FOR THIS SOCIETY**
> If a claim can't be traced past a blog post, it goes on the slide as "the story goes…" and not as a fact. The Modjeska anecdote, the mint behind the clubhouse, and "the first American cocktail" all belong in that category. It costs you nothing to say so, and it makes everything else you say more believable.

## Not confirmed

Checked in November 2026 and not settled by a primary source. The script says each of these as written by the owner unless noted.

- **Mr. Julip in The Candidates.** Reported by Wondrich and repeated widely; the play's cast list could not be reached for this edition. The play's title is plural.
- **The 1770 hunting poem's couplet.** The poem is catalogued (Virginia Gazette, Rind, 11 January 1770, "A Short Poem on Hunting"); its text was not reached. The second line ("Which doctors storm at, and which some adore") is quoted the same way in secondary sources.
- **Toulmin 1793, the Wig-Wam advertisement of 4 May 1807, Orsamus Willard 1817, the 1830s hailstorm julep by mallet.** From Wondrich; the original documents were not reached. Power's 1836 "julep or hail-storm" confirms the hailstorm julep by the mid-1830s.
- **Cato Alexander:** the 1835 "Not to know Cato's is not to know the world"; the site at 54th Street and Second Avenue; that he poured brandy juleps; that he was freed "under" the 1799 Gradual Abolition Act (that Act freed children born after 4 July 1799; he may have been freed by his owner in that year). Power confirms Cato's, the four miles, and the mint.
- **Jasper Crouch as "the first acknowledged master of the julep"** and as a free man: Wondrich. Mordecai (1856) confirms the Quoit Club, its juleps, Marshall and Crouch as its cook, but uses neither phrase.
- **Carter and the Hole in the Wall:** not reached.
- **Jim Cook and Dabney as master and assistant at the same bar, both enslaved, both keeping a cut:** the 1861 account names only Cook and does not say he was enslaved; Wondrich says Cook was enslaved and calls him Dabney's rival.
- **"Years later, the only thing the future King of England could remember about Richmond was that julep."** No source found. Wondrich says Cook's julep was "the Prince's fondest memory of the city". Kept in the script as the owner's line; say it as "the story goes" if challenged.
- **Dabney's birth year.** "c. 1824" follows Encyclopedia Virginia and Wondrich; the Hail-Storm researchers' reading of his death certificate puts his birthday six weeks after 7 June, in 1825.
- **The Planet's twelve words** were read in the Hail-Storm transcription, not in the Planet itself.
- **Roosevelt "noted that six cents was about the price of a good newspaper"; the Iron Ore "sold for three";** "Roosevelt has waited years for someone to put that in print". From McCommons, which could not be reached; not found in the 1913 wire reports read.
- **Kentucky and the excise:** the four years of grand juries, Thomas Marshall's offer of half the penalties, the letter to Senator Brown. From Veach and Wondrich; not reached in a primary source.
- **"More men than he commanded at most Revolutionary battles"** (slide 13): not checked against battle strengths.
- **The Modjeska anecdote,** Clark's mint behind the clubhouse, "Grant, Sherman and Mark Twain were friends", and "godmother to Ethel Barrymore" (Wikipedia states it in one place; Ethel Barrymore's own account, as summarised there, names Modjeska as Lionel's godmother and others as hers).
- **About 120,000 juleps** is a 2009 Churchill Downs figure.
- **The Wondrich line** ("so high you can't get over it…") is quoted as the owner has it; its exact wording in print was not checked.
- **Buckner's letter** wording ("a novice, a statistician, nor a Yankee") was not checked against the letter.
