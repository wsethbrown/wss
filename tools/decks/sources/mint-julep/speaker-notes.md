## Part One: before the night

Everything in roman type below is written to be read aloud, more or less as written. Green lines are stage directions. Boxed asides are optional: use them if it feels right, skip them if you're running long. Total speaking time is about 30 minutes.

### The one-sentence version

The mint julep is a thousand-year-old Persian medicine that lost its roses, crossed the Atlantic, got packed with ice by enslaved and free Black bartenders in Virginia, and only became a bourbon drink because brandy got expensive. If you remember nothing else tonight, remember that the bourbon is the newest thing in the glass.

### Making the drinks for the room

In terms of efficiency, to make the drinks for the room without too much delay:

- Get a bag of ice and crush it with a rubber mallet into very, very small pieces outside. Put it into a cooler for quick access.
- Batch the Virginia julep's liquor. Muddle a mint in each glass, fill each with ice, then fill with the batch and hand them out at slide 2. Don't reveal the ingredients, or that it's not the "traditional" julep they may have had before.
- Then, after slide 12, take a quick break, refresh their glasses, and make the bourbon version before continuing on.

### Cast the roles, then find the bottles

Two drinks, three bottles. Each bottle has a job in the story.

- **The brandy (Glass A).** The spirit a good julep was built on around 1820. Tonight: a cognac.
- **The peach brandy (Glass A).** The domestic lift Virginians added. Half an ounce per drink.
- **The bourbon (Glass B).** The spirit that took the drink over after the war and phylloxera.

### Bottle ideas by role

Suggestions, not instructions. Prices are rough US shelf prices checked in November 2026; they move, and they vary by state. Buy what you can find.

**The brandy**

- **Pierre Ferrand 1840 Original Formula cognac.** About $45. A cognac sold for mixing.
- **Any VS cognac you like.** It is going under sugar, mint and a mound of ice.

**The peach brandy**

- **A real peach brandy, distilled from peaches.** Hard to find. Catoctin Creek in Virginia makes one; Mount Vernon sells a peach brandy from George Washington's estate at about $125. Check the label: peach liqueur and peach schnapps are sweetened and are not peach brandy.
- **If you cannot find one,** make Glass A with 2 oz cognac and say so.

**The bourbon**

- **Old Forester 100 Proof.** About $24. A higher-proof bourbon holds up as the ice melts.
- **Any bourbon you would drink neat.**

## Part Two: the script

_SET UP: crushed ice in a cooler, the Virginia julep's spirits batched, mint and glasses ready. One empty julep cup to hold up at slide 1._

## [SLIDE 1] Mint Julep

_Hold up an empty julep cup. Don't pour yet._

Tonight's drink is the one everybody thinks they know. Bourbon, mint, sugar, crushed ice, silver cup, first Saturday in May.

Almost all of that is a modern invention. The bourbon is recent. The Derby is recent. The only part that is genuinely old is the mint, and mint isn't even the original leaf.

So here's my promise for the next half hour: by the end of it, you will not be able to call this a Kentucky drink with a straight face.

## [SLIDE 2] Start with glass A

_Pour Glass A now: the Virginia julep. Don't say what's in it yet._

Taste that first. Sit with it a second. I'll tell you what it is in about twenty minutes, and I want you to have an opinion before I do.

What I'll say now is what goes into every julep: sugar, mint, spirit and crushed ice.

## [SLIDE 3] The road to the julep

We're going to start with the word "julep", because it's older than the drink by several centuries.

It's a Persian word, originally gulāb: gul, rose; āb, water. Rosewater. A syrup of rose petals and sugar. It moves into Arabic as julāb, into medieval Latin as julapium, into Old French, and lands in Middle English around the year 1400.

And somewhere along that road, as the drink moves west through the Mediterranean, where roses are a luxury and mint grows in every ditch, the rose gets swapped for mint. That substitution is the single most important thing that ever happened to this drink.

> **SAY THE CAVEAT OUT LOUD**
> Nobody knows who swapped the rose for mint, or where. The map puts it in the Levant to show the direction of travel, not because a document says so.

## [SLIDE 4] It's good for you

For most of its history a julep was a medicine.

A julep, in the medical sense, is a sweet liquid you put medicine into: the spoonful of sugar, essentially. Eighteenth-century British medical books are full of them, and they are, by and large, disgusting: camphor juleps, juleps with balsam in them, juleps cut with salt.

In 1784 you can find a physician in Medical Communications prescribing a mint julep to a woman with a sick stomach and trouble swallowing, right alongside an emetic and some powders.

And in America it keeps that flavor for a long time. Virginians drank it at six in the morning, before breakfast, as a restorative: what the period called an antifogmatic, a morning dram to burn off the damp and keep the fever away. A julep was a health measure. So was, conveniently, being drunk by seven.

The first people who were serving this drink were typically enslaved people offering their owners a bit of tummy relief in the mornings.

## [SLIDE 5] The paper trail

1770. Two Virginia references in the same year. Robert Munford writes a satirical play, The Candidates, with a drunkard in it named, subtly, Mr. Julip.

And on the eleventh of January, the Virginia Gazette in Williamsburg prints a foxhunting poem by an anonymous author signed only "S. X." Read the couplet:

> **THE FIRST JULEP IN AMERICAN LITERATURE**
> "The sportsmen ready, and the julep o'er,
> Which doctors storm at, and which some adore."
> S. X., "A Short Poem on Hunting," Virginia Gazette, 11 January 1770

The doctors storm at it. So by 1770 the julep has already escaped the prescription pad: the physicians disapprove, and the sportsmen drink it anyway, before the hunt, at dawn. That is not medicine. That is a morning drink, and everyone reading that poem got the joke without being told.

Which means the drink is already old in 1770. This is the first time it appears in print. It is not the first time anybody made one.

1784. The medical prescription in Medical Communications. That one is a London doctor and a London patient.

1787. A traveler in American Museum magazine describes the Virginian "rising at six and drinking a julep of rum, water and sugar, very strong". Note: rum. And no mint.

1793. The Reverend Harry Toulmin, an English minister, is in Norfolk and writes down what is essentially the first recipe: "a tumbler of rum and water, well sweetened, with a slip of mint in it".

1803. An Englishman named John Davis publishes his Travels of Four Years and a Half in the United States and defines a julep as "a dram of spirituous liquor that has mint steeped in it, taken by Virginians of a morning".

Davis puts that definition in a footnote to the story of an enslaved groom he calls Dick, whose young master called for a julep the moment he got out of bed. Dick mixed them.

Apart from that London prescription, every single one of those takes place in Virginia. Not one of them is Kentucky!

> **IF SOMEONE ASKS "WHAT ABOUT KENTUCKY?"**
> Kentucky was Virginia until 1792. When Virginians walked west over the mountains, they took their breakfast habits with them. The julep didn't travel to Kentucky. Virginia moved there.

## [SLIDE 6] In 1789, all of this was Virginia

Kentucky was part of Virginia until 1792. The early julep references all come from the south-east corner of the state.

_Point to the circle: Williamsburg and Mecklenburg County in 1770, Norfolk in 1793 and 1807._

## [SLIDE 7] The Virginia julep

This is what was in a julep around 1820, in Virginia.

Rum has fallen out of fashion after the Revolution. So the good julep is built on fine French brandy, sometimes domestic peach brandy, sometimes cut with Spanish or Portuguese wine. There are berries in it, whatever's in season. There's a large bunch of mint on top. And underneath all of it is a mountain of shaved ice.

It was an expensive drink, and serving one was a way to show wealth and hospitality.

## [SLIDE 8] Ice, ice, baby

_Optional: ask the room to guess the year ice shows up in the recipe._

Now the thing that actually made the julep matter.

Ice. Before ice, a julep is a warm sweet dram. With ice, it becomes the most refreshing thing anyone in the American South has ever put in their mouth.

The first known advertisement for an iced julep runs on the fourth of May, 1807, in a Norfolk newspaper, for a pleasure garden called the Wig-Wam. Ten years later, in 1817, a bartender named Orsamus Willard at New York's City Hotel is taught to make an iced julep by a visiting Virginian, and the thing goes national.

By the 1830s New York bartenders were crushing ice with a mallet and calling the result a hailstorm julep.

## [SLIDE 9] Wholly American

The julep, in its great period, was overwhelmingly made by Black Americans, enslaved and free. Between roughly 1820 and the Civil War, the most celebrated bartenders in this country were Black men, and the julep was their signature.

Cato Alexander, born around 1780, enslaved in New York, freed under the state's gradual abolition act in 1799. As a boy working in an inn he waited on George Washington.

Around 1811 he opened his own place, and where he put it tells you everything. He built it four miles up the Harlem Road, out at the mile marker. New York in 1811 was just the southern tip of Manhattan; four miles out was countryside. Today you'd be standing on 54th Street, a block east of Second Avenue.

It was a ten-minute gallop out of town, and that was the point. Cato's became the place the city's fast young men drove out to. They raced their carriages up the Harlem Road, drank his gin cocktails and his brandy juleps and his punches, ate his game and his curried oysters, and raced each other back, sometimes, the record notes, with disastrous results. He had invented the drink-drive, and sited the bar at exactly the distance to make it irresistible.

For thirty years it was one of the most fashionable rooms in America. In 1835 a New York paper asked who hadn't heard of Cato Alexander, and answered itself: "Not to know Cato's is not to know the world." The actor Tyrone Power called him "foremost amongst cullers of mint, whether for julep or hail-storm" and "second to no man as a compounder of cock-tail." He lost it all lending money to customers who didn't pay him back.

And note what he was pouring. Brandy juleps. In New York, at the most famous bar in the country, in the julep's golden age. This is not a Virginia peculiarity. Nobody, anywhere, was making this drink with whiskey.

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> Brandy was the julep's spirit of choice, but not the only one. Davis's Dick, in Virginia in the 1790s, said he dated his own love of whiskey from mixing and tasting his young master's juleps. If someone knows that, agree with them: whiskey was in some juleps early. It was not the drink's spirit until much later.

Jasper Crouch, a free man of color in Richmond, chef and bartender to the Quoit Club and the Richmond Light Infantry Blues, the poshest rooms in the city. He is generally named the first acknowledged master of the mint julep. Chief Justice John Marshall drank his juleps.

Samuel Mordecai, writing about old Richmond in 1856, remembered the Quoit Club's drinks as toddy, punch and mint julep, and Jasper Crouch as the cook who officiated at all its dinners. Marshall was a member.

There was a man known only as Carter who kept a bar called the Hole in the Wall inside the United States Capitol until it was demolished in the building's renovations. One does wonder whether Congress would be quite so partisan if it still had one.

And Tom Bullock. Born in Louisville in 1872; his father was a formerly enslaved man who fought for the Union. Bell boy at Louisville's Pendennis Club, then the bar, then the St. Louis Country Club. In 1917 he published The Ideal Bartender, the first cocktail book by a Black author in America. We'll come back to him, because in 1913 a former President of the United States went to court over one of his juleps.

> **THE LINE TO LAND ON**
> David Wondrich puts it best, and I'll borrow it: the African-American influence on the American bar ran the same way it ran through Southern cooking and through American music, so high you can't get over it, so deep you can't get under it, so wide you can't go around it.

## [SLIDE 10] The Prince's julep

October 1860. The Prince of Wales, nineteen years old, the future Edward VII, is touring North America and stops in Richmond, at the Ballard House. The bar there is run by Jim Cook, with a young assistant named John Dabney, both enslaved, both permitted to keep a cut of what they earned. The Prince is told Cook is the best compounder of cooling drinks in the world.

What Cook builds for him takes more than three hundred words to describe in the published record of the royal tour. It is not a drink, it is an installation: basins and blocks and obelisks of ice with holes drilled through them for straws, a tumbler holding a pint and a half, and a bouquet of flowers on top.

The Prince takes one sip and demands the recipe. He drinks the whole thing, orders a second, which his party helps him finish, and books a third for the following morning. That one is described as very large. It has thirteen straws, and thirteen of the royal party drink from it at once. Then they go and tour the city.

Years later, the only thing the future King of England could remember about Richmond, Virginia, was that julep.

John Dabney bought his own freedom, and his wife's, on bartending money. He made his living selling the most memorable, creative, and delicious drinks to men who owned him.

> **SAY THE CAVEAT OUT LOUD**
> The 1861 account names Jim Cook and puts him at the Exchange Hotel, which was joined to the Ballard House. It does not mention Dabney. Most Richmond sources credit the Prince's julep to Dabney; David Wondrich credits Cook. You are allowed an opinion.

## [SLIDE 11] John Dabney

Born enslaved in Hanover County around 1824. He was small, so they made him a jockey. When he outgrew the horses they put him in a kitchen, found he could cook, and hired him out to a Richmond hotel bar. He could not read or write. When a member of his enslaver's family tried to teach him letters, she was sent away and he was whipped.

He shaved the ice for those juleps himself, with a carpenter's plane turned blade-up, wooden sides fitted to catch the fall.

His only real income was tips. He had already been buying his own freedom from them, in instalments. When Elizabeth's owners were preparing to sell her away from him after she had their first child, he paused those payments and bought his wife's freedom instead. Then he went back to buying his own.

Then the war ended, and slavery ended, and the debt evaporated.

He paid it anyway, in full, to the woman who had owned him.

> **WHY HE PAID**
> Richmond's white papers called it honesty and personal obligation, and said it made him stand higher than ever in the estimation of every Richmond gentleman.
> His son Wendell gave a different reason: his father felt he owed her because she had let him suspend the payments during the crisis when he was fighting to keep his wife from being sold.
> And there is a third reading, which I think is the true one. A Black man in the capital of the defeated Confederacy, staring at a very uncertain future, bought something more durable than cash. A reputation. He could afterwards get credit at any bank in Richmond, and he and Elizabeth used it to buy property and open restaurants.
> All three can be true.

He never wrote the recipe down. He couldn't. He said it out loud to his son, and it is, start to finish, a Virginia julep: crushed ice packed as tight as it will go, sugar, mint bruised in with the ice, then your good brandy, crowned with more mint, a strawberry, a cherry, a slice of pineapple. And then, he said, "any other fixings you like."

Note the brandy. The greatest julep maker who ever lived was not making a bourbon drink.

> **DO NOT OVERSELL IT**
> This is the recipe as his son remembered it. Other accounts of Dabney's juleps mention whiskey as well as brandy, so say "his recipe called for brandy", not "he never used whiskey".

He worked until the week he died, in June 1900. All four of Richmond's white daily newspapers reported his death. The Richmond Dispatch said he "had the reputation of being the best man living to mix mint juleps."

The Black newspaper in town, the Richmond Planet, gave him twelve words, tacked onto the end of a sentence about somebody else's funeral. Its editor was a radical, and a caterer who had spent his life charming rich white men was not his idea of a hero.

## [SLIDE 12] The six-cent julep

Back to Tom Bullock.

In 1912 a Michigan paper called the Iron Ore printed that Theodore Roosevelt got drunk, and not infrequently, and all his intimates knew it. He sued.

On the stand in Marquette in May 1913, he swore he had never been intoxicated in his life. He admitted to a mint bed at the White House. And he testified that since leaving office he had drunk a julep at the St. Louis Country Club, but only part of it.

The St. Louis Post-Dispatch would not let that stand. Who, it asked, was ever known to drink just part of one of Tom's? It called him the greatest mixologist of any race.

It is the only time in American history a man's sobriety defence was rejected on the grounds that his bartender was too good.

Roosevelt won. The editor folded, admitting he could not produce a single witness. Roosevelt asked the court for the smallest sum the law allowed: six cents. He noted that six cents was about the price of a good newspaper. The Iron Ore sold for three.

Bullock reprinted that article in the front of his own book.

> **THE PART THAT ISN'T FUNNY**
> Nobody was defending Tom Bullock here. The Post-Dispatch was mocking Roosevelt, and Bullock was the stick they hit him with: the paper's whole joke depends on the reader agreeing, instantly and without argument, that no man alive leaves one of Tom's juleps unfinished.
> That's the measure of his standing. His excellence was so taken for granted it could be used to call a former President a liar.
> But read their compliment again: the greatest mixologist of any race. That last phrase is doing a lot of work. In 1913 a white newspaper could hand a Black man the highest praise it had, and still put a fence around it in the same sentence.
> The full wording, as Bullock reprinted it, is "no greater mixologist of any race, color or condition of servitude": nearly the words of the Fifteenth Amendment.

## [SLIDE 13] Damn the excise

_Pour Glass B now: the Kentucky julep. Leave Glass A in front of them._

So how does the brandy drink become a whiskey drink? Three reasons, and none of them are about flavor.

One: taxes. In March 1791 Congress passes an excise on distilled spirits, Hamilton's tax, to pay down the war debt. It is the first federal tax on an American-made product, and it is hated. Western Pennsylvania takes up arms, and Washington rides out with thirteen thousand militia to end it.

But the interesting resistance isn't the one with guns. It's Kentucky's. Kentucky simply declined. Distillers ignored the paperwork. Even the small farmers who were legally exempt wouldn't bother to register for the exemption. Grand juries refused to indict for four straight years in the face of open, public, cheerful non-compliance. Thomas Marshall, the revenue chief, and John Marshall's father, offered informants half of everything collected if one would simply report someone breaking the law, and got no takers. Washington couldn't even find a lawyer willing to prosecute; as one man wrote to Senator Brown, "the excise was so odious no lawyer with a reputation would touch it".

Kentucky defied that law for eight years. Jefferson repealed it in 1802. So when you hear that distillers fled to Kentucky to escape the whiskey tax: it's not that the tax didn't apply in Kentucky. The tax applied everywhere. Kentucky just didn't pay it.

Two: the war. In the ruined post-war South, French brandy and imported rum are luxuries nobody can afford. Corn whiskey is right there.

Three: the bug. In the 1870s and 80s, phylloxera eats the French vineyards. Cognac becomes scarce and expensive across the entire world. By 1900, a julep means bourbon, and nobody remembers it ever meant anything else.

> **THE POINT, IF YOU ONLY MAKE ONE**
> Bourbon didn't win the julep on merit. It won on tax policy, war, and a French aphid. The most iconically Kentucky bourbon drink in America is bourbon by accident.

## [SLIDE 14] The julep goes out of style

By the late nineteenth century the julep is in trouble. It takes too long to make: you're hand-crushing ice and building a bouquet while the bar is three deep. It's too strong; it's essentially neat spirit and ice. And the new drinks are faster and lighter: fizzes, Collinses, coolers. Things you can make in fifteen seconds.

It became an old-fashioned drink, made for occasions.

## [SLIDE 15] The woman who drank it all

It is rescued, essentially, by marketing.

1877, Louisville. Meriwether Lewis Clark Jr., grandson of that Clark, has built Churchill Downs and planted mint behind the clubhouse. He hands the visiting Polish actress Helena Modjeska an enormous julep meant to be shared around the table. She drinks the whole thing herself and asks for more.

> **SAY THE CAVEAT OUT LOUD**
> This is "the story goes". Nobody has found it in an 1877 newspaper, and Modjeska's first American performance in English was in San Francisco that August, so a Louisville visit at Derby time that May is hard to fit in.

That's the origin of the association. It takes another sixty years to become official: the track noticed people were stealing the glasses, so they started selling them as souvenirs, and the julep was made the Derby's official drink at the end of the thirties.

Today Churchill Downs moves about 120,000 juleps over Derby weekend. And if you want, you can buy the charity cup: in 2026, two thousand dollars got you pewter, and five thousand got you gold-plated sterling. The money goes to charity, which is nice, but let's call it what it is: that is a rosewater cordial for sick people, marked up to five thousand dollars.

## [SLIDE 16] Two juleps

_Both glasses in front of everyone. Let the room argue before you weigh in._

So. Glass A is a Virginia julep: the drink as it existed before Kentucky got hold of it. Cognac, with a lift of peach brandy, sugar, mint, ice.

Glass B is the Kentucky julep. Bourbon, sugar, mint, ice. The one you know.

Many think the brandy julep is rounder: the fruit and the mint are on the same team, and the drink tastes like a garden. The bourbon julep is hotter and drier; the corn and the oak fight the mint a little, and that friction is part of why we like it.

Neither is wrong. But only one of them is historical, and it's the one you've probably never had before tonight.

_Ask: "Which one would you make at home?"_

## [SLIDE 17] Building it at home

The spec, if you want to make these:

Kentucky julep. Eight mint leaves, a quarter ounce of simple syrup, two ounces of bourbon. Wipe the mint around the inside of a chilled cup and drop it in. Add syrup and bourbon. Pack with crushed ice, stir until the cup frosts, then mound more ice on top and crown it with a fat sprig of mint. Short straw, so your nose is in the mint while you drink.

Virginia julep. Same build, but an ounce and a half of cognac and half an ounce of peach brandy in place of the bourbon, and a berry or two buried in the ice if you have them.

The two rules that actually matter: the ice must be crushed, not cubed, because the drink is supposed to dilute as you go. And don't beat the mint to death: bruise it, don't pulverize it, or you get chlorophyll and bitterness instead of oil and perfume.

## [SLIDE 18] Sláinte

A thousand years ago this was a Persian rose syrup handed to people who were ill.

It became a brandy drink in Virginia, made famous by men who were not free. It became a bourbon drink in Kentucky.

One last thing. The most quoted line ever written about this drink comes from a 1937 letter by Lieutenant General Simon Bolivar Buckner Jr., who was asked for the recipe and refused to give one. A julep, he wrote, is not a formula. It is a ceremony, a rite not to be entrusted to "a novice, a statistician, nor a Yankee."

His father was a Confederate general. He was not. He was a United States Army officer, and in June 1945 he was killed at Okinawa, the highest-ranking American killed by enemy fire in the Second World War.

So the definitive statement of Southern julep chauvinism was written by a man who died in the uniform of the United States, about a Persian medicine, made famous by men who were not free, in a drink that was never meant to have bourbon in it.

Every drink you like has a story like this. Most of them just haven't been written down.

Sláinte.
