# The Whiskey Rebellion

Research outline: Western Pennsylvania, 1791–1794

Western Pennsylvania, 1791–1794. A guide for anyone who wants to research the subject and build a talk of their own.

---

**A note on sources.** The facts in this deck were checked in November 2026 against the sources listed under each module: the statutes, Founders Online, George Washington's Mount Vernon, the Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia and published historians. What could not be confirmed is listed under "Not confirmed" near the end, with what would settle it.

## Module 1. The debt and the excise

**The question.** Why did the new federal government tax whiskey in 1791?

### Nailed down

- In 1790 Congress, at Hamilton's urging, funded the national debt and assumed the states' Revolutionary War debts. Import duties were the main federal revenue.
- The Excise Act was approved on 3 March 1791 (1 Stat. 199, chapter 15).
- It taxed spirits distilled from domestic materials at 9 to 25 cents a gallon, according to proof (11 to 30 cents for spirits from foreign materials).
- Owners of country stills, outside cities, towns and villages, could pay instead 60 cents a year for each gallon of still capacity, a figure based on a still working about four months a year.
- The 1792 revision (act of 8 May 1792) lowered the rates: 7 cents a gallon produced, or 10 cents per gallon of capacity for each month the still worked.

### Sources

- _Statutes at Large_, vol. 1, 1st Congress, 3rd session, ch. 15: https://govtrackus.s3.amazonaws.com/legislink/pdf/stat/1/STATUTE-1-Pg199.pdf and https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/United_States_Statutes_at_Large/Volume_1/1st_Congress/3rd_Session/Chapter_15
- Congressional Research Service, _Federal Excise Taxes on Alcoholic Beverages_ (RL30238, 1999): https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/19990615_RL30238_806510761ada40f4249ec9ea00c1336eddc8a4c0.html
- Founders Online, Hamilton, _Report on the Difficulties in the Execution of the Act Laying Duties on Distilled Spirits_ (1792), with editorial notes on the 1792 rates: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-11-02-0079
- TTB, "The Whiskey Rebellion": https://www.ttb.gov/public-information/whiskey-rebellion

### Primary documents

- Hamilton's _Report on Public Credit_ (January 1790), Founders Online.
- The acts of 3 March 1791 and 8 May 1792, above.

### Traps

- The effective rates often quoted (about 6 cents a gallon for a large distiller against 9 for a small one) come from secondary accounts and were not confirmed against the statutes. The deck states the statutory rates instead.
- In the 1791 act the capacity option was for country stills only. Do not describe it as an option that favoured town distillers.
- "The first federal tax" is wrong: import duties came first. It was the first federal tax on a domestic product.

## Module 2. Why the west distilled

**The question.** Why did the tax fall so heavily on the four western counties of Pennsylvania?

### Nailed down

- Washington, Allegheny, Westmoreland and Fayette counties lay west of the Alleghenies, with poor roads east. Whiskey was the usual way to carry surplus grain to market, and the tax was due in money in a region short of coin.
- Pinckney's Treaty (Treaty of San Lorenzo), concluded with Spain on 27 October 1795, gave Americans free navigation of the Mississippi and the right of deposit at New Orleans.

- William Findley, _History of the Insurrection in the Four Western Counties of Pennsylvania_ (1796). Findley was the region's congressman.

### Traps

- The packhorse ratio (four bushels as grain against twenty-four as whiskey) is widely repeated and untraced. The script presents it as an illustration only.

## Module 3. Resistance, 1791–1794

**The question.** How did the opposition move from petitions to violence?

### Nailed down

- Robert Johnson, collector for Washington and Allegheny counties, was tarred and feathered by disguised men on Pigeon Creek in September 1791. Sources split between 6 and 11 September.
- A meeting at Pittsburgh on 21–22 August 1792, with John Cannon in the chair and Albert Gallatin as clerk, resolved that the tax was unjust and oppressive and that collectors should be refused all help and treated with contempt.
- Washington issued a proclamation against obstruction of the excise on 15 September 1792. Hamilton drafted it; Jefferson countersigned it although he opposed the excise.
- In 1794 notices signed "Tom the Tinker" threatened distillers who complied with "a few holes through your still". The name was first used in the attack on William Cochran's still; Brackenridge says destroying a still was called "mending" it.
- "An Exciseman": an anonymous hand-drawn satire. The verse on the sheet reads "This Elegy was made August 13th 1792". Explore PA History credits the image to the Atwater Kent Museum, Philadelphia. The _Journal of the American Revolution_ dates it c. 1790; the date written on the sheet is the better evidence.

### Sources

- Founders Online, "Proclamation, 15 September 1792": https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/05-11-02-0058
- Founders Online, Jefferson Papers note on the proclamation and the Pittsburgh resolutions: https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/01-24-02-0352
- Founders Online, Alexander Hamilton to George Washington, 5 August 1794 (the government's list of incidents, including Wells and the Tom the Tinker notice): https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Washington/05-16-02-0357
- Teaching American History, Hamilton's 1794 report (Cannon chairman, Gallatin clerk): https://teachingamericanhistory.org/document/the-disagreeable-crisis-in-the-western-counties-report-on-the-whiskey-rebellion/
- PBS, _The Whiskey Rebellion_ (Johnson, Pigeon Creek, 6 September 1791): https://www.pbs.org/video/the-whiskey-rebellion-0zeivq/
- Explore PA History, "An Exciseman, anonymous, 1792": https://explorepahistory.com/displayimage.php?imgId=1-2-70E
- Teaching American History, "An Exciseman": https://teachingamericanhistory.org/document/an-exciseman/

### Traps

- Accounts by both sides were written to justify themselves afterwards. Hamilton's and Brackenridge's are both partisan.
- Where "An Exciseman" was drawn is not known. Do not call it western Pennsylvanian.

## Module 4. Bower Hill and Braddock's Field, July–August 1794

**The question.** What turned resistance into an insurrection?

### Nailed down

- U.S. district attorney William Rawle obtained writs against more than sixty western distillers, entered on 31 May 1794 and returnable in the federal court at Philadelphia. An act of 5 June 1794 allowed such cases to be tried in state courts, too late for these writs.
- In July 1794 the marshal David Lenox, with the inspector John Neville, served the writs, and shots were fired at them.
- On 16 July about fifty armed men came to Neville's house at Bower Hill. Shots from the house mortally wounded Oliver Miller.
- The soldiers at Bower Hill came from Fort Fayette, the army post at Pittsburgh that had replaced Fort Pitt in 1792, under Major Abraham Kirkpatrick. The rebels let the soldiers go and held Kirkpatrick for a time.
- On 17 July about five hundred returned. Major James McFarlane, their leader, was shot dead during what he took to be a truce; who fired is disputed. The house was burned and the defending soldiers surrendered.
- On 1 August armed men mustered at Braddock's Field, about 7,000 by the usual estimate (others give 5,000 or 6,000). Some talked of independence, even of joining Spain or Britain. They then marched through Pittsburgh. Bradford's party had robbed the mail beforehand to learn who in Pittsburgh opposed them.

## Module 5. The army, the arrests and the pardons

**The question.** How did the government end it, and how harsh was it?

### Nailed down

- Justice James Wilson certified on 4 August 1794 that the courts could not enforce the law, as the Militia Act of 2 May 1792 required. Washington's proclamation of 7 August ordered the insurgents to disperse; three commissioners (James Ross, Jasper Yeates and Attorney General William Bradford) were sent west.
- Militia came from New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Maryland and Virginia: 12,950 men by Mount Vernon's count, about 13,000.
- Washington joined the army at Carlisle on 4 October 1794, reviewed troops there and at Fort Cumberland, went as far as Bedford, then returned to Philadelphia and handed command to Henry Lee, governor of Virginia. Hamilton stayed with the army. Washington is the only sitting president to lead troops in the field.
- About 150 men were seized in the night arrests of mid-November 1794. About twenty were sent to Philadelphia, arriving around Christmas, and paraded through the city.
- David Bradford escaped to Spanish territory. John Adams pardoned him in 1799.
- Philip Wigle (Vigol) and John Mitchell were convicted of treason in 1795 and sentenced to hang. Mitchell's acts included robbing the mail at Bradford's urging; Wigle's were attacks on excise officers. Execution was respited, doctors found Wigle of unsound mind, and Washington pardoned both on 2 November 1795.

- A general amnesty proclamation of 10 July 1795 is sometimes confused with the individual pardons of 2 November 1795.
- The Fort Cumberland painting is attributed to Frederick Kemmelmeyer by the Metropolitan Museum; its date is uncertain.

## Module 6. Repeal and the Kentucky legend

**The question.** What happened to the tax, and where does the bourbon story come from?

### Nailed down

- A federal spirits tax returned briefly during the War of 1812 and again in 1862: the Revenue Act signed by Lincoln on 1 July 1862 taxed spirits at 20 cents a proof gallon, and the tax has continued since.
- Commercial distilling in Kentucky is dated to at least 1783 (Evan Williams, Louisville, by the state marker; the "first distiller" title is a later claim). Kentucky became a state in 1792. Kentucky distillers largely refused to pay the excise and the Treasury could not force collection; some were later prosecuted.
- Virginia created Bourbon County out of Fayette County by an act of 1785, effective 1786 (the Library of Virginia gives 1786 as the date of formation).
- The earliest known printed use of "bourbon whiskey" is a Stout and Adams advertisement in the _Western Citizen_ (Paris, Kentucky), June 1821.
- Elijah Craig was a Baptist preacher, jailed in Virginia for preaching without a licence, who distilled in Kentucky. Richard Collins's history of Kentucky (1874) credited him with the first bourbon in 1789; historians including Henry Crowgey and Charles Cowdery treat this as legend. Heaven Hill still credits him with first using charred barrels.
- Jefferson's Republicans had opposed the excise, and repeal was a leading recommendation of his First Annual Message. "An Act to repeal the Internal Taxes" was signed on 6 April 1802 (2 Stat. 148) and took effect on 30 June 1802. Its first section concerns the duties on stills and spirits.

## The four pours

- **Pour 1. Old Overholt Straight Rye.** A brand from Westmoreland County, one of the four counties of the rebellion
- **Pour 2. Pikesville Straight Rye.** Maryland rye; Maryland sent militia west in 1794; 110 proof
- **Pour 3. Michter's US*1 Straight Rye.** A name from an old eastern Pennsylvania distillery
- **Pour 4. Elijah Craig Small Batch.** A bourbon, named for the subject of another legend

Producers, proofs and availability change. Check each bottle's own label. Prices vary too much by region to quote.

### Nailed down

- Old Overholt Straight Rye: 86 proof, four years old (Beam's own page), made by James B. Beam Distilling Co. in Kentucky. Abraham Overholt, a Mennonite farmer, began distilling at West Overton, Westmoreland County, in 1810; the West Overton distillery never reopened after production moved away.
- Pikesville Straight Rye: 110 proof, at least six years old, made by Heaven Hill at its Bernheim distillery in Louisville. A Maryland brand from 1895 that Heaven Hill bought as Maryland rye distilling ended; it has been made in Kentucky since.
- Michter's US*1 Kentucky Straight Rye: single barrel, 84.8 proof. The Michter's distillery at Schaefferstown, Lebanon County (earlier Shenk's, then Bomberger's) closed in 1989 after bankruptcy (one source dates the final closing to February 1990). Chatham Imports bought the lapsed name in the 1990s and has distilled in Shively, Louisville, since about 2015, with a second distillery at Fort Nelson downtown.
- Elijah Craig Small Batch: Kentucky straight bourbon, 94 proof, made by Heaven Hill; no age statement since 2016.

## Module 7. The rye label

### Nailed down

- Rye whisky: at least 51% rye, distilled at 160 proof or less, stored in charred new oak. Straight: at least two years in those containers. Whisky under four years old must carry an age statement. Bottled in bond: one distiller, one distilling season, at least four years, 100 proof.

### Sources

- 27 CFR 5.143 (whisky standards of identity), 5.74 (age statements), 5.88 (bottled in bond): https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-27/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-5
