## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] The Whiskey Rebellion

Thanks for coming. Tonight is about a tax on whiskey. In 1791 the new federal government taxed whiskey made in America, and a lot of people in western Pennsylvania refused to pay it.

The story runs from 1791 to 1794. It ends with President Washington riding out with an army. No other sitting president has led troops in the field. After that we'll look at an old story that the rebels ran off to Kentucky and invented bourbon. It is probably not true.

## [SLIDE 2] In 1790 the federal government took on the war debts.

The Revolutionary War was won, but it had been paid for mostly with borrowed money. Congress owed a lot, and so did the states. Alexander Hamilton was the first Secretary of the Treasury. In 1790 he got Congress to take over the states' war debts as well as its own.

Hamilton wanted the country to pay its debts on time, so that it could borrow again when it needed to. That meant paying interest every year, and for that the government needed a steady income.

Most federal money came from taxes on imports. Hamilton thought those were about as high as they could go. So he proposed a tax on spirits made in the United States. Congress passed it, and it became law on the third of March 1791. It took effect that summer.

## [SLIDE 3] Eastern distillers and western farmers

The tax was between 9 and 25 cents a gallon, depending on how strong the spirit was. If your still was out in the country, you had another choice. You could pay 60 cents a year for each gallon your still held. That figure assumed a farm still ran about four months a year. In 1792 Congress lowered the rates.

A big distiller in the east ran his still most of the year and sold his whiskey for cash. A farmer in western Pennsylvania ran a small still for a few weeks after harvest, to use up his spare rye. Some historians say the small producers ended up paying more per gallon than the big ones.

Either way the tax had to be paid in coin, and there was very little coin west of the mountains.

## [SLIDE 4] West of the Alleghenies, whiskey was how grain got to market.

There were four counties in western Pennsylvania: Washington, Allegheny, Westmoreland and Fayette. The Allegheny mountains cut them off from the eastern cities, and the roads over the mountains were bad. The other way out was by river, down the Ohio and the Mississippi to New Orleans. Spain controlled the lower Mississippi and kept it closed to American trade until 1795.

So a farmer who grew rye could not easily sell it as rye. A packhorse could carry only a few bushels of grain, and grain spoiled on the way. The same horse could carry the whiskey made from many more bushels. People usually say four bushels as grain, or twenty-four as whiskey.

Whiskey also kept, and people used it as money. Farmers paid debts, store bills and wages with it.

## [SLIDE 5] Pour 1 of 4: Old Overholt Straight Rye

First pour. This is Old Overholt, a straight rye.

The Overholts were Mennonite farmers in Westmoreland County, one of the four counties in tonight's story. Abraham Overholt started distilling there, at West Overton, in 1810. Old Overholt became one of the best-known Pennsylvania ryes. Today it is made in Kentucky by Jim Beam. It is four years old and 86 proof.

The farmers in tonight's story were making rye whiskey too. Theirs spent little or no time in a barrel, and it would have been much rougher than this. If you mostly drink bourbon, notice that rye is drier. It tastes more of bread and pepper.

## [SLIDE 6] Collectors were attacked from 1791

People fought the tax almost from the start.

In September 1791 a tax collector called Robert Johnson was caught at Pigeon Creek by a group of men in disguise. Johnson collected for Washington and Allegheny counties. The men tarred and feathered him. Other collectors were attacked over the next three years.

In August 1792 a meeting in Pittsburgh called the tax unjust. It told people to refuse collectors any help and to treat them with contempt. The clerk of that meeting was Albert Gallatin, who later became Jefferson's Secretary of the Treasury. In September Washington issued a proclamation telling people to stop getting in the way of the law. Not much changed.

In 1794 notices started to appear signed "Tom the Tinker". They threatened farmers who had registered their stills, and some of those farmers found holes shot in them. The joke was that Tom had come to mend the still.

The drawing at the bottom of the slide is called "An Exciseman". An exciseman is a tax collector. He is carrying off two kegs of whiskey, and two farmers are chasing him with tar and feathers. The verse on it is dated the 13th of August 1792.

## [SLIDE 7] July 1794: summonses to a court in Philadelphia

For three years almost nobody in the west paid the tax. In the summer of 1794 the government tried to make them.

The man in charge of the tax in the region was John Neville. He had been an officer in the Revolutionary War and he was one of the richest men in the area. He lived on a big estate south of Pittsburgh called Bower Hill.

The government had court orders against more than sixty distillers who had not registered their stills. In July 1794 the federal marshal, David Lenox, rode out with Neville to deliver them. The orders told each distiller to appear in federal court in Philadelphia, about three hundred miles away. For a farmer that meant weeks away from home at harvest time.

Congress had changed the law in June so that cases like these could be heard in local courts. These orders had been written a few days before the change, so they still said Philadelphia.

On the fifteenth of July, Lenox and Neville delivered an order at a farm, and someone shot at them.

## [SLIDE 8] 17 July 1794: Bower Hill is burned

The next morning, the sixteenth, about fifty armed men came to Neville's house at Bower Hill. Shots were fired from the house, and a farmer called Oliver Miller was hit. He died of the wound.

On the seventeenth they came back, and this time there were about five hundred. A few soldiers from the fort at Pittsburgh were defending the house.

The rebels were led by a militia officer, James McFarlane. During a pause in the shooting he stepped out into the open and was shot dead. People never agreed on who fired, or on whether a truce had been called. After that the rebels set fire to the house and the outbuildings, and the soldiers gave up.

## [SLIDE 9] Braddock's Field, 1 August 1794

After Bower Hill the most extreme rebels ran the region for a few weeks. The best known was David Bradford, a lawyer from the town of Washington. His men robbed the mail to find out who in Pittsburgh was writing letters against them. Then they called the armed men of the region to meet on the first of August at Braddock's Field, about eight miles from Pittsburgh.

About seven thousand came. Some accounts say five or six thousand. Some of the men talked about attacking the fort and burning parts of Pittsburgh. Some talked about the western counties leaving the United States, and even joining Spain or Britain.

The leading men of Pittsburgh went out to meet them. They agreed to throw out the people the rebels were angry at, and they handed out food and whiskey. The crowd marched through the town and went home. Little damage was done.

## [SLIDE 10] Pour 2 of 4: Pikesville Straight Rye

Second pour. This is Pikesville, a straight rye. It was a Maryland brand, started in 1895 and named after a town outside Baltimore. Maryland and Pennsylvania were the two old rye states. Maryland also sent militia to the army you're about to hear about.

Today Pikesville is made in Kentucky by Heaven Hill. It is 110 proof, which is strong. Take a small sip neat, then add water and try it again.

People used to say Maryland rye was a little softer and sweeter than Pennsylvania rye. Taste this next to the Old Overholt and see if you agree.

## [SLIDE 11] Washington sends the militia west in autumn 1794

In Philadelphia, Washington and Hamilton decided they had to act. Under a law from 1792 the president could call out the militia if a federal judge said the courts could not enforce the law. A Supreme Court justice, James Wilson, said so on the fourth of August. Washington then called for militia from New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Maryland and Virginia.

On the seventh of August he ordered the rebels to go home. He also sent three men west with an offer. Anyone who signed a promise to obey the law would not be punished. Many signed, but not enough to satisfy Washington, and in the autumn the army set off.

It was about thirteen thousand men. Washington joined it at Carlisle on the fourth of October. He inspected the troops there and at Fort Cumberland, and he rode with them as far as Bedford. The painting on the first slide shows him at Fort Cumberland. Then he went back to Philadelphia for the opening of Congress and handed command to Henry Lee, the governor of Virginia. Hamilton stayed with the army.

## [SLIDE 12] The rebellion ended without a battle

The army crossed the mountains in November and found nobody to fight. The rebels had gone home.

One night in the middle of November, soldiers went round the western counties and pulled men out of their beds. About a hundred and fifty were taken and held for questioning, in the cold. Most were let go.

David Bradford escaped down the Ohio into Spanish territory. He was never tried, and President John Adams pardoned him in 1799.

About twenty prisoners were marched to Philadelphia. They got there around Christmas and were paraded through the streets. In 1795 two of them were convicted of treason and sentenced to hang. One was Philip Wigle, who had attacked tax officers. The other was John Mitchell, who had robbed the mail for Bradford. Washington delayed the hangings. Doctors found that Wigle was not of sound mind. On the second of November 1795 Washington pardoned both men.

## [SLIDE 13] 1802: Jefferson's Congress repeals the excise

The government had shown it could enforce a federal law in a place where people did not want it. That mattered a lot to Hamilton.

The rebellion also became a fight between the parties. Washington blamed the political clubs that supported Jefferson for stirring up the trouble. Jefferson's party had been against the whiskey tax from the beginning.

Jefferson became president in 1801. In April 1802 he signed a law that ended the federal taxes on goods made inside the country, and the whiskey tax went with them. Whiskey was taxed again for a short time during the War of 1812. In 1862 Congress taxed it to pay for the Civil War, and that tax has never been lifted.

## [SLIDE 14] Pour 3 of 4: Michter's US*1 Straight Rye

Third pour. This is Michter's straight rye. The name comes from an old distillery in Schaefferstown, in Lebanon County in eastern Pennsylvania. It was one of the last Pennsylvania rye distilleries, and it closed in 1989. In the 1990s a new company bought the name and started again in Kentucky. It distills in Louisville now.

So all three of tonight's ryes have Pennsylvania or Maryland names, and all three are made in Kentucky. Rye was made in Pennsylvania and Maryland for more than a century after the rebellion. Prohibition, and the drop in rye drinking after it, ended that.

This is a single barrel rye at 84.8 proof. Taste it next to the other two. All three are straight ryes. See how different they are.

## [SLIDE 15] The rebels and bourbon: a legend

You will read in bourbon books and on some bottles that the whiskey rebels ran away to Kentucky to escape the tax, and invented bourbon there.

It probably did not happen that way. Kentucky had distillers in the 1780s, before there was a tax. Kentucky became a state in 1792, so the tax applied there as well. The distillers in Kentucky mostly refused to pay it too. Moving there did not get anyone out of the tax.

Bourbon County was created in 1785, when Kentucky was still part of Virginia. The first time we know of that "bourbon whiskey" appeared in print was in 1821, in a newspaper advertisement in Paris, Kentucky. That is nearly thirty years after the rebellion.

## [SLIDE 16] Pour 4 of 4: Elijah Craig Small Batch

The last pour is a bourbon. It is named after a man with a legend of his own.

Elijah Craig was a Baptist preacher. He was jailed in Virginia for preaching without a license, and later he moved to Kentucky. He ran several businesses there, and one of them was a distillery. In 1874, long after he died, a history of Kentucky said he made the first bourbon in 1789. Later people added that he was the first to char his barrels. There is no good evidence for either claim.

This is Elijah Craig Small Batch, made by Heaven Hill, at 94 proof. Taste it after the ryes. It is sweeter, and that comes from the corn. Corn is the main difference between bourbon and the rye whiskey those Pennsylvania farmers made.

## [SLIDE 17] Reading a rye whiskey label

One more slide before the toast. This is what the words on a rye label mean.

"Rye whiskey" is defined by law in the United States. The grain has to be at least 51 percent rye, and the whiskey has to be aged in new charred oak. "Straight" means it is at least two years old and has nothing added. If it is less than four years old, the label has to give the age. "Bottled in bond" means it was made by one distillery in one season, aged at least four years and bottled at exactly 100 proof.

"Pennsylvania style" and "Maryland style" have no legal meaning. Neither does "Monongahela", which was the old name for western Pennsylvania rye. It comes from the river. A bottle with one of those names on it can be made anywhere.
