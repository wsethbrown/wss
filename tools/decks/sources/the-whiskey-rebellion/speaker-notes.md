## Part Two: the script

## [SLIDE 1] The Whiskey Rebellion

Thanks for coming. Tonight is about the first tax the new federal government put on anything made in America, which was a tax on whiskey, and what happened in western Pennsylvania when people refused to pay it.

It runs from 1791 to 1794, with a postscript in 1802, and it includes the only time a sitting president has ridden out with an army. At the end there's a well-known piece of whiskey folklore about the rebels and Kentucky, which I'll treat as the legend it is.

## [SLIDE 2] In 1790 the federal government took on the war debts.

The Revolutionary War had been won, but it had been paid for largely on credit, and both Congress and the individual states owed a great deal of money. Alexander Hamilton, the first Secretary of the Treasury, persuaded Congress to have the federal government take over the states' debts as well as its own.

His argument was that if the country paid its debts reliably it could borrow again when it needed to. But the interest had to be paid every year, and that needed a steady income.

Most federal money came from duties on imports, which Hamilton thought were about as high as they could go. So he proposed a tax on spirits distilled in the United States. Congress passed it, and it was signed on the third of March 1791. It took effect that summer.

## [SLIDE 3] Eastern distillers and western farmers

The Act charged between 9 and 25 cents on each gallon, depending on the strength of the spirit. The owner of a country still, outside a town, could choose instead to pay 60 cents a year for each gallon the still held, a figure worked out on the assumption that a farm still ran about four months a year. In 1792 Congress lowered the rates and changed the options.

Historians such as William Hogeland argue that the arrangement suited large commercial distillers in the east, who ran their stills most of the year and sold for cash, and that small producers ended up paying more per gallon. I couldn't confirm the figures often quoted for this, six cents a gallon against nine.

A farmer in western Pennsylvania ran a small still for a few weeks after harvest to turn his surplus rye into whiskey. Either way, the tax was due in money, and there was very little coin in circulation west of the mountains.

## [SLIDE 4] West of the Alleghenies, whiskey was how grain got to market.

The four western counties of Pennsylvania, Washington, Allegheny, Westmoreland and Fayette, were cut off from the eastern cities by the Allegheny mountains, and the roads over them were poor. The other way out was down the Ohio and the Mississippi to New Orleans, and Spain controlled the lower Mississippi and kept it closed to American trade until a treaty was signed in 1795.

So if you grew rye, you couldn't easily sell it as rye. A packhorse could carry only a few bushels of grain, and it would spoil on the way. The same horse could carry the whiskey made from many times that much grain. The figures often quoted are four bushels as grain against twenty-four as whiskey; I haven't been able to trace where they come from, so take them as an illustration.

Whiskey also kept, and people used it as money. Farmers paid debts, store bills and wages in it. A tax on whiskey, payable in coin, was a tax on the one thing in the west that worked like cash.

## [SLIDE 5] Pour 1 of 4: Old Overholt Straight Rye

First pour: Old Overholt, a straight rye.

The Overholt family were Mennonite farmers in Westmoreland County, one of the four counties in tonight's story, and Abraham Overholt started distilling at West Overton in 1810, and the brand still uses that date. It became one of the best-known Pennsylvania ryes. It's made in Kentucky by Jim Beam now, four years old and 86 proof, the gentlest pour of the night.

This is the closest we can get to the sort of thing those farmers were making, though their whiskey would have been unaged or nearly so, and much rougher. If you mostly drink bourbon, notice how much drier rye is, with more of a bread and pepper taste.

## [SLIDE 6] Collectors were attacked from 1791

Resistance started almost as soon as the tax did.

In September 1791 a collector named Robert Johnson, responsible for Washington and Allegheny counties, was caught on Pigeon Creek by a group of men in disguise, who tarred and feathered him. Other collectors and their helpers were attacked over the next three years.

On the 21st and 22nd of August 1792 a meeting in Pittsburgh passed resolutions calling the tax unjust and oppressive, and telling people to treat collectors with contempt and refuse them any help. The meeting's clerk was Albert Gallatin, who later became Jefferson's Secretary of the Treasury. Washington answered with a proclamation on the 15th of September 1792 telling people to stop obstructing the law. Not much changed.

In 1794 notices signed "Tom the Tinker" began to appear, and farmers who had registered their stills found holes shot in them. The joke was that Tom had come to "mend" the still.

The drawing at the bottom is called "An Exciseman". It shows one carrying off two kegs of whiskey, chased by farmers with tar and feathers, and the verse on it says it was written on the 13th of August 1792. Where it was drawn isn't known; the copy we have comes from a Philadelphia museum.

## [SLIDE 7] July 1794: summonses to a court in Philadelphia

For three years the tax was barely collected in the west. Then in the summer of 1794 the government tried to enforce it.

The man in charge locally was John Neville, the inspector of the excise for the region. He was a Revolutionary War officer, one of the richest men in the area, and he lived on a large estate south of Pittsburgh called Bower Hill.

The federal district attorney, William Rawle, had obtained writs against more than sixty distillers who hadn't registered, and in July 1794 the federal marshal, David Lenox, rode out with Neville to serve them. The writs ordered them to appear in the federal court in Philadelphia, about three hundred miles away. For a farmer that meant weeks away at harvest time.

Congress had actually changed the law that June so that cases like these could be heard in local state courts, but these writs had been entered at the end of May, a few days before the change, and still named Philadelphia.

When Lenox and Neville served a writ at a farm on the fifteenth of July, shots were fired at them.

## [SLIDE 8] 17 July 1794: Bower Hill is burned

The next morning, the sixteenth, a group of about fifty armed men came to Bower Hill. Shots were fired from the house, and a farmer named Oliver Miller was mortally wounded.

On the seventeenth they came back, about five hundred of them. The house was held by a handful of soldiers from Fort Fayette, the army post at Pittsburgh, under Major Abraham Kirkpatrick.

The rebels' leader was a militia officer, Major James McFarlane. During a pause in the shooting he stepped out, and he was shot and killed. Who fired, and whether the pause was a real truce, was argued about at the time and still is. After that the attackers set fire to the house and outbuildings, and the soldiers surrendered.

## [SLIDE 9] Braddock's Field, 1 August 1794

After Bower Hill the radicals were in charge of the region for a few weeks. The best-known of them was David Bradford, a lawyer from the town of Washington. His group robbed the mail to find out who in Pittsburgh was writing against them, and then called a muster at Braddock's Field, about eight miles from the town, on the first of August.

Around seven thousand armed men turned up. That's the usual estimate; some accounts say five or six thousand. Some of them talked about attacking Fort Fayette, about burning parts of Pittsburgh, and about the western counties breaking away altogether, even joining Spain or Britain.

Pittsburgh's leading citizens went out to meet the crowd, agreed to expel the people the rebels objected to, and supplied food and whiskey. The crowd marched through the town and dispersed without much damage.

## [SLIDE 10] Pour 2 of 4: Pikesville Straight Rye

Second pour. Pikesville is a Maryland brand going back to 1895, named after a town outside Baltimore, and Maryland was the other old American rye state, alongside Pennsylvania. Maryland's militia was part of the army we'll come to next.

It's made in Kentucky by Heaven Hill these days. It's 110 proof, which is strong, so take a small sip neat, then add water and try again.

Maryland rye was traditionally described as a little softer and sweeter than Pennsylvania rye. That's a generalisation from the past, so treat it as something to look for next to the Overholt.

## [SLIDE 11] Washington sends the militia west in autumn 1794

In Philadelphia, Washington and Hamilton decided this couldn't be left alone. Under a 1792 law, the president could call out the militia if a federal judge certified that the courts couldn't enforce the law. Justice James Wilson of the Supreme Court gave that certificate on the fourth of August, and Washington called for militia from New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Maryland and Virginia.

On the seventh he issued a proclamation ordering the insurgents to go home, and he sent three commissioners west, who offered an amnesty to anyone who signed a pledge to obey the law. Many did, but not enough for Washington, and the army set off in the autumn.

It came to about thirteen thousand men; Mount Vernon's figure is 12,950. Washington joined it at Carlisle on the fourth of October, reviewed the troops there and at Fort Cumberland, and got as far as Bedford. The painting on our first slide shows the Fort Cumberland review, and it's attributed to a Baltimore artist called Frederick Kemmelmeyer. Then he went back to Philadelphia for the opening of Congress, and handed command to Henry Lee, the governor of Virginia. Hamilton stayed with the army.

## [SLIDE 12] The rebellion ended without a battle

When the army crossed the mountains in November there was nobody to fight. The rebels had gone home.

On a night in mid-November soldiers went round the western counties pulling suspects out of their beds and holding them, in cold weather and rough conditions, for questioning. About a hundred and fifty men were taken. Most were let go.

David Bradford got away down the Ohio to Spanish territory and never stood trial. John Adams pardoned him in 1799.

About twenty prisoners were marched to Philadelphia, arriving around Christmas, and paraded through the streets. Accounts of how many were then tried differ, from ten to two dozen. In 1795 two were convicted of treason and sentenced to hang: Philip Wigle, for a series of attacks on excise officers, and John Mitchell, whose acts included robbing the mail at Bradford's urging. Washington put off the executions, and on the second of November 1795 he pardoned both. Doctors had found Wigle of unsound mind.

## [SLIDE 13] 1802: Jefferson's Congress repeals the excise

The government had shown it could enforce a federal law where people didn't want it, which mattered a great deal to Hamilton.

It also became party politics. Washington blamed the political clubs that supported Jefferson's side, the Democratic-Republican societies, for stirring the trouble up. Jefferson's party had opposed the excise from the start.

After Jefferson became president in 1801, Congress repealed the internal taxes, the whiskey tax among them, by an act he signed on the sixth of April 1802. There was a short-lived return during the War of 1812, and then nothing until 1862, when Congress taxed whiskey to pay for the Civil War. That tax has never gone away.

## [SLIDE 14] Pour 3 of 4: Michter's US*1 Straight Rye

Third pour. Michter's is a name from eastern Pennsylvania, from an old distillery in Schaefferstown, Lebanon County, which was one of the last of the Pennsylvania rye distilleries when it closed in 1989. The present company bought the lapsed name in the 1990s and started again in Kentucky, and it distills in Louisville now.

So none of tonight's three ryes is made in Pennsylvania or Maryland any more. The rye tradition there outlived the rebellion by more than a century; Prohibition and the slump in rye drinking afterwards ended it.

This is a single barrel rye at 84.8 proof. Put it next to the other two and notice how much difference the barrel and the proof make within the same style.

## [SLIDE 15] The rebels and bourbon: a legend

Now the folklore. You'll read on bottles and in bourbon books that after the rebellion the whiskey rebels fled to Kentucky, out of the government's reach, and invented bourbon there.

This story is told a lot and the evidence for it is weak. Kentucky already had distillers in the 1780s, before the tax existed. Kentucky was a state from 1792 and the tax applied there too; the distillers there refused to pay it and it was hardly collected, so moving to Kentucky didn't get you out of anything.

Virginia created Bourbon County by an act of 1785, which took effect in 1786, while Kentucky was still part of Virginia. The earliest known printed use of "bourbon whiskey" is an advertisement in a Paris, Kentucky newspaper in 1821, nearly thirty years after the rebellion.

## [SLIDE 16] Pour 4 of 4: Elijah Craig Small Batch

So the last pour is a bourbon, and it's named after the subject of a different legend.

Elijah Craig was a Baptist preacher, jailed in Virginia for preaching without a licence, who moved to Kentucky and did run a distillery among his other businesses. In 1874 a Kentucky historian, Richard Collins, credited him with the first bourbon, in 1789, and the story was later extended to say he was the first to char his barrels. Historians find no good evidence for either.

It's made by Heaven Hill, and it's 94 proof. Taste it after the ryes and notice the sweetness that the corn brings, which is the main thing that separates bourbon from what the western Pennsylvania farmers were making.

## [SLIDE 17] Reading a rye whiskey label

To finish, what the words on a rye label mean.

"Rye whiskey" has a legal meaning in the United States: at least 51 percent rye in the mash, and aged in new charred oak containers. "Straight" means at least two years old with nothing added, and if it's under four years the label has to give the age. "Bottled in bond" means one distillery, one season, at least four years, and exactly 100 proof.

"Pennsylvania style" and "Maryland style" have no legal definition, and neither does "Monongahela", which was the old name for western Pennsylvania rye, after the river. They describe a tradition, and the bottle can be made anywhere.
