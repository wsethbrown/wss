# Bottled in Bond

Research outline: 1897

1897. A guide for anyone who wants to research the subject and build a talk of their own.

---

**How this was checked.** The facts in the deck, the script and this outline were checked in November 2026 against the sources listed under each module: the statute and federal regulations, court records, the American Presidency Project, FDA and TTB history pages, the producers' own pages, and established whiskey writers. Where a source is only the company's own account, the script says so. What could not be confirmed is listed under "Not confirmed" near the end, with what would settle it.

## Module 1. The trust problem before 1897

**The question.** How did Americans buy whiskey in the 1870s and 1880s, and what could a buyer actually know about it?

### Nailed down

- In 1870 selling whiskey from the barrel, not in sealed bottles, was the norm; that is the point of the Old Forester story.
- Rectifiers made "whiskey" from neutral spirit, coloured and flavoured; straight-whiskey makers and rectifiers were opposing interests in the fights of 1897 and 1906–1909.
- Historians disagree about motive: some read the pure-food fight over whiskey as straight distillers using the law against rectifiers as much as consumer protection.

### Primary documents

- Annual Reports of the Commissioner of Internal Revenue (free, HathiTrust). Production, rectifier licences, warehouse stocks by year.
- Period trade papers, especially _Bonfort's Wine and Spirit Circular_.
- Rectifiers' recipe manuals of the period.

### Sources

- Old Forester, "Our legacy": https://www.oldforester.com/our-legacy
- High and Coppin, "Wiley and the Whiskey Industry: Strategic Behavior in the Passage of the Pure Food Act", _Business History Review_ (Cambridge): https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-history-review/article/wiley-and-the-whiskey-industry-strategic-behavior-in-the-passage-of-the-pure-food-act/FE8D17CE997C0D3AE353AAA31F1F7C18
- Cato Institute, _Regulation_, "Straight whiskey, bad regulation" (Fall 2023): https://www.cato.org/regulation/fall-2023/straight-whiskey-bad-regulation

### Traps

- The lurid ingredient lists (tobacco, creosote, acid) are mostly sourced to the rectifiers' opponents. The manuals are the better evidence, and no specific additive is named in the deck.
- "Rectified" did not always mean bad or dangerous. Many blends were consistent and popular.

## Module 2. The sealed bottle, 1870

**The question.** Who first tried to solve the trust problem with packaging, and why?

### Nailed down

- George Garvin Brown, a Louisville pharmaceutical salesman, launched Old Forester in 1870 in sealed glass bottles.
- Each bottle carried Brown's signature as a guarantee; current labels still quote "There is nothing better in the market."
- Brown bought whiskey from other distilleries and blended it (company account and secondary sources).
- "America's First Bottled Bourbon" is a Brown-Forman registered trademark; no independent confirmation was found.

### Primary documents

- Brown-Forman's corporate history and archive.
- Louisville city directories and newspaper advertising from the 1870s (Chronicling America, free).

### Sources

- Old Forester, "Our legacy": https://www.oldforester.com/our-legacy
- Brown-Forman, "Discover our roots": https://www.brown-forman.com/about/history
- Kentucky Historical Society, ExploreKYHistory, "Bottled Bourbon": https://explorekyhistory.ky.gov/items/show/416
- Wikipedia, "Old Forester" (for the blending and pharmacy-sale summary, with its citations): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Forester

### Traps

- The doctors' complaint is the company's origin story. Treat it as such.
- The spelling of the brand name changed over time, and the usual story about the doctor it was named for is company tradition.

## Module 3. The bonded warehouse

**The question.** Why was a federal officer already standing in the warehouse?

### Nailed down

- The internal revenue act of 20 July 1868 (15 Stat. 125) required each distiller to provide a warehouse which, once approved, became a bonded warehouse under the collector's control with an internal revenue storekeeper assigned to it. Tax was due on withdrawal.
- Storekeepers held custody of the warehouse; the Supreme Court treated their duties as continuous (_United States v. Powell_, 1871).
- The bonding period was about a year in 1868 and eight years by the 1890s; the eight-year limit was still in force in 1954.

### Primary documents

- U.S. Statutes at Large for the internal revenue acts of 1868, 1879 and 1894 (free, govinfo.gov).
- Internal Revenue regulations for distillers of the period.

### Sources

- Act of 20 July 1868, 15 Stat. 125 (govinfo): https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-15/pdf/STATUTE-15-Pg125-2.pdf
- _United States v. Powell_, 81 U.S. 493 (1871), Cornell LII: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/81/493
- _United States v. Singer_, 82 U.S. 111 (1872), Justia: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/82/111
- Distilled Spirits Council, American Whiskey Trail, "American Whiskey History": https://americanwhiskeytrail.distilledspirits.org/american-whiskey-history
- Senate Finance Committee, report on extension of the bonding period (1954): https://www.finance.senate.gov/download/1954/07/28/extension-of-bonding-period-for-certain-distilled-spirits-report-83-1990&download=1

### Traps

- The system existed to protect tax revenue. Consumer protection was a later use of the same machinery.

## Module 4. The Act of 3 March 1897

**The question.** What exactly did the law require, and who wanted it?

### Nailed down

- "An Act to allow the bottling of distilled spirits in bond", chapter 379, 29 Stat. 626, approved 3 March 1897 and signed by Grover Cleveland, the day before William McKinley's inauguration.
- Voluntary: it allowed bottling under government supervision for distillers who met the conditions.
- Current conditions (27 CFR 5.88): one distilling season, one distiller, one distillery; at least four years in wooden containers in a federally bonded warehouse; unaltered except by filtration, chill-proofing or similar physical treatment, with proof reduced only by pure water to exactly 100 proof; the label names the distillery (real or trade name) and the plant number.
- A distilling season (27 CFR 5.1) is January 1 to June 30 (spring) or July 1 to December 31 (fall).
- Label terms used on slide 16: "straight" bourbon or rye is stored at least two years in charred new oak (27 CFR 5.143); an age statement gives the age of the youngest whisky and is required on whisky aged less than four years (27 CFR 5.74).
- Advocates: Col. Edmund Haynes Taylor Jr., Frankfort distiller, and John G. Carlisle, Secretary of the Treasury under Cleveland. Sources differ on which did more.
- Opponents: rectifiers, including Isaac W. Bernheim and George Garvin Brown, argued that the law gave straight distillers an unfair advantage.
- Earlier federal consumer laws exist (Drug Importation Act 1848; oleomargarine 1886), so "first consumer protection law" is wrong.

### Primary documents

- The Act itself: 29 Stat. 626 (free, govinfo.gov).
- Current rule: Title 27, Code of Federal Regulations, Part 5 (free, ecfr.gov).
- Congressional Record for the debates, including the rectifiers' objections.

## Module 5. "What is whisky?", 1906–1909

**The question.** How did the argument continue after 1897, and how did it end?

### Nailed down

- Pure Food and Drug Act, passed and signed 30 June 1906; enforced at first by the Bureau of Chemistry under Harvey W. Wiley.
- Wiley held that whisky made from neutral spirit should not pass as whisky without qualification.
- Taft had Solicitor General Lloyd Bowers rehear the matter and issued his decision on 27 December 1909: neutral-spirit whisky is whisky, but "straight whisky" is so labelled, a mixture of straight and neutral-spirit whisky is a blend, and whisky from neutral spirit names its principal ingredient.

### Primary documents

- The Taft Decision (American Presidency Project).
- Wiley's autobiography (1930), read as an advocate's account.
- The record of the hearings before Solicitor General Lloyd Bowers.

### Sources

- American Presidency Project, "Decision on the Meaning of the Term 'Whisky' Under the Pure Food Act…": https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/decision-the-meaning-the-term-whisky-under-the-pure-food-act-and-the-proper-regulations
- Britannica, "Pure Food and Drug Act": https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pure-Food-and-Drug-Act
- Library of Congress, "Harvey Wiley and the pure food movement": https://blogs.loc.gov/inside_adams/2024/12/harvey-wiley-and-the-pure-food-movement/
- Distillery Trail, "What is Whisky? The Taft Decision of 1909": https://www.distillerytrail.com/blog/what-is-whisky-the-taft-decision-of-1909/
- Wiley, "The History of a Crime Against the Food Law" (reprint): http://www.whale.to/a/wiley_b.html

### Traps

- Wiley is usually cast as the hero. On whiskey, Taft overruled him.
- One newspaper-derived source dates the decision 26 December; the Presidency Project gives the 27th.
- The modern definitions of straight and blended whiskey come from later regulation, after Repeal. Taft set the vocabulary, not today's exact standards.

## Module 6. Decline and return

**The question.** Why did bonded whiskey go from the top shelf to the bottom, and why did it come back?

### Nailed down

- During national Prohibition (January 1920 to December 1933) six companies were licensed to sell medicinal whiskey, dispensed on prescription through pharmacies as bonded, 100-proof pints. Sources note it did not always meet every condition of the 1897 Act.
- From the 1960s bonded whiskey lost share to vodka; 1970s inflation pushed distillers to lower proofs. Whiskey's share of the US liquor market fell from 74% to 54% between 1960 and 1975.
- The federal strip-stamp requirement for spirits was repealed effective 1 July 1985 (TTB Industry Circular 84-06).
- A revival in the 2010s, driven by craft distillers with four-year-old stock and by bartenders' interest in higher-proof whiskey.

### Primary documents

- Surviving prescription forms and medicinal pint labels in museum collections.
- Distilled Spirits Council statistics for volume by category.
- Federal Register notices and TTB circulars for changes to stamp and labelling rules.

### Sources

- Ohio State University, Temperance and Prohibition, "Medicinal alcohol": https://prohibition.osu.edu/american-prohibition-1920/medicinal-alcohol
- Chuck Cowdery, "Who had medicinal whiskey licenses": https://chuckcowdery.blogspot.com/2014/03/who-had-medicinal-whiskey-licenses_7.html
- American Institute of the History of Pharmacy, "Prohibition-era medicinal liquors": https://aihp.org/remembering-pharmacys-past-prohibition-era-medicinal-liquors/
- TTB Industry Circular 84-06, repeal of stamp requirement (archived): https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20120928001540/http:/www.ttb.gov/industry_circulars/archives/1984/84-06.html

### Traps

- Exact dates for "decline" and "revival" are a matter of interpretation. Sales figures by year are the evidence.
- Not every 100-proof whiskey is bonded, and not every old bottle marked "bonded" met today's wording.
- The green bottled-in-bond strip was dropped earlier than the general stamp; collectors give December 1982 (see Not confirmed).

## The four pours

- **Pour 1. Evan Williams Bottled-in-Bond (white label).** Maker: Heaven Hill, Bernheim distillery, Louisville. Label: Kentucky straight bourbon whiskey, 100 proof. Why it is in the flight: the baseline, and usually the least expensive.
- **Pour 2. Old Grand-Dad Bonded.** Maker: Jim Beam (Beam Suntory), Kentucky. Label: Kentucky straight bourbon, bottled in bond, 100 proof; published mash bill about 63% corn, 27% rye, 10% malted barley. Why it is in the flight: a higher-rye bourbon for contrast; a label to read.
- **Pour 3. Rittenhouse Rye Bottled-in-Bond.** Maker: Heaven Hill. Label: Straight rye whisky, 100 proof. Why it is in the flight: shows the law is not limited to bourbon.
- **Pour 4. Old Forester 1897 Bottled in Bond.** Maker: Brown-Forman. Label: Kentucky straight bourbon whisky, 100 proof; Whiskey Row Series no. 2. Why it is in the flight: Brown's brand, named for the year of the Act.

Producers, mash bills and availability change. Check each bottle's own label for the distillery (DSP) number. Prices vary too much by region to quote.

## Not confirmed

- **Specific rectifiers' additives (prune juice, burnt sugar, glycerin).** Removed from the slide and script. Settle with a dated rectifiers' manual of the 1880s.
- **Rectifiers' share of the market.** "Most of the whiskey" was softened to "a large share". Settle with Commissioner of Internal Revenue reports.
- **Bonding periods of 1879 and 1894 by statute.** Secondary sources conflict on 1868 (one year or three) and 1879. The script says only "short at first … eight years by the 1890s". Settle with the Statutes at Large.
- **Carlisle as Speaker of the House.** Not searched; removed from the script. Settle with the House history office.
- **The bonded strip stamps carrying Carlisle's portrait.** Not used. Settle with a dated stamp example.
- **Green bottled-in-bond strip discontinued 1 December 1982.** Collector sources only; the timeline uses the TTB-confirmed 1985 date and the script says "early eighties". Settle with the ATF notice of 1982.
- **Which distillery the slide 7 engraving shows.** The Commons title says only "the distillery of E. H. Taylor (Jr.) & Company", Frankfort, 1891, and the caption repeats that. The sunken garden court with a fountain suggests Old Taylor on Glenn's Creek (built 1887) rather than the O.F.C. and Carlisle plant on the Kentucky River, but no source names it. Settle with the book the engraving comes from or the Kentucky Historical Society collection. Sources consulted: Pre-Pro distillery records (https://www.pre-pro.com/midacore/view_distillery.php?did=DST438), Garden & Gun on the Old Taylor gardens (https://gardenandgun.com/first-look-reviving-a-lost-bourbon-landmark).
- **The "nothing added" rule for straight whiskey.** Removed from slide 16 and the script; the search returned the ageing rule in 27 CFR 5.143 but not the additive wording. Settle with the full text of 5.143 and 5.23 on ecfr.gov.
- **Rittenhouse's Pennsylvania origin.** Removed. Settle with Heaven Hill's brand history or 1930s trade records.
- **Bonded apple brandy (Laird's).** Removed. Settle with Laird's current product page.
